Summary

This review covers work that explains or mechanistically interprets robot foundation models: vision-language-action models (VLAs) such as OpenVLA, pi0, pi0.5 and GR00T, large diffusion and flow policies, and world-action models. It includes 151 works (85 core, 66 adjacent), found on 2026-10-19 through the arXiv API, a web sweep of workshop and venue pages, and the reference lists of 83 downloaded papers. The core papers were read in full.

What to take away:

  • The field is about a year old. Every core paper dates from 2025 or 2026, and 63 of the 73 with an arXiv id from 2026. pi0.5 and OpenVLA are the default subjects, and LIBERO in simulation is the default testbed.
  • Readable is not used. Linear probes recover almost anything from VLA internals: object positions, progress, success, the instruction. But the policy often does not act on what is decodable, and generic vision encoders decode much of the same. Controlled counterfactuals show that high probe accuracy on natural trajectories can come from correlated cues such as arm pose Kim et al. (2026), and policies go to the object the scene implies rather than the one the instruction names Jiang et al. (2026).
  • Language is shallow in benchmark fine-tunes. On LIBERO-style tasks, the instruction mostly selects a memorized trajectory family Chen et al. (2026), and most of the language model can be removed Sun et al. (2026).
  • Interventions work, labels do not. Sparse-autoencoder features, steering directions and task vectors change closed-loop behavior, including on real robots Swann et al. (2026); Miao et al. (2026). But edits are not local Wang et al. (2026), semantically named directions often do something else Babu et al. (2026), and linear steering fails inside flow-matching action experts Khayatan et al. (2026).
  • The backbone hands over to the action expert through a narrow channel of a few mid-to-late layers Swann et al. (2026).
  • Failure prediction works, but may not need internals. Hidden-state monitors flag failures well, yet action statistics and generic image features often match them Yang et al. (2026); Huang et al. (2026).
  • Reasoning text steers actions only partly. A correct reasoning chain recovers about half of the success lost to a bad instruction Trinh et al. (2026), and executors can ignore explicit plans Xie et al. (2026).
  • Evidence standards are still low. Few papers report statistics, most tune interventions on the tasks they evaluate, and no paper has yet traced a component-level circuit in a VLA.

The strongest single papers to start with: Swann et al. (2026) (narrow interface, causal), Kim et al. (2026) (why probe accuracy misleads), Chen et al. (2026) (instructions retrieve trajectories), Wang et al. (2026) (edits are not local), Trinh et al. (2026) (reasoning faithfulness, pre-registered) and Jacobs et al. (2026) (geometry, belief versus action edits).

Why robot foundation models need their own interpretability

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) take a camera image, a language instruction and often the robot’s own state, and output motor commands. Most are built by taking a pretrained vision-language model (VLM) and fine-tuning it on robot demonstrations, so they inherit a large model whose internals nobody designed. When such a policy drops a cup, ignores the instruction, or works in the lab and fails in a new kitchen, the people deploying it need to know why, and ideally need to know before it happens.

Language-model interpretability already has a toolbox for this: linear probes, sparse autoencoders, activation steering, activation patching, attention analysis. The question this review asks is what happens when that toolbox is pointed at robot policies. Five things make the robot setting different, and they recur through every section:

  1. The output is motion, not text. Many current VLAs do not produce actions through the language-model head at all. They attach a separate action expert (a flow-matching or diffusion network) that reads the backbone’s internal states. Methods that read meaning off the output vocabulary, such as the logit lens, have nothing to read there.
  2. The loop is closed. An intervention on an internal state changes the action, which changes the next camera image, which changes the next internal state. An effect measured on a single forward pass can vanish, reverse or compound over a rollout. Whether a paper measures effects in closed-loop execution or on open-loop predictions turns out to be one of the most important things to check.
  3. Evaluation is expensive. A rollout takes seconds to minutes in simulation and much longer on hardware. Sample sizes are small, often tens of rollouts per condition, and simulation benchmarks dominate.
  4. Inputs are multimodal and temporal. Images from several cameras, proprioception, language and a history of frames all feed one decision, so “which input mattered” is itself a hard question.
  5. Ground truth is sometimes available. A simulator knows the true object poses, contacts and task stage. That is a resource language-model interpretability does not have, and it changes what can be validated.

Scope and method

Question and inclusion. A work is core if it analyses, explains or intervenes on the internal computation or the decisions of a robot foundation model: a VLA, a large visuomotor diffusion or flow policy, or a world-action model. It is adjacent if it supplies context the core work builds on or should be compared against: interpretability of video world models and of reinforcement-learning agents, driving VLAs, behavioral robustness studies of VLAs, language explanations of robot behavior, interpretable-by-design policies, and older explainable-AI work on robot policies. Works that only use “interpretable” in passing, or that steer a policy’s outputs at inference time without touching its internals, were excluded.

Retrieval, 2026-10-19.

  1. arXiv API: 16 field-scoped boolean queries combining robot terms (vision-language-action, VLA, visuomotor policy, diffusion policy, world model, embodied, autonomous driving) with interpretability terms. Every query returned its full result set (at most 317 matches per query), 1,572 records in total.
  2. Crossref: the same 16 topics as keyword queries, 20 results each (320 records). Crossref contributed no work that was not also found elsewhere.
  3. Web sweep: 73 web searches by a separate agent, aimed at what a keyword search misses: workshop papers (ICML 2026 Mechanistic Interpretability workshop, NeurIPS 2026 RoboPAD and Grounded and Faithful VLMs workshops), lab blogs, project pages and papers using other vocabulary. Every arXiv id it reported was checked against the title on its arXiv page. 61 works.
  4. Backward chaining: every arXiv id cited in the reference lists of the 83 downloaded papers, 850 distinct ids, resolved through the arXiv API.

Semantic Scholar’s keyless API answered one request and then returned HTTP 429 for more than seven minutes, OpenAlex’s shared keyless budget was nearly spent by another review run the same day, and DBLP serves an anti-bot page, so none of them was used. Screening. After merging duplicates, 2,245 unique works remained. A rule screen kept works with a robot term and either one strong interpretability term (such as “mechanistic”, “sparse autoencoder”, “linear probe”, “activation patching”, “neurons”) or two weaker ones (such as “interpretable”, “attribution”, “steering”, “counterfactual”); 437 passed. A first, looser version of the rule passed 669, two thirds of them on a single weak term, and was tightened after inspection. Screening was done by one person, with no second screener.

Background: what is inside a robot foundation model

Three architecture families

Where an interpretability method can look depends on how the model produces actions. Three families cover almost every model studied in this literature.

Family Examples How actions come out What that means for interpretability
VLM with action tokens RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2023), OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024), pi0-FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025) Each action dimension is discretized and emitted by the language-model head as ordinary tokens. OpenVLA maps 256 bins per dimension onto rarely used vocabulary entries. Closest to a language model. Vocabulary projection and logit-lens style reading work directly, because actions live in the same output space as words.
VLM backbone plus a continuous action expert pi0 (Black et al., 2024), pi0.5 (Physical Intelligence, 2025), GR00T N1 (NVIDIA, 2025), SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025) A separate flow-matching or diffusion transformer generates a chunk of continuous actions while attending to the backbone’s internal states. The output vocabulary carries no actions. Meaning has to be found in the backbone states the expert reads, or inside the expert.
Visuomotor diffusion or flow policies Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023), Octo (Octo Model Team, 2024) An action sequence is denoised, conditioned on observation embeddings. Octo pairs a transformer backbone with a diffusion head. Smaller models, often trained on robot data only, so there is no language prior to inherit and fewer concepts with names.

Two variants blur the lines. OpenVLA-OFT (Kim et al., 2025) keeps the OpenVLA backbone but replaces token-by-token generation with parallel decoding of continuous action chunks. Reasoning VLAs such as embodied chain-of-thought policies (Zawalski et al., 2024) write out a plan, subtasks or object locations as text before acting, which creates a second kind of explanation to audit: the model’s own stated reasons.

Most of the models above were trained on pooled robot datasets such as Open X-Embodiment (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2023) and DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024). Most of the experiments in this review run in simulation on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), a Franka-arm benchmark with suites that vary spatial layout, objects, goals and task length, or on SimplerEnv (Li et al., 2024), which re-creates real evaluation setups in simulation.

The borrowed toolbox

Every method family in this review comes from language-model interpretability. A short reminder of what each one answers, and the caveat that travels with it:

Method Question it answers Caveat carried over from language models
Linear probe (Alain and Bengio, 2016) Is variable X linearly decodable from layer L? Decodable does not mean used. A probe can learn the task itself, so it needs control tasks or baselines (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Belinkov, 2021).
Sparse autoencoder (Cunningham et al., 2023) Which sparse, nameable directions does the model represent? Features are only as meaningful as their labels, and labels are usually assigned by eye or by another model.
Activation steering (Turner et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023) If I push the state along direction d, does behavior change as predicted? Effects can be off-target, and a working steering vector does not prove the model uses that direction on its own.
Value-vector projection (Geva et al., 2020; Geva et al., 2022) Which output tokens does a feed-forward neuron promote? Only reads what the neuron writes towards the output vocabulary, which the action-expert models do not use for actions.
Activation patching and causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022) Which components carry the information that changes the output? Patching a subspace can create the effect it claims to find (Makelov et al., 2023).
World-state probing (Li et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023) Does the model build an internal model of the environment, and is it linear? Probing a nonlinear representation linearly can understate it, and the reverse error is also common.
Faithfulness tests for stated reasoning (Lanham et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023) Does the written reasoning actually drive the output? Plausible reasoning text can be produced after the fact.

What robot training does to the pretrained VLM

A VLA starts as a VLM that knows a great deal about the visual world. The first question is how much of that survives action fine-tuning, because every later method assumes the backbone still carries usable concepts.

The answer so far is: less than in the VLM, but not nothing, and how much depends on what is measured. In OpenVLA, a linear probe for ImageNet-100 classes on intermediate visual features drops from 79.9% to 77.5% after supervised fine-tuning, and on a suite of symbol-grounding tasks the VLA falls below its own base VLM in most domains (for example 0.26 vs 0.47 on arrows), with colour the only domain at parity Kachaev et al. (2025). A benchmark that makes VLAs answer visual questions by placing a cube on one of two images finds the same shape across seven models: colour survives (82-91%), richer categories such as emotion, counting or celebrities sit near chance, and the one model co-trained on visual question answering (Magma) is the clear exception Kachaev et al. (2026). In a weight-matched pair of a VLM and the VLA built from it, depth is less decodable at every one of 36 layers, with a sharp drop in the last eight; zeroing the final MLP writes recovers most of that drop Hackett et al. (2026). A further training-side study calls the loss an “embodiment tax” and asks, from a dual-stream perspective, whether VLAs have to forget Zhang et al. (2026).

The open question is whether the knowledge is gone or just no longer routed to the output. Two results point to routing. In the cube-placement benchmark, the correct answer stays decodable above chance in middle layers and fades only towards the action layers Kachaev et al. (2026). And adding a geometric foundation model’s depth tokens to GR00T closes the probing gap almost completely, yet gives no significant improvement in manipulation success under standard fine-tuning (71.7% baseline vs 68.3-71.0% for three fusion designs, all p > 0.1) Yang et al. (2026). If missing depth were what limited the policy, restoring it should have helped.

Assessment. Both retention benchmarks compare a VLM answering in text with a VLA answering through motion. Part of the gap is the harder readout channel, not lost knowledge. The headline also fails for pi0: its base PaliGemma is at chance on every category including colour, where pi0 itself scores 86% Kachaev et al. (2026). The depth-recovery result in Hackett et al. (2026) restores the VLA to roughly its own layer-32 level, which fits “late layers reformat for the action interface” as well as “late layers destroy information”. Every study here uses one training seed, and all but one are simulation-only.

Probing: what VLAs encode

Probing is the most common method in this literature, and its results look impressive. Linear probes recover symbolic predicates such as “on”, “left of” or “grasped” at above 0.90 accuracy in almost every OpenVLA layer Lu et al. (2025). They recover task progress (R2 0.95 already at layer 0 of pi0.5’s language model) Bhardwaj et al. (2026), eventual task success Zhang et al. (2026), end-effector and target positions in near-Euclidean three-dimensional subspaces (held-out R2 0.92 and 0.75) Jacobs et al. (2026), contact Jacobs et al. (2026), and future changes in the scene Molinari et al. (2025).

The papers that test these readouts hardest show that most of them mean less than they appear to. Three findings carry the section.

1. Much of what is decodable is not specific to the policy. Progress is readable from SigLIP’s early layers, before any robot-specific computation Bhardwaj et al. (2026). Eventual success is predicted almost as well from plain DINOv2 or CLIP features (task-split R2 0.51) as from VLAs (0.55), while a random projection gets 0.39 Zhang et al. (2026). A probe that reads generic scene state from a strong vision encoder is a fact about the encoder, not about the policy.

2. Accurate on natural data is not the same as tracking the variable. On natural trajectories, time and arm pose already predict object state, so a probe can score well by reading the arm. When the object state is changed with the robot pose held fixed, readouts that were accurate to 3.9 mm on natural data barely move: the endpoint gain is 0.15 where 1.0 would mean full tracking, and similar results hold across SmolVLA, OpenVLA, pi0, GR00T N1.6 and four non-VLA encoders, with Holm-corrected sign tests Kim et al. (2026). This is the most important methodological result in the probing literature, and it undercuts most probe claims that lack controlled counterfactuals.

3. Decodable is not the same as used. With the scene held fixed and only the instruction changed, pi0.5, GR00T and two world-action models go to the object their training scene implies in 86-98% of LIBERO-Object trials, and to the newly instructed object in 0.5-13%. The same holds on a real robot (74% vs 6% for pi0.5) Jiang et al. (2026). Prompt category is 99.3% decodable in pi0.5 but has no measurable effect on behavior Grant et al. (2026). In Octo, the previous frame is decodable at every depth, yet the policy uses it only when the current frame is blacked out, and stops using it by the middle of the network Liao and Cao (2026). A workshop study probes OpenVLA-OFT for a signal of its own past actions, finds none that separates self-caused motion from hijacked motion, and concludes the policy reacts by visual servoing “Reaction Without Attribution” (2026); a blog post describes the same study “Hijacking a 7B robot policy’s” (2026).

Where probing does connect to control, it is through geometry and through the action side. In MolmoBot, distance to the target is carried by two nearly orthogonal “rulers”, one for far and one for near: erasing 32 far directions drops far-distance R2 from 0.84 to about zero while near-distance R2 stays at 0.71 Jacobs et al. (2026). The same paper separates two kinds of edit. Editing what the policy believes, such as the wrist’s position along its arc, makes the policy correct against the edit in closed loop. Editing what it commands, such as the gripper, changes the action. A control-theoretic version of the linear representation hypothesis makes the same point formally: future physical quantities should be linear in the representation given the planned action, and on OpenVLA-OFT, action-conditioned probes predict object motion where static probes do not Jeong et al. (2026).

The remaining probing work is weaker. A claim of emergent world representations in OpenVLA rests on predicting scene-embedding changes from activations that already contain the instruction and task phase, with R2 of 0.09-0.18 at one step ahead Molinari et al. (2025). Clustering GR00T’s internal states gives weak structure (silhouette 0.08-0.16) Wulff et al. (2026). Attention knockouts show that pi0.5 routes action generation almost entirely through image tokens: blocking action-to-image attention drops average LIBERO success from 96.9% to 1.0% Shi et al. (2026). A master’s thesis probes OpenVLA layer by layer and finds it ignores fixed parts of the prompt and fails on negation “Layer-Wise Structural and Mechanistic Interpretation” (2026).

Assessment. The default reading of a high probe accuracy in this literature should be “the scene contains this information and a vision encoder can see it”, until a paper shows (a) it beats a non-robot encoder baseline, (b) it responds to controlled changes of the variable Kim et al. (2026), and (c) intervening on it changes behavior in closed loop. Few papers do all three; Jacobs et al. (2026) comes closest. Probes trained only on successful demonstrations Lu et al. (2025) never see failure states, which matters for the monitoring use cases they motivate. And the two strongest negative results, “encoded but not in control” and “decodable but not responsive”, generalize across architectures, which suggests they describe how these policies work rather than a flaw in one model.

Sparse autoencoders and dictionary features

Sparse autoencoders promise what probes cannot: features discovered without deciding in advance what to look for. In robot policies they have produced the clearest closed-loop demonstrations that single internal directions matter. They have also produced a list of practical traps specific to robotics.

What works. SAEs on pi0.5 and OpenVLA residual streams give features that the authors judge interpretable in 79% of 120 sampled cases, against 30% of feed-forward neurons Swann et al. (2026). On a real Franka arm, projecting out the four most “general” features (active across many episodes) drops success on two held-out tasks from 39/40 to 0/40, while ablating episode-specific features costs almost nothing (37/40). With an empty instruction, steering one object feature moves the robot’s choice from 1/25 to 22/25 grasps of a sponge Swann et al. (2026). Ranking features by how well they line up with kinematic events (grasp, release) finds OpenVLA features whose single zero-out drops success from 70.0% to 48.8%, against 1.3 points for random features Jin et al. (2026). The largest study in the field, 394,000 closed-loop episodes across six architectures, uses SAE concept ablation and steering alongside activation injection and finds that action experts carry motor programs while the VLM pathway carries goals Grant et al. (2026).

The most careful dictionary work replaces one-dimensional features with block-sparse ones, where each feature is a small subspace. Trained on 52 million unpooled tokens from MolmoAct2 and pi0.5, these featurizers redirect the robot’s target with about two features where SAEs need three to four, and they pick out 1.4% of DROID training tokens whose removal specifically suppresses failure-recovery behavior in the retrained policy Swann et al. (2026). That last result is the first in this literature to use interpretability to shape training data rather than to read or steer a fixed model.

The traps.

  • Inserting an SAE can break the policy. In discrete-token OpenVLA, replacing a layer’s activations with the SAE reconstruction gives 0% task success at layers 16 and 24, even though the SAE explains 91-99.9% of the variance offline; only the last layer survives (34.8% vs about 67% raw) Jin et al. (2026). The six-architecture study reports the same failure Grant et al. (2026). Offline reconstruction quality does not predict closed-loop fidelity, so it should not be reported alone.
  • Mean-pooling destroys the signal. Reconstructions built from activations averaged over tokens collapse pi0.5’s success Grant et al. (2026), yet several papers pool before training the SAE.
  • The action expert and the backbone respond very differently. In pi0.5, zeroing a single backbone feature changes success by at most about 3 points, while the action expert collapses even when a random feature is zeroed Jin et al. (2026).
  • Some “dictionary” methods are not dictionaries. SAFE-Dict uses one supervised PCA direction per concept named by a language model Wen et al. (2026), and another paper in this group uses supervised probes Buurmeijer et al. (2026). Both are useful, but they find what they were told to look for.

Two papers turn features into fixes. SAFE-Dict dampens hazard-concept directions at inference time and reports attack success falling from 84.7% to 7.8% on a hazard-augmented LIBERO for OpenVLA Wen et al. (2026). SOUL uses SAE features that fire more in “state hallucination” failures, where the robot acts as if it has grasped something it has not, as targets for a LoRA unlearning objective, and reports hallucinations falling from 55.0% to 33.5% on average Lee et al. (2026). A workshop paper adds a negative result: SAE features on Octo are diagnostic and can be optimized, but reranking actions by them does not raise success “When Internal Feedback Goodharts” (2026).

Assessment. The closed-loop real-robot results are genuine progress over language-model SAE work, where behavioral validation is rare. But the controls lag behind. The “most general features” ablated to 0/40 in Swann et al. (2026) have the highest coverage by construction, and no norm- and frequency-matched random-direction control is reported, so the result may be generic damage. In Jin et al. (2026) the pi0.5 SAEs are 1x width with k=64, which explains why zeroing even a random action-expert latent collapses the policy. In SAFE-Dict the hazard lives only in the instruction text while the scene and motion stay ordinary LIBERO, and LIBERO-tuned policies largely ignore instruction details (see the probing section), so a lower attack success rate may partly measure lower task success. SOUL lacks a plain fine-tuning baseline on the same successful rollouts. Trial counts are typically 5-25 per condition, steering strengths are hand-tuned, and statistics are rare. The block-sparse work Swann et al. (2026) is the most rigorous, but its own table and text disagree on the data-shaping numbers (3.13 s to 12.93 s with 15 trials, against 1.89 s to 14.25 s with “five trials” in the text).

Activation steering and editing

Steering is where interpretability in robotics tries to become useful: change an internal state at inference time, and the robot behaves differently without retraining. The literature has moved quickly from “can it be done” to “how to do it safely”, and the results are better at the first question than the second.

The founding demonstration projects OpenVLA and pi0-FAST feed-forward neurons onto the vocabulary, finds neurons whose top tokens are “slow” or “low”, and upweights them Häon et al. (2025). In simulation the arm’s motion shifts measurably (paired t-tests on 10 tasks x 10 rollouts). On a real UR5, “slow” and “low” visibly change motion more than writing those words into the prompt, but “fast” and “high” barely differ from no intervention, and task success under steering is not reported. The method needs action tokens in the vocabulary, so it does not transfer to flow-matching action experts.

Steering costs success, so it needs control. At a fixed strength, steering the same vocabulary-chosen neurons drops average LIBERO success from 71.4% to 27.4% for height and 1.8% for speed Babu et al. (2026). Wrapping the steering strength in a PID or reinforcement-learning controller keeps success near or above baseline, but on LIBERO the controlled runs barely change height or speed at all, and the neurons selected because they map to “up” actually lower the arm Babu et al. (2026). The same closed-loop idea works in world-action models: an LQR controller on locally linearized activation dynamics raises Cosmos-Policy’s success under camera-orientation shift from 46.0% to 59.3% (30 trials per task), and avoids the over-steering that open-loop activation addition causes in LingBot-VA Hong et al. (2026).

Flow-matching experts are linearly readable but not linearly steerable. In pi0.5 and SmolVLA, speed is almost perfectly linearly separable inside the action expert, yet mean-difference and regression steering vectors move speed inconsistently, often in the same direction whether the vector is added or subtracted. An optimal-transport map between low-speed and high-speed activation distributions in late expert layers does work, mostly in the “slower, lower” direction Khayatan et al. (2026). In world-action models, linear separability of perturbed and nominal activations predicts in advance whether steering will help: it does for Cosmos-Policy and DiT4DiT, and not for LingBot-VA Hong et al. (2026).

Success-labelled steering raises success, but it is closer to adaptation than to interpretation. COAST fits subspaces from 15 successful and failed rollouts per task and softly projects the action expert’s states towards success, raising pi0.5 on LIBERO-10 from 0.43 to 0.80 with gains across four architectures and on a real DROID arm Miao et al. (2026). RoboIRS trains success-failure probes on pi0.5’s action expert and adds the probe direction, raising LIBERO-PRO success from 44.4% to 66.2% and two real-robot tasks from 18% to 38% Lei et al. (2026). Selecting attention heads by how well they retrieve demonstrated actions, and fine-tuning only those, beats full LoRA on most of five real Franka tasks with 96% fewer trainable parameters Mitra et al. (2025). Outside foundation models, the same family of ideas steers frozen multi-robot quadrotor policies by editing their latent activations Das et al. (2025); Das et al. (2026).

Edits switch behavior off, but they are neither local nor permanent. The only study that checks what an edit does to other skills subtracts per-skill task vectors from a multitask VLA. Five of ten LIBERO-Goal skills can be switched off (0/20 on held-out states), but unrelated skills keep only 52% of their success on average, four of five edits drive at least one unrelated skill to zero, and the removed skill is quickly relearned Wang et al. (2026). No activation-steering paper in this review measures off-target effects this way.

A workshop paper reports decoding and steering a VLA policy in plain English, but its page could not be checked “Reading and Steering a Vision” (2026). One further paper claims that primitive-segmented training creates a subspace that mediates few-shot transfer, but labels its own key figures “illustrative pending verification” Singh et al. (2026), so it is not used as evidence here.

Assessment. Control works more often than the semantic label does. Directions chosen because they mean “up” lower the arm Babu et al. (2026); the steering sign that helps RoboIRS points towards the failure side of its own probe in 6 of 15 tasks Lei et al. (2026); random attention heads separate tasks almost as well as the selected ones in an appendix of Mitra et al. (2025). COAST’s simulation gains are measured over deliberately undertrained checkpoints Miao et al. (2026). Most steering hyperparameters (layer, strength, sign) are tuned on rollouts of the task being evaluated, so the line between “steering a concept” and “test-time adaptation with a handful of labelled rollouts” is thin. That is still useful for deployment, but it is weak evidence about what the model represents. Real-robot results use 10-50 trials per condition, usually without tests.

Attention, layers and causal structure

This section collects the work that asks where in the network a decision is made, and through which pathway, using attention analysis, layer ablation, activation patching and attribution.

On LIBERO-style benchmarks, language is a weak and shallow input. Six papers reach this from different directions. Policies complete tasks at over 90% success when the instruction names absent objects or impossible relations Zhang et al. (2026). Most language-model blocks of pi0.5 and OpenVLA-OFT can be removed and the policy re-fine-tuned to baseline success (OpenVLA-OFT with half its language blocks removed: 98.3% vs 95.0%), while removing vision or action blocks breaks it Sun et al. (2026). Patching action experts under controlled instruction edits shows both pi0.5 and GR00T N1.7 are insensitive to rephrasing and to naming absent objects, though they react strongly to empty or direction-inverted instructions Wulff and Cangelosi (2026). Four LIBERO-trained VLAs complete none of 24 feasible new instructions in one scene, and often substitute a trained behavior instead Darwish et al. (2026). A diagnostic benchmark finds pi0.5, GR00T N1.6 and Being-H0.5 follow learned shortcuts rather than grounding instructions Xu et al. (2026).

The sharpest version of this result is causal. In fine-tuned pi0.5 and GR00T N1.7, the instruction retrieves a memorized trajectory family: 69% of 381 targeted probes replay a familiar behavior, and swapping in another task’s conditioned key-value state redirects the arm in 109 of 200 runs against 0 of 200 controls, replicated across both models Chen et al. (2026). The same paper found that LIBERO-PRO’s released evaluation code fed the robot the original instruction in its Semantic and Task cells, which affects any paper reporting those cells.

A workshop study on SmolVLA narrows the story: the failure appears only for untrained word combinations, and the policy follows a changed command on 94.6% of trials when the arm is already carrying an object, so “vision overrides language” is too broad “Partial Composition, Not Blind Substitution” (2026).

The channel from backbone to action expert is narrow. Over 775,000 activation-patching interventions in pi0.5, MolmoAct2 and MolmoBot place most of the conditioning in a band of 1-3 mid-to-late backbone layers carrying target location. Pruning MolmoBot to that band cuts compute by about a third with unchanged closed-loop success Swann et al. (2026). A unified vision-language, video and action model shows a similar depth-ordered handoff: action tokens read semantics in shallow layers, predicted future frames in middle layers and their own tokens in deep layers, and masking the middle-layer future features collapses behavior Wang et al. (2026). A claim that deep layers of OpenVLA and pi0 “ignore vision” Luo et al. (2026) is better explained by the same routing: by the deep layers, location information has moved into other token positions Swann et al. (2026).

Attention works as a monitor, but it has not been validated as an explanation. The entropy of three attention heads in a navigation VLA flags path deviations (about 42% of deviated episodes at about 10% false alarms) Jeong et al. (2026). One pi0.5 attention head identifies the object the policy is reaching for, which feeds a collision filter Park et al. (2026). Comparing object-grounded attention traces to successful references stops most failing episodes early Darwish et al. (2026). Blocking selected attention heads reportedly improves robustness to spatial perturbations, in a workshop paper whose page could not be checked “Block the Heads” (2026). None of these monitors is tested causally. Where attribution methods are compared against interventions, cheap ones fail: attribution patching is uncorrelated with activation patching for pi0.5 (r = 0.089) Wulff and Cangelosi (2026), and a masking-based visual attribution score is more faithful than attention maps or token norms Zhang et al. (2026).

Attribution across time and modality. Closed-loop Shapley attribution over phase-by-modality input blocks shows that OpenVLA-OFT switches which sensor it relies on between task phases in 25 of 30 LIBERO tasks, for example wrist camera early and proprioception late. Closed-loop attribution identifies critical inputs better than one-step action sensitivity, with task-cluster bootstrap intervals Kim and Park (2026). Empirical neural-tangent-kernel statistics distinguish policies that memorize, adapt to or ignore a factor of training-data variation, mostly on small policies Zhang et al. (2026).

Assessment. The best work here, Swann et al. (2026) and Chen et al. (2026), does what language-model interpretability does at its best: states a hypothesis, predicts which interventions should have no effect, and checks closed-loop consequences. Much of the rest uses open-loop metrics (flow loss against demonstrations, action error) to make claims about behavior. The workshop paper “Partial Composition, Not Blind Substitution” (2026) is a warning for the whole section: a 130-site patching sweep appeared to localize a grounding failure until the identical sweep on a contrast with no failure reproduced the same profile. Only 2 of 8 public checkpoints passed its competence gate, and it documents seven measurement artifacts that gave convincing but wrong results in the authors’ own pipeline. Nobody has yet traced a component-level circuit in a VLA. And nearly every finding about language comes from policies fine-tuned on LIBERO’s fixed scenes, which reward memorizing the scene; whether generalist checkpoints behave the same way is open.

Failure prediction from internals

The most practical use of a VLA’s internals is to warn before the robot fails. This is the largest cluster of applied work in the review, and it has a clear lineage.

The precedent and the reference method. Before VLAs, failure detectors for diffusion and flow imitation policies were trained only on successful rollouts, treating failure as out-of-distribution, and calibrated with conformal prediction; they reach about 78% balanced accuracy in simulation and 72% on hardware Xu et al. (2025). FIPER combines a novelty score on the policy’s observation embedding with the entropy of sampled action chunks Römer et al. (2025). SAFE carried the idea to VLAs: a small MLP or LSTM on last-layer features of OpenVLA, pi0 and pi0-FAST flags failure on held-out tasks (simulation ROC-AUC 77-82 on seen and unseen tasks), and t-SNE plots show failed rollouts from different tasks drifting into a shared region Gu et al. (2025). Most later work builds on SAFE.

Improvements on SAFE. Contrastive objectives that find the most failure-like time steps, instead of copying the episode label onto every step, beat SAFE on OpenVLA, pi0 and pi0.5, including a real arm Park et al. (2026). Pseudo-labels from action consistency plus 15% actively chosen frame annotations localize failure onset much better (timestamp AUROC 90.5 vs 76.6 for SAFE on pi0, unseen tasks) Ma et al. (2026). Training on perturbed rollouts improves robustness under deployment shift Rajaprakash et al. (2026). A probe on OpenVLA’s layer-16 MLP activations ranks pre-failure steps under occlusion at AUROC 0.97 in retrospect Mahato and Ren (2026). Workshop papers report that action-direction probe confidence in pi0.5’s action expert is the strongest failure signal on LIBERO “Interpretable Signals Reveal Failure-Relevant Representations” (2026), and that a linear probe on MemoryVLA’s memory state predicts failure at 0.86 AUC before the episode could have ended “Predictable Before Action, Resistant to” (2026).

The uncomfortable control: much of this does not need internals. SAFE’s own appendix shows that generic DINOv2 plus CLIP image features nearly match VLA features on the real robot Gu et al. (2025). Three information-theoretic statistics of the action stream (entropy and two mutual-information terms) predict failure about as well as SAFE in-domain and transfer better across models and to real robots; in-domain they reach the same balanced accuracy with or without the model’s embeddings (0.880 vs 0.881) Yang et al. (2026). Hidden states add only about 0.06 ROC-AUC over simple action statistics in a shift-aware predictor Zhu et al. (2026). Two signals computed from emitted action chunks beat hidden-state probes on early detection, by 4.6 points on average and 9.0 on unseen tasks Huang et al. (2026). The internal signals that do add something look like grounding or novelty, not knowledge of failure: ablating the most salient visual and language cache entries and measuring how little the action changes gives a detector that generalizes to unseen tasks better than SAFE Hegde and Katta (2026), and a Mahalanobis distance on noise-fixed action-expert features, combined with a chunk-consistency check, matches 32-sample baselines at about 2% extra latency Seligmann et al. (2026).

From prediction to intervention. Prediction does not yet buy recovery. The MemoryVLA workshop study tests four interventions triggered by its 0.86-AUC probe, and none reliably improves success, although aborting predicted failures early saves 27% of compute at a 2% cost in successes “Predictable Before Action, Resistant to” (2026). A probe that reads object positions from OpenVLA-OFT activations feeds a kinematic failure monitor and a safety filter, lifting LIBERO-plus success from 69.6% to 74.1%, but the probe’s share of that gain is not isolated Zhang et al. (2026).

Assessment. Failure prediction is useful engineering, but most of it is weak evidence about what VLAs represent. If generic image features or action statistics do as well, then a working monitor on hidden states shows only that failures are visible, not that the policy “knows”. Evaluation is mostly offline on recorded rollouts, “unseen tasks” usually come from the same LIBERO suite, and metrics are not comparable across papers: SAFECAST’s “ROC-AUC” is an area over a conformal alpha sweep that goes below 0.5, and on its real pi0 setup both detectors trail a trivial always-fail baseline in F1 Rajaprakash et al. (2026). The early-warning probe with AUROC 0.97 also fires more than three false warnings per clean episode Mahato and Ren (2026). Two inconsistencies were noted by the readers: GUARD’s pi0 SimplerEnv baseline numbers appear identical to SAFE’s Table 1 Hegde and Katta (2026), and ProbeAct reports 10.4 cm in a table and 12.4 cm in the text for the same quantity Zhang et al. (2026).

Faithfulness of reasoning and self-explanation

Reasoning VLAs write a plan or explanation before acting. That text looks like the explanation users want, so the question is whether it actually drives the action. Language-model work found that stated reasoning can be unfaithful (Lanham et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023). The robot work finds the link is real but partial.

The cleanest test is pre-registered. In DeepThinkVLA on LIBERO-Goal (600 episodes per condition), injecting the policy’s own clean reasoning chain against a corrupted instruction recovers 47.8 points of success (95% CI 31.7 to 63.8; all 10 tasks in the predicted direction), and corrupting the chain alone costs 20.8 points. The authors had predicted the clean chain would recover almost everything; the 38-point gap that remains is honestly reported Trinh et al. (2026). In a driving VLA, corrupting the chain of thought collapses navigation F1 from 72% to 12% Lin et al. (2026).

Three results show the limits. A pi0-based reasoning VLA writes plans that are 100% accurate on in-distribution tasks, while most task failures come from actions that do not execute the plan Wu et al. (2025). In a pi0.5-derived planner-executor model, erasing the planner’s waypoint endpoints changes the flow-matching expert’s actions by 0.6% of the change caused by resampling flow noise, and costs no success at all, so the executor ignores the plan. A naive fix makes the expert 348% more sensitive to the plan but drops success to 15% because it learns a shortcut; a gated, noise-regularized goal path makes the plan matter without that collapse Xie et al. (2026). And in driving, reinforcement learning post-training improves trajectories even when the stated reasoning stays the same or gets worse Foutter et al. (2026). Generating the action first and the explanation afterwards does slightly better than reasoning first, which suggests chain of thought works best as a training signal rather than an inference-time cause Lin et al. (2026). A workshop paper monitors and steers chain of thought in a driving VLA with a token-level reward model “Reasoning We Can TRUST” (2026).

Assessment. This is the subfield with the best experimental designs: input-level interventions on the reasoning text, closed-loop outcomes, and in Trinh et al. (2026) a pre-registered prediction that failed in an informative way. What it lacks is any look inside the network at where the instruction and the chain compete. “Sensitive to the plan” is also not the same as “faithful to the plan”, as the 348% sensitivity with 15% success in Xie et al. (2026) shows.

Security through internals

Three papers use internal signatures to defend against attacks. An SAE feature in pi0.5 and SmolVLA switches on with an adversarial patch, and a linear probe detects the patch with about 82-85% recall and under 1% false positives. Suppressing the feature only when the probe fires recovers a few points of success (pi0.5 from 26.0% to 32.4% under frequent attack), while always-on suppression badly hurts clean performance Horiba et al. (2026). Backdoored OpenVLA and DexGraspVLA models show deep-layer attention captured by the trigger, and masking and reconstructing the anomalous image tokens cuts attack success on real robots from over 90% to under 14% Li et al. (2026). TrustVLA finds a shared signature across two backdoor attacks in OpenVLA and pi0.5 (late-layer evidence becomes uniform across the image while the trigger region is promoted in deep fusion layers), validates the trigger region by counterfactual masking and inpaints it, with zero false alarms on 2,000 clean episodes Fu et al. (2026).

Assessment. The internal signatures are real and good for detection, but every effective fix ends in repairing the input image, not in editing the model. TrustVLA is the only one with counterfactual validation, paired 500-episode cells and intervals. The readers noted setup inconsistencies in Li et al. (2026): it gives OpenVLA a ViT-B/16 encoder, and states a 30% poisoning rate while 60 of each 100 demonstrations are poisoned.

Diffusion, flow and world-action policies

Policies that generate actions by denoising raise their own questions, and the answers so far are partly sobering. A small-data diffusion policy for cup grasping almost always replays one of its 30 training trajectories, even for interpolated positions or for images of cats and dogs, and an explicit lookup table matches it at 0.34% of the inference time He et al. (2025). That is a useful null model for any claim that such a policy has learned structure. When a diffusion policy is co-trained on simulation and scarce real data, success tracks how well sim and real features align while remaining linearly separable by domain, and enforcing both raises real-robot success from about 15 to 21 of 30 Lei et al. (2026). In pi0.5, the jerk at action-chunk boundaries can be steered by moving only the initial flow noise (r of about 0.97), which works for deterministic DDIM sampling but not DDPM sampling of the same Diffusion Policy Wang (2026).

World-action models, which predict future frames together with actions, are the newest target. Compared with three VLAs on LIBERO and RoboTwin, four world-action models move more smoothly and target objects more selectively at 3-20 times the latency, and their SAE features look more future-predictive, though all of this is correlational Mai et al. (2026). Their activations separate perturbed from nominal states linearly in some architectures and not others, and that separability predicts whether activation steering will help Hong et al. (2026). A workshop paper swaps imagined futures between episodes and patches attention keys and values to find the pathway through which a world-action model’s predicted future steers its actions “How Do World Action Models” (2026).

Interpretable by design

A smaller line of work builds the explanation into the model. LM-X, a 6B VLA pretrained on 20,000 hours of real robot data, also outputs task progress, the actions up to the next semantic event and per-action uncertainty, and beats GR00T N1.7 on RoboTwin (74.1% vs 55.4%) Lou et al. (2026). Other designs ground visual attention on keypoints Murooka et al. (2026), organize skill selection as a concept tree Wen et al. (2026), replace learned visual embeddings with structured image representations Mattes et al. (2026), generate task-specific policies instead of one generalist Sheidlower et al. (2024), attach self-generated reasoning to a diffusion VLA Wen et al. (2024), or add aligned world modeling to the policy Lanji et al. (2026). Earlier work measures how interpretable a neural policy is through disentangled tree representations Wang et al. (2022). A Berkeley dissertation by a co-author of the steering paper reports that VLAs stay strongly semantic after action fine-tuning “Between pixels and policies” (2025).

Assessment. Interpretable-by-design outputs are only explanations if the actions depend on them. The faithfulness results above show that this cannot be assumed: plans and reasoning are often written and then ignored. None of the by-design papers tests whether its explanatory heads drive the actions.

Methodology, positions and tools

Two pieces step back from individual results. A workshop paper lists seven conveniences that language-model interpretability tools quietly rely on: one residual stream, one unembedding matrix, homogeneous layers, a single forward pass per output, a verifiable target token, and two more. It sorts VLAs into seven architecture types, and shows that between two and six of these conveniences disappear depending on the type, while the tools keep producing plausible-looking numbers “The Hitchhiker’s Guide to Mechanistic” (2026). A position paper argues that task success rate cannot separate semantic grounding, physical execution and overlap between training and test distributions, so current evaluations cannot show that a VLA reasons about physics at all Chen et al. (2026). The behavioral evidence for that position is in LIBERO-Plus, where strong VLAs collapse under camera or initial-state changes and largely ignore language on LIBERO-Object Fei et al. (2025).

For tooling, Prisma is an open-source mechanistic interpretability toolkit for vision and video models that can be pointed at VLA vision towers “Prisma” (2025). A broad survey of VLA safety maps attacks, defenses and benchmarks but gives little attention to interpretability-based defenses “Vision-Language-Action Safety” (2026).

Adjacent literatures

Behavioral diagnostics of VLAs. A cluster of benchmark papers probes VLAs from the outside and reaches the same conclusions as the internal work: policies fail on counterfactual instructions when vision suggests a different task Fang et al. (2026), struggle to extrapolate beyond demonstrated combinations Fang et al. (2025); Li (2025), replay learned motions rather than adapting to new object properties Xu (2026), and lose 38 points of success when OpenVLA’s instruction is reworded Li et al. (2026). These are the behavioral facts that interpretability work should explain.

Video world models. Interpretability of video world models is ahead of world-action models. In V-JEPA 2 and VideoMAE-v2, physical information appears abruptly about a third of the way into the network, motion direction is a distributed circular code that needs dozens of dimensions to steer, and local spatiotemporal attention there is causally needed for physics judgments “Interpreting Physics in Video World” (2026). Related work probes world-model latents for discrete physical symbols “Probing the Latent World” (2026), steers physics with concept activation vectors “Causal Physics Steering in Video” (2026), diagnoses kinematic, contact and object-permanence structure “Event-Conditioned Diagnostics of Kinematic, Contact,” (2026), probes object memory under partial observability McMillen and Yilmaz (2026), examines world-model recovery in fine-tuned language-model planners “A Close Look At World” (2026), finds world models emerging in a navigation agent Jin and Jia (2025), analyses misgeneralization in physical sequence models Nishi et al. (2026) and offers mechanistic foundations for goal-directed control Lago (2026). A workshop paper audits how robot world-model latents affect downstream decisions “Decision-Level Auditing of Latent Robot” (2026).

Reinforcement-learning agents. The strongest causal interpretability of embodied decision making is still on game and gridworld agents, and it sets the standard robot work should meet. In a Sokoban agent, probes reveal internal plans that improve with extra thinking time, and adding probe directions makes the agent follow a different plan in up to 99% of designed levels, against about 30% for random vectors “Interpreting Emergent Planning in Model-Free” (2025). Leela’s chess network represents the optimal move two turns ahead and uses it to choose its current move, shown by activation patching and single attention-entry ablation Jenner et al. (2024); “Understanding the learned look-ahead behavior” (2025). A maze policy has 11 channels that track the cheese, and editing them or subtracting a “cheese vector” partly redirects it Mini et al. (2023). Related work dissects procedural-maze agents Trim and Grayston (2024), extracts new chess concepts from AlphaZero Schut et al. (2023), and releases 140 instruction-following Minecraft agents for studying goal misgeneralization “MultiSTEVE-1s” (2026).

Driving models. Inserting a sparse autoencoder into an end-to-end driving model exposes spurious links, such as “car ahead” coupled to traffic-light compliance, and ablating three features raises the NAVSIM score from 0.50 to 0.59 “Driving the Wrong Way” (2026). In a driving VLA, the navigation intent is linearly readable from the planning token after layer 1, but the planner produces its best trajectory only from the final layer Babu et al. (2026). Other work analyses velocity perception in driving VLMs Winter et al. (2026), steers attention towards safety-critical actors at inference time Prasad et al. (2026), shows explanations improve human mental models of self-driving cars Kenny et al. (2024), and verifies language grounding in driving VLAs “Reacting to Text Is Not” (2026).

Language explanations of robot behavior. Before VLAs, a separate literature used language models to explain robot behavior to people: summarizing multisensory logs so GPT-4 can explain and correct failures Liu et al. (2023), explaining agent behavior from observed trajectories Zhang et al. (2023); Zhang et al. (2023); Xi-Jia et al. (2025), generating explanations in human-robot interaction Sobrín-Hidalgo et al. (2024), detecting and explaining action issues with tool-using agents Izquierdo-Badiola et al. (2025), fine-tuning a VLM to detect and reason about manipulation failures Duan et al. (2024), decomposing rewards for high-level explanations Lu et al. (2023), and collecting the questions users actually ask robots Wachowiak et al. (2025). This work explains behavior from the outside and makes no claim about the policy’s internals; its strength is that it starts from what users need to know.

Earlier explainable AI for robot policies. Surveys of explainable autonomous robots “Explainable Autonomous Robots” (2021) and explainable reinforcement learning “A Survey of Explainable Reinforcement” (2022) frame the older field. Deep Taylor decomposition and relative attribution have been applied to deep manipulation policies Kim and Choi (2023), and a modified Grad-CAM has been used to explain Octo’s zero-shot failures in industrial simulation “Beyond performance” (2025).

Synthesis: what the field knows, and how well

The field is new and moving fast. Every core paper with an arXiv id dates from 2025 or 2026, and most from 2026 (see the chart in the method section). Counting over the 72 core papers read in full, with a keyword classification of the reading notes that is approximate: pi0.5 appears in 38 and OpenVLA or OpenVLA-OFT in 35, followed by pi0 or pi0-FAST (22) and GR00T (11). 29 papers are simulation-only and 6 use offline data only; 36 include a real robot, often as a small demonstration. 43 check their claims in closed loop and 37 validate an interpretation by intervening on it. Only 14 report proper statistics (intervals or tests on the main claims); 30 report some, and 27 none.

Five findings look established, because several independent papers reach them by different methods:

  1. Readable is not used. Task variables, instructions, history, progress and success are linearly decodable from VLA internals, and the policy often does not act on them Jiang et al. (2026); Kim et al. (2026); Liao and Cao (2026); Grant et al. (2026); Xie et al. (2026). Much of what is decodable is also decodable from generic vision encoders Zhang et al. (2026); Bhardwaj et al. (2026); Gu et al. (2025).
  2. On fine-tuned benchmarks, vision dominates and language selects a memorized behavior. Language is shallow and often removable, and changing the instruction with the scene fixed rarely changes the target Chen et al. (2026); Sun et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2026); Darwish et al. (2026); Jiang et al. (2026). This is a finding about LIBERO-style fine-tunes; generalist checkpoints are barely studied.
  3. The backbone talks to the action expert through a narrow channel, a few mid-to-late layers carrying target location Swann et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026), and edits behave very differently on each side of it: backbone edits are often absorbed, action-expert edits are often destructive Jin et al. (2026); Grant et al. (2026).
  4. Interventions change behavior, but semantically labelled directions are unreliable. Steering, SAE features and task vectors all move closed-loop behavior Swann et al. (2026); Miao et al. (2026); Lei et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026), but labels do not predict effects (“up” neurons that lower the arm Babu et al. (2026), success-steering along the failure side Lei et al. (2026)), linear steering fails in flow experts Khayatan et al. (2026), and edits are not local Wang et al. (2026).
  5. Stated reasoning steers actions only partly. A correct chain recovers about half of the success lost to a corrupted instruction Trinh et al. (2026), and executors can ignore explicit plans entirely Xie et al. (2026); Wu et al. (2025).

Three things are contested:

  • whether failure monitors use internal knowledge or just visible scene and action statistics Yang et al. (2026); Huang et al. (2026); Hegde and Katta (2026);
  • whether action fine-tuning destroys VLM knowledge or reroutes it Kachaev et al. (2026); Hackett et al. (2026); Yang et al. (2026);
  • how sensitive pi0.5 is to language: one study finds no prompt effect on LIBERO-Goal (83.3% to 80.0% with a null prompt) Grant et al. (2026), another a collapse under random sentences (98.0% to 11.7%) Jiang et al. (2026), with different baselines that point to different checkpoints or protocols.

The common weaknesses are methodological, and fixable. Most results come from LIBERO fine-tunes whose fixed scenes reward memorization. Probes are rarely compared against non-robot encoders or tested with controlled counterfactuals. Steering hyperparameters are tuned on the evaluated tasks. Random-direction controls matched in norm and frequency are rare. Real-robot results use 10-50 trials per condition, usually untested. And, as with language models, a localization result is only as good as the control that could have overturned it “Partial Composition, Not Blind Substitution” (2026). The workshop map of broken language-model assumptions “The Hitchhiker’s Guide to Mechanistic” (2026) and the position that success rates cannot verify physical reasoning Chen et al. (2026) frame the same problem from the top.

What has not been done at all. No paper traces a component-level circuit in a VLA. No paper compares discrete-token and flow-expert versions of the same model under matched interventions in a controlled way, although a few compare them in passing Grant et al. (2026); Buurmeijer et al. (2026); Jin et al. (2026). Forward-looking questions such as how representations change under reinforcement-learning post-training are posed but not answered “Reaction Without Attribution” (2026).

Open problems and research directions

These are my suggestions, drawn from the gaps above; they are not claims of the reviewed papers.

  1. A standard validation ladder for VLA interpretability claims. Every claim should report: a non-robot encoder baseline; a controlled counterfactual test in the style of Kim et al. (2026); a closed-loop intervention with a norm- and frequency-matched random control; and off-target effects on other skills in the style of Wang et al. (2026). Several papers do one or two of these; almost none do all four.
  2. Ground truth from the simulator. Robot interpretability has a resource language models lack: the simulator knows object poses, contacts, task phase and which demonstrations were used for training. That makes it possible to build benchmarks where the right explanation is known by construction: plant a known shortcut or a known feature in the training data, train a policy, and score whether a method recovers it. The data-shaping result Swann et al. (2026) and the memorization null model for diffusion policies He et al. (2025) point in this direction. This connects to the semi-synthetic ground-truth benchmark idea in this repository’s synthetic_data/ work, transferred from tabular explanations to robot policies.
  3. Interpretability of the action expert itself. Flow-matching experts are where actions are made, they are the most sensitive part of the model, and linear tools fail there Khayatan et al. (2026); Jin et al. (2026). Methods that respect the denoising dynamics, such as distribution-matching maps Khayatan et al. (2026) or control-theoretic steering Hong et al. (2026); Jeong et al. (2026), are a starting point.
  4. Generalist checkpoints, not LIBERO fine-tunes. The language-is-shallow result may be a property of benchmark fine-tuning. Repeating the key experiments on generalist checkpoints (pi0.5 base, GR00T base, DROID-trained policies) is cheap relative to its value.
  5. Do failure monitors need internals? Every internals-based failure detector should report action-only and generic-encoder baselines Yang et al. (2026); Huang et al. (2026). If internals help only under distribution shift, that is the claim to establish and the setting to evaluate in.
  6. Faithfulness inside the network. The reasoning-faithfulness work intervenes on text. The next step is to find where instruction and reasoning compete inside the model, building on the narrow-interface results Swann et al. (2026).

Limitations of this review
  • Forward citation chaining is missing. Semantic Scholar rate-limited and OpenAlex’s keyless budget was spent, so works citing the key papers were not collected systematically. Given how fast this field moves, this is the most likely source of omissions.
  • One screener. Inclusion decisions were made by one person without a second screener or agreement statistics.
  • Workshop papers were not read in full. Twelve core items have no arXiv version. Seven were read from their full abstracts on conference listing pages and one (a blog post) in full. Four were read only from one-line listing summaries, because OpenReview answers automated requests with HTTP 403 or the link pointed to the workshop’s own page. Each is marked in the reference list with how much was read. Their PDFs could be fetched with a real browser.
  • Reading was delegated. Core papers were read by three reader agents following a fixed schema that separates claims from critique. The section text was written from those notes and from targeted checks, not from my own full reading of every paper. Numbers in the text come from the notes, which record them from the papers’ text and tables. Where a reader flagged an inconsistency inside a paper, it is reported as such.
  • The field is very recent. Many included works are preprints from the last few months, unreviewed, and some will change between versions.
  • Classification counts are approximate. The counts in the synthesis come from keyword classification of the reading notes (scripts/notes_stats.py), not from a second pass by hand.
