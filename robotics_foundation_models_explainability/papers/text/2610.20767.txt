Can better feature discovery improve control over robot behavior through internal interventions and training data? Recent work in vision and language models reveals multidimensional features, motivating block-sparse featurizers (BSFs), which represent each feature in a low-dimensional subspace. We investigate whether recovering this structure provides an interpretable interface for steering vision-language-action models and shaping the data from which they learn. We show that BSFs recover several multi-dimensional task-relevant features underlying the model’s computation (e.g., perception, language, proprioceptive state, episode phase, and control variables). We causally validate these features through fine-grained steering: targeted changes in visual perception, semantic representation, and spatial localization produce predictable changes in downstream action prediction. Finally, we use feature-based data attribution to isolate where a specific behavior (failure recovery) arises in the training dataset, identifying fewer than 1.4% of training tokens whose masking is sufficient to remove

the targeted behavior. Overall, our results show how matching featurizers to representation geometry can provide more effective tools for understanding and changing robot behavior.
1 Introduction
A vision-language-action (VLA) model maps visual observations, language instructions, and robot state to actions in the physical world (Black et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026; Black et al., 2024; Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Deshpande et al., 2026). Although today’s VLAs rarely perform consequential real-world tasks, fully learned robotics models may soon see widespread deployment. Yet little is known about the computations underlying their actions. Understanding these internal representations will be essential for safe deployment.
One way to understand the internal representation of a learned model is through the perspective of features, which are recurring internal variables that encode concepts, states, relationships and ultimately underlie the process by which the model converts input to output. A promising line of work, e.g., sparse autoencoders (SAEs), leverages the linear representation hypothesis (LRH) (Park et al., 2023a; Huben et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Elhage et al., 2022) and assume that activations are composed of a sparse set of vectors. These methods have been well studied in language and vision modalities (Huben et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2025). Examining models in the basis of the features that they express is useful for a plethora of problems, such as to study generalization (Gallifant et al., 2025; Olson et al., 2025), steer model behavior (Templeton et al., 2026; Prasad et al., 2026; Turner et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024), attribute behaviors to training data (Kowal et al., 2026; Bergen et al., 2026; Koh & Liang, 2017), and identify safety-relevant computation (Templeton et al., 2026; Marks et al., 2025).
However, a featurizer is ultimately a hypothesis about the data-generating process of the activations (Fel et al., 2026a). The LRH is particularly questionable for visual domains involving perception, since continuous factors such as viewpoint, illumination, position, and motion naturally generate low-dimensional manifolds that are reflected in learned representations (Cohen et al., 2020; Kvinge et al., 2024; Fel et al., 2026a; Engels et al., 2025; Michaud et al., 2025). Despite this, the majority of work in VLA interpretability assumes the LRH, with varying degrees of success. Early studies showed that specific MLP directions can offer limited control of variables like speed and height (Häon et al., 2025). Complementary studies use linear probes and activation interventions to characterize the contributions of visual, language, and action pathways and identify representations of environmental dynamics and task progress (Molinari et al., 2025; Grant et al., 2026; Bhardwaj et al., 2026; Buurmeijer et al., 2026). More recently, SAEs have been applied to recover semantic concepts, motion primitives, task structure, and episode-specific memorization, demonstrating that interventions on individual features can alter policy behavior (Swann et al., 2026; Khan et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026; Grant et al., 2026).Prior work has improved our understanding of VLA policies, but precise behavioral steering remains challenging, with interventions often producing inconsistent, off-target, or weak effects. Much of this work relies on pooled visual activations or small activation corpora, leaving open whether training SAEs on individual token activations at greater scale improves feature discovery and steering.
In this work, we apply recent methods for multi-dimensional feature discovery—block-sparse featurizers (BSFs) (Fel et al., 2026a)—to frontier open-source VLA models, training BSFs and baseline SAEs on the largest corpora of unpooled VLA token activations to date. BSFs represent features as sparse low-dimensional blocks (Yuan & Lin, 2006; Eldar & Bölcskei, 2009; Jenatton et al., 2010; Bach et al., 2012) rather than single directions, making them a natural fit for robotic representations. Across models and layers, we recover a broad vocabulary of task-relevant features spanning visual perception, language, proprioceptive state, task variables and episode progress. BSFs outperform matched SAEs on recovering and intervening on task-relevant structure, suggesting that the additional geometry is useful rather than simply excess representational capacity. We then causally validate them through fine-grained steering experiments across multiple layers, inducing targeted changes at several stages of the policy: what is perceived in the observation, which object is selected, where that object is localized. Finally, we perform feature-based attribution to connect interpretable behaviors directly to the episodes in the fine-tuning dataset that shaped them. Related robot data-curation methods select demonstrations with datamodels, influence estimates, or mutual information (Dass et al., 2026; Agia et al., 2025; Hejna et al., 2025). Using a ‘failure-recovery’ feature as as case-study, we isolate fewer than 1.4% of fine-tuning tokens in DROID whose influence is disproportionately associated with this behavior. 2 From Sparse Directions to Sparse Subspaces

The goal of a featurizer is to factorize model activations into a set of interpretable concepts (Huben et al., 2024; Bricken et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2025; Bussmann et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024; Fel et al., 2025; Hindupur et al., 2025; Bussmann et al., 2025). Moreover, each featurizer also implicitly encodes a hypothesis about how activations are generated Hindupur et al. (2025). An activation with k active features lies in the Minkowski sum Sg1 + · · · + Sgk (Fel et al., 2026b). The decoder range becomes a union of sums of subspaces instead of a union of sums of rays. The observed contributions need not fill Sg. A larger unit also changes what can be read from an active feature. Unlike an SAE feature which provides a single scalar value, a block conveys both how strongly the feature is present via the block norm ||zg(x)||2, and where the activation lies on Mg, i.e., the position of zg(x) within the block, without privileging any particular basis for the block coordinates. Specifically, for any orthogonal Q ∈ O(b), replacing zg with zgQ and Dg with Q⊤Dg, combined with a matching rotation of the block encoder, preserves the feature subspace, the reconstruction, and all block norms. Unless otherwise specified, we use this freedom to display block codes in a principal-component basis in Section 3.3.

Selecting features jointly. BSFs assume that a block is active or inactive as a whole. If its coordinates could be selected independently, the block norm would no longer measure the presence of one feature, and the block would decompose back into scalar features. Sparsity must therefore act on groups rather than on coordinates, so that the coordinates of a selected block vary together and unselected blocks are exactly zero. Activation corpus and SAE baselines. Since our claims about block structure’s benefits are relative to an SAE, we aim for the strongest possible baseline. Prior SAEs trained on VLA activations have two limitations: the main experiments of Swann et al. (2026) mean-pool activations, combining all modalities into one vector; Grant et al. (2026) and Jin et al. (2026) avoid pooling but use small corpora—500k action-token activations and a few hundred simulated rollouts per SAE, respectively. Instead, we retain each image, text, and state token as a separate activation, yielding approximately 52M training activations collected uniformly across 45,344 episodes, roughly two orders of magnitude more than the pooled activations in the next largest dataset from Swann et al. (2026). All our featurizers train on this corpus, so differences in results can be attributed purely to featurizer structure.
3 Results
Given the featurizers covered in Section 2, we now turn to the features they discover and the understanding and control they admit. We first perform a quantitative comparison of BSFs to SAEs (Section 3.1) and causally validate the discovered features through open- and closed-loop steering experiments (Section 3.2). We then review a broad range of feature phenomenology, spanning visual, language, control, and proprioceptive features (Section 3.3). Finally, we demonstrate an application of BSF features for the goal of behavior modification through data attribution (Section 4).
3.1 Quantitative Comparison to SAEs
We hypothesize that BSFs—due to their inductive prior of low-dimensional features that are smooth over time— are a more natural fit to VLAs than traditional SAEs. We perform a quantitative comparison between SAEs, Vanilla BSFs, and Fisher-weighted BSFs on MolmoAct2, and use the following three metrics: (i) activation reconstruction, (ii) preservation of output behavior (i.e., action likelihood), and (iii) robotics-grounded measures of causal influence and task-variable decodability.
Figure 3 (column 1 and 2) shows held-out activation reconstruction and action fidelity (i.e., how much the teacherforced log-likelihood of recorded actions changes when prompt activations are replaced by their reconstructions) for various methods and block sizes (note, SAEs are just a special case of BSFs where block size is one). As expected, larger blocks improve both metrics at fixed block sparsity while Fisher weighting trades lower R2 for better preservation of action likelihoods. We further estimate the effective dimensionality for the features recovered using the stable rank of each (Figure 3 column 3). Vanilla and Fisher BSFs both grow slower than the block dimension, reaching a mean stable rank of approximately 5.3 for 16-dimensional blocks.

Figure 3 ((column 4 and 5) shows the comparison for the two robotics-specific metrics. First, task-variable decodability measures how well a linear probe predicts robot state and task progress from individual features, i.e., the best held-out single-feature R2 for each target, averaged across targets. Specifically, the targets are comprised of: end-effector position and displacement, episode progress, gripper state, distance to the first gripper-closure position, and whether that closure has occurred. Second, per-feature causal effect measures the change in predicted end-effector position after ablating an individual feature, where more causal features are assumed to induce a larger change. Indeed, we observe that BSFs produce both larger per-feature ablation effects than SAEs across layers as well as higher task-variable decodability, particularly through the middle and later layers. Moreover Fisher-weighted BSFs match or exceed vanilla BSFs across both causal effect and task-variable decodability.
3.2 Causal Validation of Features via Steering
Open-Loop Steering Results. A useful featurizer should enable targeted changes to policy behavior through a small number of feature edits that generalize to new scenes. We evaluate this with a redirection task in which two objects sit at opposite ends of a table, making the first predicted action chunk indicative of the robot’s intended target. We instruct the policy to pick up one object and test the minimum number of feature edits required to redirect its reach toward the other. To scale this evaluation across hundreds of scene–method combinations, we use an automated search to identify compact feature sets that achieve redirection (see Appendix B.1 for full details) on a ‘train’ set of episodes, and then hold the selected sets fixed and evaluate their effectiveness on held-out episodes.
We apply this procedure at three sites associated with distinct aspects of target grasping (Figure 4). Editing the early-layer noun token changes what the robot is looking for; editing mid-layer image tokens changes where it perceives each object; and editing the late-layer action-readout token changes the represented target position. At each steering location, replacing the activations with those from the condition targeting the other object flipped the robot’s target action towards the corresponding object. We use this activation-patching effect as a reference, measuring held-out recovery as the fraction of our steering relative to it. Held-out evaluation tests whether the selected feature sets generalize beyond the episodes we found them on, while task completion is evaluated separately in closed loop (Table 1). Full details are provided in Appendix B.1.

Each feature is one SAE latent or BSF block, and 100% recovery means matching the trajectory shift produced by activation patching (Appendix B.1). Closed-Loop Target-Object Redirection. We next test whether semantic steering, whose intervention does not depend on the specific observation, persists through closed-loop execution. The robot is asked to “pick up the [noun] and put it in the basket” for one object, while a fixed noun-token feature intervention, selected as in Figure 4, redirects it to the other object for the whole episode. We use three object pairs, each steered in both directions for 10 trials, and randomize the object and basket positions in every trial. BSF features redirect the policy more reliably with fewer features (Table 1): they select the redirected object in every trial and complete the task in 93.3% of trials using 2.3 features on average, compared with 88.3% selection and 75.0% completion for 2.5 SAE features.

3.3 Feature phenomenology
In this section, we highlight a representative sample of BSF features across models and layers, focusing on how variation within a block relates to interpretable properties of its inputs. Broadly, we find BSF features associated with visual, visual-language, and robot state and control concepts. Each point in the activation visualization represents the three-dimensional code of a block at an individual token where it activates and we center and rotate these codes using PCA, retaining all three dimensions.
Visual Features. We find visual features similar to those reported by Fel et al. (2026a) in DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025), now in VLA activations on robotics data (Figure 5). These include features selective for objects in the environment and for the robot itself. In contrast to the features found in DINOv3, latent reconstruction is not part of the VLA training objective. We show two example features in Figure 5, one which activates on ‘markers’ and another which activates on the robot. Visual-Language Features. Many blocks activate across both pixels and instruction text and are common across the tested models and layers. For example, Figure 6 shows a container feature in π0.5 that activates on external-camera, wrist-camera, and text tokens. The fruit-and-vegetable feature in Figure 1 provides another example of a block spanning related objects. Notably, three SAE features tile much of the displayed text-token geometry, while the BSF represents these related groups within a single block.
Task and Control Variables. Beyond visual and semantic content, we find blocks associated with robot configuration and spatial variables (Figure 7). A gripper feature forms an X-shaped point cloud whose organization reflects both gripper opening and the side of the gripper on which the feature activates. Notably, each side is tiled by exactly one SAE feature. The BSF block brings these complementary views of a changing two-side gripper into a single feature.
A second feature varies with the end-effector’s Cartesian coordinates over a single episode. This association is notable because the policy receives joint angles as proprioceptive input, rather than explicit Cartesian end-effector coordinates. The feature also primary activates on unused image token positions near the robot base. This use of image-tokens for information storage is a unique phenomenon to π0.5 given its bidirectional attention mask. A third example comes from MolmoBot, which is trained on synthetic data. Known camera poses allow us to relate the feature’s geometry to camera position relative to the robot, including whether the camera lies to its left or right. This feature captures a property of the observation viewpoint alongside the robot-state variables illustrated above.
Proprioceptive Features. Proprioception, the robot’s state information, enters the VLM backbone as part of the prompt. The two models encode these inputs differently: π0.5 expresses discretized state values as numeric text, while MolmoACT2 uses dedicated discrete state tokens. Despite this difference, both models exhibit features with geometry related to proprioceptive values as shown in Figure 8.
The highlighted MolmoACT2 feature notably forms a complex manifold of three looping branches that converge into a common extension. Coloring by quantized state value reveals an ordering along the curves, while coloring by state-component index reveals additional structure associated with which component is represented. The block thus organizes both numeric value and component identity. Other examples exhibit discrete clusters, curved trajectories, and more diffuse clouds, demonstrating the range of geometries associated with proprioceptive inputs across layers and models.

5 Conclusion
We have shown that BSFs recover interpretable, multidimensional features in vision-language-action models, providing tools for steering robot behavior and tracing it to training data. Targeted interventions establish these features’ behavioral relevance, while feature-guided data masking selectively delays failure recovery and preserves task performance in our evaluation. In terms of limitations, our analysis does not fully explain how these features emerge or participate in policy computation; tracing their geometry to proprioceptive inputs is a

Robust closed-loop visual and geometric steering will require tracking moving image regions and adapting to changing observations and robot state. BSFs are also not uniformly superior: SAEs perform better on some quantitative metrics, and our stronger baselines underscore the importance of unpooled activations and training scale alongside feature geometry.

Concept Based Attribution. We labeled 81 timesteps of failed-grasp recovery and 25 timesteps of successful grasps, drawn from real-robot rollouts of π0.5-DROID on a pick-and-place task. F1515 fires on 56 of the 81 recovery timesteps and on none of the 25 successful grasps. We use this feature to scan the full DROID training set for other failure-recovery behavior. For each timestep we record the F1515 block norm summed over all image and text tokens, E, and the number of tokens where the block is active, C. A timestep is active if E ≥ 150 and C ≥ 50. Runs of at least two consecutive active frames, with single-frame gaps bridged, form a recovery event. Across the entire dataset this flags 4,866 of 74,604 episodes (6.5%). The median duration is 1.1s.

Baselines. Every baseline scores the same evaluated frames. Its threshold is chosen so that its windows, built with the same event and padding rules, cover exactly as many raw frames as the F1515 windows (150,656).
• Difference of means. A direction in the mean-pooled layer-9 residual, fit to separate the 81 recovery timesteps from all other frames of the same seven episodes, with the within-class covariance shrunk toward the identity (α = 0.95, chosen by nested leave-one-episode-out cross-validation). Frames are scored by projection onto this direction.
• kNN. Each frame’s mean-pooled layer-9 residual, centered on the annotated-rollout mean, is scored by its mean cosine similarity to the five nearest recovery timesteps minus its mean cosine similarity to the five nearest successful-grasp timesteps.
• Influence Functions. Following CUPID (Agia et al., 2025), we estimate action influence, the effect of a training state–action pair on the policy’s log-likelihood of a query action, with TRAK (Park et al., 2023b). We compute per-example gradients of the flow-matching loss with respect to the action expert (430M parameters) of the baseline policy, randomly project them to 4,096 dimensions, and apply the Gauss–Newton kernel. CUPID weights action influence by the binary return of closed-loop rollouts, estimating each demonstration’s effect on expected return, and curates whole demonstrations. We target a specific behavior rather than task success. The query is therefore the annotated recovery timesteps, contrasted against the successful-grasp timesteps, and each training frame is scored as its mean influence on the recovery queries minus its mean influence on the successful-grasp queries. Scores stay at the frame level, so the same budget and windowing apply as for every other labeler.

B Steering

B.1 Open-Loop Steering Protocol

Our goal is to find a set of features which steers the robot toward a target object on a set discovery inputs and then evaluated, on held-out inputs with fresh noise seeds. Consider a scene with a banana on the left and an apple on the right. We task the robot with “pick up the banana", our goal is to steer it to instead pick up the apple using three different means: steering semantic representations (changing what the model thinks the target object is), steering visual tokens (changing where the model sees the target object) or geometry representations (changing the model’s internal representation of the location of the target object). For each of these we have a different held out set to evaluate our steering on. Semantic steering is evaluated on the other mirrored layout. So for example if we have a set of features which redirects the policy from the banana towards the apple we may evaluate this on a different scene where the position of the banana and apple are flipped. For visual token steering we evaluate with the other object’s instruction and for geometry we evaluate on an entirely new scene (leave-one-pair-out over the 13 pairs). The experimental setup is shown in Figure 11. Steering results are conducted on MolmoACT2.

Finding features. We want to find a compact set of features that steers the policy as well as patching the full activation does, since the number of features needed measures how directly a featurizer’s units capture the representation that drives the behaviour. Searching all feature subsets is intractable, so we use a greedy procedure. Candidates are the BSF blocks or SAE latents are ranked by how much their activation differs between the prompted and redirected conditions. We add features to a group until its steering effect recovers at least 75% of the patching effect. Then we remove features one at a time while the set still recovers over 75% of patching effect, so the reported sets are small but not guaranteed to be the smallest possible.

Applying features. The policy represents the task at different layers and tokens in different forms. Activation patching localizes three sites where the target object is determined: the early noun token encodes which object is requested, mid-layer image tokens encode where each object appears, and the late action-readout token encodes where the arm should move. At the L3 noun token, the selected features’ contribution is replaced with that of the redirected instruction. At the 63 L7 image tokens, the recipient’s activation within the selected features’ subspace is replaced, token by token, with that of the mirrored scene. The 63 tokens are a fixed 9 × 7 window (rows 7–13, columns 2–10 of the 14 × 14 exo-camera patch grid) covering the table region where both objects appear in every layout. These tokens are 78% of the image-token activation difference between mirrored layouts and the background remains the same between scenes. Geometric edits add one fixed vector at the L23 action-readout token.

Activation Collection We record the residual stream at the output of language backbone block L (zero-indexed). Every image, text, and state token of the is kept as a separate activation. We collect activations from MolmoAct2 and π0.5 on the DROID dataset (Khazatsky et al., 2024).For MolmoBot, which is trained entirely in simulation, we collect activations on its own training distribution (Deshpande et al., 2026). We collect and train on approximately 50M activations for each model and layer combination. To eliminate issues with outlier tokens we filter tokens by activation magnitude [p0.5, min(p99.5, 8 · median)], removes between 1–5% of tokens depending on the layer. We then subtract a per-position mean computed over training tokens and rescale activations to a mean squared norm of d.
