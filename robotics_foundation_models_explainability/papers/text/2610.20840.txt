Robotic manipulation is inherently geometric, but what shape does the world take inside the policy that performs it? Prior work on vision-language-action (VLA) policies establishes that task-relevant variables are linearly readable from activations, yet readability certifies only that a variable is present and says nothing about the shape its values trace, which may be curved and multi-dimensional. Using controlled sweeps, natural rollouts, linear probes, and activation interventions, we build a taxonomy of seven task-relevant concepts in VLA models. We find that realized geometry is predominantly multi-dimensional and is shaped by the constraints under which the policy acts. Physical joint limits, for example, leave wrist orientation an open arc rather than a circle, and distance to the target, a single scalar, is realized in two orthogonal subspaces, a coarse ruler for the approach and a fine ruler for the grasp. The same geometries reappear in two further VLA models on real-world robot data, indicating that they are neither model-specific nor artifacts of simulation. We demonstrate how the findings are actionable by steering along the geometry a concept realizes. Overall, this work lays out an initial phenomenology of realized geometry in VLA policies and demonstrates that a concept’s shape carries as much importance as its readability.

1 Introduction
Robotic manipulation is inherently geometric. Robotic systems operate in three-dimensional space through articulated kinematic structures, while the objects they manipulate and the tasks they perform have their own geometric organization, including symmetries, constraints, and phases of interaction. Any general manipulation policy must contend with this structure, and vision-language-action (VLA) models have become the dominant paradigm for building such policies (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b; Lee et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026; Deshpande et al., 2026). VLAs inherit visual and semantic priors from backbones pretrained on internet-scale data, including vision encoders (Radford et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2023), language models (Touvron et al., 2023; Gemma Team et al., 2024), and full vision-language models (Driess et al., 2023; Beyer et al., 2024; Deitke et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). This data has grown by orders of magnitude in volume and diversity (O’Neill et al., 2024; Walke et al., 2023; Bu et al., 2025; Deshpande et al., 2026; Bjorck et al., 2025), with generalization improving along power-law-like trends (Lin et al., 2025). The resulting systems are increasingly capable, general, and even deployed in the real world (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b,a; Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025; Figure AI, 2026; Gemini Robotics Team, 2026; Figure AI, 2025b,a).
How such competence is reflected in the representations of these VLAs is the subject of a nascent literature (Qi et al., 2025, 2026; Swann et al., 2026; Buurmeijer et al., 2026; Häon et al., 2025; Bhardwaj et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2025; Molinari et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025; Grant et al., 2026; Goyal, 2026; Yang & Mokalla, 2026; Shi et al., 2026). In particular, one line of work uses probes, testing whether a quantity of interest can be read out from the model’s activations (Joseph et al., 2026; Buurmeijer et al., 2026; Bhardwaj et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2025; Molinari et al., 2025; Grant et al., 2026; Yang & Mokalla, 2026). A second trains sparse autoencoders (SAEs) (Cunningham et al., 2024; Bussmann et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b; Fel et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025; Hindupur et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Bricken et al., 2023; Lubana et al., 2026; Bhalla et al., 2026b; Templeton et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a; Lieberum et al., 2024; Thasarathan et al., 2025; Makhzani & Frey, 2014) to discover concepts without supervision (Swann et al., 2026; Khan et al., 2025; Grant et al., 2026), and the discovered features even provide control over the policy via steering Joseph et al. (2025).
Both lines of work share a limitation. A probe can ensure that a variable is decodable, yet says nothing about the structure through which the model “realizes” it, and an SAE inherits the linear representation hypothesis (LRH) (Park et al., 2024; Costa et al., 2025), which casts each concept as a single direction in activation space and therefore fixes that structure in advance. Interpretability for language and vision models, conscious of this limitation, has moved beyond the single direction to multi-dimensional features and curved geometry (Modell et al., 2025; Feucht et al., 2026; Wurgaft et al., 2026; Bhalla et al., 2026a; Fel et al., 2026b,a; Engels et al., 2025; Lubana et al., 2026; Karkada et al., 2026; Dooms & Gauderis, 2025; Kantamneni & Tegmark, 2025; Gurnee et al., 2026; Yocum et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025a; Sarfati et al., 2026; Costa et al., 2025; Pearce et al., 2025; Diego-Simón et al., 2024; Chung et al., 2018; Cohen et al., 2020; Hosseini et al., 2026; Dhimoila et al., 2026; Fel et al., 2025; Gorton, 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Tvetkova et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025b, 2026), a line of work now gathered under the name of Neural Geometry (Geiger et al., 2026).
Do VLAs realize their variables through similarly rich geometry? Two facts suggest they might. First, vision models, including the backbones VLAs inherit, already carry colors, shadows, and position as multi-dimensional features rather than single directions (Fel et al., 2026b,a; Pach et al., 2026; Uselis et al., 2026). Second, representations tend to inherit the symmetries of their training data, so that cyclic concepts, for example, come to be arranged on circles (Karkada et al., 2026). The variables a manipulation policy must track, such as poses, distances, contacts, and task phase, are geometric objects with symmetries of their own before the network ever represents them.
We take up this question and make the following contributions:
• Realized geometry as a lens for VLA interpretability. We formalize the distinction between concept realization—the capacity to extract information about a concept under a given readout—and its realized geometry—the structure activations trace as the concept varies. Linear readability establishes only that a concept is present, while its realized geometry determines how its values are structured and whether they can be manipulated.
• A taxonomy of realized geometry in MolmoBot. Combining controlled sweeps, natural rollouts, linear probes, and activation interventions, we chart the realized geometry of seven control concepts: gripper state,

2 Realization of Control Geometry
Before specifying the seven concepts of interest that this study will examine and documenting their realized geometry, we first clarify what is meant by realized and by geometry. In particular, our aim here is to distinguish two questions that are frequently conflated in the literature: whether a concept can be recovered from a policy’s activations – which we term realized – and what geometric shape that recovered concept takes within activation space – which we term realized geometry. The first question asks about recoverability, the second about structure; we now formalize those notions.

Notation. Throughout, bold symbols denote vector or matrix valued quantities and calligraphic symbols their
domains. We model observations as arising from a data-generating process whose latent state collects m concepts, writing [m] = {1, . . . , m} and z = (z1, . . . , zm) ∼ pz for a state drawn from Z = Z1 × · · · × Zm. Borrowing the language of conceptual spaces (Gärdenfors, 2000), we take each concept i ∈ [m] to be a structured domain
(Zi, di) rather than a single vector, so that Zi supplies the values the concept can assume; a domain may be discrete, continuous, or manifold-valued, with an intrinsic dimension ki = dim(Zi) when smooth. We write x and a for a single input and activation, and X and A ∈ Rn×d for n of them stacked row-wise.

Data-generating process. We consider the minimal data-generating process

z ∼ pz, x = g (z) ∈ X , a = f (x) ∈ A ⊆ Rd,

(1)

in which the (potentially stochastic) generator g : Z → X combines the concepts z and instantiates an observation x (e.g. an image), while f : X → A maps that observation to an activation a at a fixed layer of a VLA. In this work, we will focus on a particular set of concepts which are control-relevant and are modeled by both a quantity and its possible values1, so that Zi may describe a binary gripper state, a scalar distance in R+, an end-effector position in R3, or a wrist orientation in S1. These are properties of the world or of the control process, and the policy accesses them only through the observation that the generator g produces, so that nothing guarantees a concept present in z remains recoverable from a. Whether it does is the first thing to establish, and it is a question that shall become well posed only once the means of recovery is fixed.
Definition 1 (Concept realization). Let (Zi, di) be the concept domain of interest and V be a class of readouts γ : A → Zi, for ε ≥ 0, we say that concept i is (V, ε)-realized in a if

inf E (di (γ (a) , zi)) ≤ ε,

(2)

γ ∈V

where the expectation is taken over the process in Equation 1.

This definition is relative to both a readout class and an error tolerance. It therefore specifies not only whether information about zi is present, but also how “easily” it can be extracted. This follows the same operational principle as predictive V-information (Xu et al., 2020): usable information is defined relative to a class of predictors. In particular, unless specified, our main case is the class of affine readouts γ (a) = W a + b. When concept i is realized under this class, we say that it is linearly readable. We reserve realization for the general, readout-relative notion and use linear readability only for this particular case.
An important remark before jumping to the realized geometry is that realization does not imply that the activations are isomorphic to the concept domain2 or that the policy causally “uses” the recoverable information. Finally, it also lacks a description of the geometry of the conceptual space itself, which we now turn our attention to.

Realized geometry. Knowing that a concept can be read from the activations tells us nothing about how its values are laid out, and to describe this layout we fix a context c = z[m]\{i} containing all the other concepts and look at how the activations move when only zi changes.

Definition 2 (Realized geometry). The activation map of concept i in context c is

φi(v; c) = E a | zi = v, z[m]\{i} = c , v ∈ Zi,

(3)

and the realized geometry of the concept is the image Mi(c) = φi(Zi; c) together with the map that produces it.

In words, the realized geometry is the path traced by the activations when only concept i moves, for example two points for the gripper state, a curve for a distance, or an arc for the wrist angle. This path is what just monitoring would overlook, since, for example a linear probe only looks at a few directions of activation space and the path can take any shape in the others without changing its accuracy (App. A). The rest of this work estimates φi, directly through controlled sweeps when the simulator lets us move one concept and hold the rest fixed, and through teleports and natural rollouts when it does not, and then tests with interventions whether the policy actually uses the geometry we find.

3 A Taxonomy of Realized Geometry in MolmoBot
We now build a taxonomy of how a model realizes the variables of pick and pick-and-place tasks. Our subject is MolmoBot (Deshpande et al., 2026), which pairs a VLM that reads the language instruction and camera images of the scene with an action expert that reads the robot’s proprioceptive state and generates action chunks via flow matching, cross-attending to the VLM at each layer (Appendix C.1). We choose MolmoBot for three reasons: it is a relatively strong policy on numerous benchmarks (Deshpande et al., 2026), and we hypothesize that stronger models realize task geometry more faithfully; its weights are open, so we can record and edit activations; and it runs in MolmoSpaces (Kim et al., 2026), which provides ground-truth task variables and lets us intervene on the world mid-rollout. We study seven variables in three groups: spatial geometry (EE position, target object position, and distance to the target), task structure (contact and task phase), and action geometry (wrist orientation and gripper aperture). For each, we ask whether the model represents it and with what geometry.

We begin with spatial geometry in the VLM backbone, which receives no proprioception and must infer these variables from images: EE position, target object position, and distance to the target.

For both variables, three dimensions are necessary and sufficient: held-out R2 rises sharply up to rank 3 and does not improve beyond it, reaching 0.92 for EE position (layer 20) and 0.75 for the target (layer 21), with per-axis error within the gripper’s width (Fig. 2a–c). Position thus sets the taxonomy’s baseline: from pixels alone, the VLM re-encodes a Euclidean quantity at its native dimension and approximately its native metric.3 We next turn to a variable that couples the two: distance to the target.
Distance to target object. Distance to the target is a scalar, but the policy needs it at two very different scales: it must tolerate noise during the approach yet resolve the grasp to within a centimeter. A single direction with finite precision may struggle to carry both, so we hypothesize that the model encodes distance in more than one subspace. We fit separate linear probes on near (<15 cm) and far (15–70 cm) states and iteratively erase one probe’s directions from the activations (Appendix C.4). Each code survives removal of the other: erasing 32 far directions drops far R2 from 0.84 to near zero while near R2 stays at 0.71, and vice versa (Fig. 3a).
To isolate the two codes, we fit a meter probe with targets clipped below 15 cm and a centimeter probe with targets clipped above 15 cm, a cutoff that is Pareto-optimal for both ranges (Fig. 3b). Each probe reads its own range well (R2 = 0.81 and 0.70) and saturates outside it, so activations projected onto the two readouts trace an L (Fig. 3d). The two probe directions are nearly orthogonal, with a cosine of 0.04 against a split-half ceiling of ∼0.23 (Fig. 3c). Clipping does not impose this orthogonality, since a probe can only select directions the activations contain. On synthetic activations that encode distance along a single direction, the near and far probes at the 15 cm boundary come out nearly parallel (cosine 0.95 for the clipped pair, 0.92 for the range-split pair), so neither clipping nor range-restricted fitting yields orthogonal probes on its own. In the model, probes fit separately on each range, without clipping, are also nearly orthogonal (0.04 vs. ∼0.18; Fig. 3c). Distance thus departs from the position baseline: the model realizes a single scalar along separate directions for coarse and fine scales, consistent with the limited precision of a representation bending how a concept is encoded.
3.2 Task Structure

Contact. Contact with the target is binary, so we treat it as a two-class problem and use linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to find the direction that separates the classes. We label each state of the rollouts by simulator contact and fit the discriminant on VLM activations (layer 24). Contact and no-contact states separate cleanly even in an unsupervised PCA of natural activations (Fig. 4a), and the discriminant decodes contact with 91.3% accuracy. Belief. Some structures encode the model’s estimate of its current state, such as wrist angle, or its relationship to the scene, such as the distance to the object. Editing such structures effectively amounts to affecting the policy’s perception and can thus only affect behavior indirectly. The policy always seeks to close the gap between perceived state and goal state, meaning the behavior will shift against the edit, producing what we call a compensation effect.
Action. Other structures directly represent the commanded behavior, such as the gripper. Editing such structures affects behavior directly, while leaving other outputs, such as other joints, unchanged. To identify these edits, we reverse engineer the desired behavior by pulling a target output action back into activation space.

3.3 Action Geometry
So far, we have studied variables by reading them out. For the last two, wrist orientation and gripper aperture, we also write to them, which reveals a distinction that readout alone cannot: whether an edit lands on what the model believes or on what it commands (Box 1).
Wrist orientation. Orientation is cyclic, so its most compact code would be a circle. However, the wrist’s roll joint is limited to ±172.8◦ and never completes a full turn, and the action expert receives this angle directly as a raw, non-wrapping joint reading. Consistent with this, sweeping the joint through its range (Appendix Fig. 20) traces an open arc in the action expert (layer 9), whose ends lie far apart in activation space despite being only 14◦ apart physically (Fig. 6a). The action expert thus encodes an unwrapped angle: after a full turn past the joint limit (overridden in simulation), the images are identical but its activation does not return to the start, while the VLM’s does (Appendix Fig. 22). To write to this code, we cannot add one fixed vector: on an open arc, the direction that advances the rotation depends on where the activation sits (Fig. 6a). We instead train a small MLP autoencoder that maps activations onto an exact circle (Fig. 6b), so that rotating by θ is the same operation at every state; the edit is the decoded difference between the rotated and unrotated points (Appendix C.8). This edit is built from the representation alone and does not command motion, making it a belief edit (Box 1). Applied once mid-rollout, it produces a compensation effect: the decoded action rotates 0.33 ± 0.08 against each written degree (Fig. 6c), as if the policy were closing the gap between its believed angle and its goal. Held at every query, the corrections compound, and the wrist rotates opposite the edit by 1.0–1.3× the written angle (Fig. 6d). Thus, even a geometry-matched edit lands on a belief, so for the gripper we work from the action side.

Gripper. The gripper command is an action variable (see Box 1), so a natural question is, then, how can we edit just the gripper action? Here, we derive the edit from the action output. For each sampled rollout state, we use pullback (Wurgaft et al., 2026) to find a steering vector δ, added as h ← h + δ in the action expert (layer 32), optimized with gradients from the action output until the decoded gripper command flips while the arm

joints stay fixed. Natural activations form two clouds by gripper state, and each successful steering vector points from its own cloud toward the other (Fig. 7c), so edits derived only from action gradients recover the axis that separates the states. These edits are largely one-dimensional: their first principal component captures ∼70% of their variance (Fig. 7a) and is sufficient to accurately decode the commanded gripper state (Fig. 7b). This direction is not fully isolated, however: steering along it also moves the arm joints, and suppressing this leakage requires additional components (Fig. 7d). Together, the wrist and gripper show two things. First, physical limits shape realized geometry: a variable we hypothesized would be circular is encoded as an arc. Second, recovering a variable’s geometry does not determine how editing it affects behavior: edits along the wrist’s arc land on a belief and elicit compensation, whereas edits derived from gripper action gradients change the action.
4 Representations in Real Robots
All experiments in Section 3 were run in simulation. Since the gap between simulation and reality remains a central open problem in robotics (Jakobi et al., 1995; Tobin et al., 2017; Kadian et al., 2020), we ask whether the geometry we found carries over to real data. We repeat our analyses on MolmoBot and two additional VLAs, π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b) and MolmoAct2 (Fang et al., 2026), using episodes from the real-world DROID dataset (Khazatsky et al., 2024). Real data lacks the privileged information of simulation, such as ground-truth object poses and mid-rollout interventions, so we focus on four variables we can measure: gripper aperture, wrist orientation, EE position, and distance to target, for which we use distance to the eventual grasp location as a proxy. All four variables are recovered in every model, with cross-model Procrustes similarities well above a shuffledvariable null (Fig. 8). The gripper structure is the most consistent across models: pullback steering vectors again separate closing from opening, with the first principal component carrying 31–59% of their variance. EE position is linearly decodable in all three models (R2 of 0.78–0.92), and the probed activations follow the arm smoothly through an episode. Wrist orientation is decoded less well (R2 of 0.52–0.69) and agrees least across models. Most notably, the two-scale distance code reappears: in all three models, far points spread along the meter ruler while near points split off onto the centimeter ruler. This structure is sharper in π0.5 and MolmoAct2 than in MolmoBot, consistent with MolmoBot being evaluated on real data outside its simulated training distribution. Together, these results suggest that the geometry we identified is neither an artifact of simulation nor specific to one model, but a recurring way in which VLAs organize the variables needed for manipulation.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we asked what geometry manipulation variables realize inside VLAs. In MolmoBot, the answer is rarely a single direction: position occupies a near-Euclidean 3-D subspace, wrist orientation an open arc imposed by joint limits, and distance two nearly orthogonal rulers, and this structure largely recurs in π0.5 and MolmoAct2 on real data. Two lessons follow. First, decodability is only a starting point: a variable can be linearly readable yet require a geometry-matched edit to steer, and even then the edit may land on a belief the policy compensates against rather than an action it executes. Interpretability tools for VLAs should therefore match both the geometry a concept realizes and its role in control. Second, this geometry is shaped by the policy’s inputs and physical limits, so design choices such as how joint angles are encoded shape how the model represents them. Our taxonomy is supervised, but it provides ground truth against which unsupervised methods, such as block-sparse featurizers (Fel et al., 2026a), can be validated. We believe the geometry of VLAs is a promising direction for both interpretability and better policies.

A Realization does not determine Geometric Realization.

For example, linear readability does not imply linear realized geometry. Consider the map

t

φ (t) = cos t .

(4)

sin t

The value t is exactly linearly readable through γ (a) = a1, yet its realized geometry is a helix. The concept is intrinsically one-dimensional, but its trace is curved and occupies several activation dimensions. Thus, the subspace used by a linear readout should not be confused with the shape traced by the concept itself.

This distinction also matters for control. A single fixed steering direction v can follow a concept globally only

when

φi,c (u + δ) − φi,c (u) ≈ vδ

(5)

throughout the relevant range. For a curved or multiscale geometry, the appropriate activation steering vector instead changes with the current concept value. Another way to put it is that a readout specifies how to measure a concept from an activation but it does not necessarily specify how to move the activation while remaining on the geometry realized by that concept. Linear readability therefore does not imply linear steerability.

B Extended Results
This appendix collects the figures that support the main text; Figure 34 gives an overview of the seven task variables, and experimental details are in Appendix C.
End-effector and target object position (App. C.3). Figure 35 shows the crossed arm×object teleports used for both position variables: unlike the object teleports, both the arm and the object move, so the horizontal positions of the two vary nearly independently on the same states. The action expert also carries rank-3 subspaces for EE position (Figure 9) and target position (Figure 10); the EE subspace is a stretched rather than rigid copy of world coordinates, and the target, which reaches the action expert only through cross-attention to the VLM, is decoded with larger error than in the VLM. Figure 11 marks the token groups and layers used in Figure 2: within a scene, EE position is decodable from the first layer, whereas target position appears on the instruction and final tokens between layers 12 and 21, and in the action expert only from layer 18. Figure 36 shows that the VLM’s target code is a position rather than a map of the workspace: an occupancy map read from the activations is no more accurate than one built from the target’s true coordinates, objects the instruction does not name are detected per image patch, and a table-position readout fit on all visible objects does not track the target as it moves within a scene, whereas one fit on the target alone does on the exo camera.
Distance to target object (App. C.4). Figure 15 shows the object placements in the object-teleport dataset used for distance and direction; only the object is teleported. Figure 12 shows that a single full-range probe is most accurate with a compressive link (log d or 1/d) below 15 cm and with a linear link in the farthest bins, and that the centimeter and meter rulers each score only on their own side of the cutoff. Figure 13 repeats the erasure test and both rulers at the action expert’s last layer and finds the VLM’s two-subspace code.
Direction to target object (App. C.5). Figure 14 shows that, unlike distance, direction to the target is read by a single probe: decoded directions point toward the target across the workspace, and splitting the probe by range does not improve near reads and improves far reads by at most 1.1◦.
Contact (App. C.6). Figure 16 shows where in the VLM contact is decodable: from the wrist-camera patches already at the first layer, while the exo-camera patches and text tokens reach a similar level only in the middle layers; at layer 24, the per-patch signal covers the whole image rather than only the fingers.
Phase (App. C.7). Figure 17 shows the phase subspace in the VLM’s vision tokens: the four phases occupy separate regions in temporal order, and freezing shifts the VLM’s decoded phase toward grasp, as in the action expert. Figure 18 compares the frozen read across the action expert and both VLM token groups: in all three, the decoded phase shifts toward grasp, while place landmarks are most often still decoded as place. Figure 19 gives the per-step time course of the freezing effect on gripper commands in each phase.

C Experimental Details
C.1 MolmoBot Architecture
MolmoBot (Deshpande et al., 2026) consists of two streams: a vision-language model (VLM) and an action expert. The VLM takes in the language instruction and images from two cameras: an exocentric camera providing a fixed third-person view of the workspace, and a wrist camera mounted on the end effector providing a first-person view that moves with the gripper. At each query after the first, the VLM receives the current frame and a frame from eight steps earlier from each camera; the first query of an episode, including every state recorded right after a teleport, sees only the current frames. The VLM receives no proprioceptive input, so any information it carries about the robot’s state must be recovered from these observations.
The action expert takes in the robot’s proprioceptive state together with noisy action tokens, which it denoises into an action chunk over 10 steps of flow matching. The two streams contain 36 layers each, and each layer of the action expert cross-attends to the VLM layer at the same depth, so visual and language information can enter the action computation at every level. Information flows in one direction only: the action expert reads from the VLM, but the VLM does not attend to the action expert. The action expert can therefore access the target object, which appears only in the camera images, solely through this cross-attention.
C.2 Data
Simulation experiments run in MolmoSpaces on ProcTHOR (Deitke et al., 2022) houses. All use the pick task (“Pick up the X”) except phase, which uses pick-and-place (“Pick up the X and place it in or on the Y”). Every probe is 5-fold cross-validated with houses held out. We collect four datasets:
1. Natural pick rollouts (1,008 episodes, 196 houses), with action-expert activations at every action-chunk query. Used for wrist orientation, the gripper pullback, and contact (for which we re-run 299 episodes in the same houses and with the same seeds to record VLM activations).
2. Object teleports (906 scenes). The target is teleported to up to 16 placements per scene (Figure 15) and activations are recorded at the first four chunk boundaries, with an additional near-range set of placements within 35 cm of the hand (68,174 states in total). Used for distance and direction.
3. Crossed arm×object teleports (945 scenes). Five object placements are crossed with ten arm poses, recorded immediately after the teleport (q0) and after one executed chunk (q1). Used for both position variables.
4. Pick-and-place rollouts (530 episodes, 181 houses, 63% success). Used for phase.
Position, distance, direction, and contact are read from the VLM residual stream at the token positions given below (a single final token, or the mean over a named set of tokens). Phase, wrist orientation, and the gripper are read from the action expert, averaged over the 16 action tokens at the final flow step unless noted. Layers are indexed from 0 in both streams.
C.3 End-Effector and Target Object Position
Data. In each scene we pick ten gripper targets at random in a box in front of the robot (20–60 cm forward, up to 30 cm to either side, 5–40 cm above the object’s resting position) and move the arm there with inverse kinematics. We keep a target only if the gripper gets within 1.5 cm of it without the arm intersecting anything, and we drop arm–object pairs in which the gripper is within 12 cm of the object. Each arm pose is paired with five object placements chosen the same way as in the object-teleport dataset. After each teleport the robot holds its new arm configuration, so the policy only sees frames taken after the move. Labels are the gripper and object positions read from the simulator after the teleport, in the robot’s base frame.
Probes. For each variable we average the activations over a group of tokens and fit a ridge regression onto its (x, y, z) position, with houses held out; both variables use the same rows and folds. We use the token group and layer that decode each variable best: for EE position, the non-image tokens before the instruction (the short labels that introduce each camera image, such as “Image 1 View 1”, the image start and end markers, and the beginning-of-sequence token) at layer 20; for the target, the instruction tokens at layer 21. To count how many dimensions a readout uses, we restrict its predictions to their top one, two, or three principal directions (the rank curves) and report the singular values of the predictions. Table height differs between scenes but not within one, so part of the z signal only identifies the scene; subtracting each scene’s mean from the true and predicted
37

positions removes it (within-scene R2: 0.92 for EE, 0.57 for the target). The true x and y of each variable predict the other’s at R2 ≤ 0.02.
Equivariance. To test whether a subspace preserves spatial geometry, we take pairs of states from the same scene that differ in one variable only: two arm poses with the object fixed, or two object placements with the arm fixed (the object moves only horizontally, so its displacement is 2-D). On training scenes we fit a map from the world displacement to the resulting change in activation within the rank-3 subspace, and on held-out scenes we report the median relative error of its predictions, once for any linear map and once for a rotation with uniform scaling (Procrustes). Shuffling which world displacement goes with which activation change gives an error of 1.00.
Curvature. We move the hand along four straight lines (forward, sideways, up, and toward the object; 47 scenes) and the object along straight lines with the arm at its home pose (43 scenes), up to 24 points per line, and project the activations onto the subspace fit without those scenes. Curvature is the median angle between consecutive ∼7 cm segments of the projected path, binned by hand–object distance and compared with the same activations projected onto random 3-D subspaces (median of eight). The paths in Figure 2 were traced separately in one of these held-out scenes.
Action expert (Figure 9). Applied to the action expert’s activations, the same pipeline decodes EE position best at layer 12 (R2 = 0.998, 1.1 cm error). There, a general linear map predicts the displacements almost exactly (error 0.04) but a rotation with uniform scaling does not (0.29), so the subspace is a stretched rather than a rigid copy of world coordinates.
C.4 Distance to Target Object
Data. We use the object-teleport dataset, keeping the 67,778 states in which the object is within 70 cm of the gripper (905 scenes; 11,534 states below 15 cm). Distance d is the straight-line distance between the gripper and the object, read from the simulator. Activations are the VLM’s final token at layer 21; the action expert’s last layer (35) gives the same main results (Figure 13). The erasure test, the left panel of Figure 12, and the EE control below use only the first round of teleports, recorded before the extra near-range placements were added (52,210 states; 4,422 below 15 cm).
Erasure (Figure 3a). We split states into near (below 15 cm) and far (15–70 cm). Within each cross-validation fold, we fit a linear probe for distance on one range, remove its direction from all activations, and repeat, fitting each new probe on the already-cleaned activations and making its direction orthogonal to those already removed, up to 32 times. After each removal we refit a probe on each range and report its held-out R2, computed against the variance within that range.
Rulers and cutoff sweep. The meter ruler is a ridge probe fit on all states to distance with every value below 15 cm set to 15 cm; the centimeter ruler is the mirror image, with every value above 15 cm set to 15 cm. Each is scored against the true distance within its own range. To judge whether two probes point in different directions, we compare the cosine between them with the cosine expected if both measured the same direction: for each probe, the cosine between two copies fit on disjoint halves of the houses (median of three random splits), taking the lower of the two probes’ values. The cutoff sweep repeats this with the cutoff moved from 3 to 56 cm, fitting the pair either only on the states on each side of the cutoff (range-split) or on all states with values clipped at the cutoff (round-to-c), and compares both with a single probe fit on the whole range and then rescaled and shifted separately on each side of the cutoff.
Controls. The near axis (the direction of the near-range probe) is not a disguised copy of EE position: EE position predicts nothing about distance among near states (R2 ≈ 0), and less than 3% of the near axis falls within the subspaces that encode EE and target position. It does not detect contact either: the centimeter ruler’s error is the same on states in which the object touches nothing but its supporting surface (1.46 vs. 1.45 cm). For the single-direction control, we build synthetic activations in which distance is written along one random direction, plus noise and a per-scene offset, and run the same probes on them.
38

C.5 Direction to Target Object
We use the same data and layer as for distance. The target is the unit bearing u from EE to object in the base frame, decoded by ridge regression onto its three components. Error is the angle between decoded and true bearing, compared to a training-mean baseline (33.1◦) and a scene-mean oracle (19.0◦). Since the bearing is a nonlinear function of the two positions, we check that the model does not simply recompute it from them: the residual of u after regressing out [pEE, pobj, d] remains decodable (R2 of 0.3–0.5 per component), and the residual probe has little energy in the position and distance subspaces (0.01–0.04). In Figure 14, decoded bearings are averaged in 6 cm cells of a target-centered frame, and the cutoff sweep follows the distance protocol.
C.6 Contact
Data and probe. We record VLM activations by re-running the policy in the same house and with the same seed as each of 299 natural pick episodes. The re-runs are not copies of the originals: in 259 of them a different target object is sampled, and bf16 inference makes even repeated runs differ, so each state is labeled from the re-run itself: contact means some part of the gripper touches the object with nonzero contact force. We average the layer-24 activations over the text tokens of the prompt (the instruction and its chat-template words) and fit a linear discriminant (LDA) for contact, with houses held out. It reaches 91.3% held-out accuracy against a 65% majority baseline (AUC 0.954); the final token alone gives the same accuracy (AUC 0.957). Figure 4a is a PCA of the same pooled activations. For Figure 4b and the tests below, the probe is never trained on intervention states: each test pose is scored by the probe refit on natural states with that pose’s house held out, so no tested scene is in its training data.
Off-schedule interventions. To make contact start or end at moments when it would not naturally change, we stop 123 natural pick episodes in the same houses (100 of them among the 299) at up to four poses of each kind, hold the arm fixed, and teleport the object in 1 cm steps along the line between the gripper and the object, continuing three steps past the change: toward the gripper at poses where the policy commands it open, until the object first touches it (early touch), or away from the gripper at poses where it touches the object, usually in a closed grasp, until contact breaks (induced slip). After each step, both cameras are re-rendered from the arm’s current pose. We repeat each test with the gripper forced to the opposite state: closed during early touches and open during slips. Forcing the gripper closed during early touches does not lower detection; forced-open slips (24 poses, as opening the gripper at a held grasp usually releases the object, which leaves few true slips) still score contact higher at most poses (75%), but they are too few to compare with natural slips.
C.7 Phase
Labels. Each step is labeled from the simulator state: the gripper command, the object’s height, and whether the object rests on the receptacle. REACH lasts until four steps before the first command to close the gripper. GRASP runs from there until transport begins, so failed grasps and retries count as grasp. TRANSPORT begins once the object, held after the final close, has risen 3 mm. PLACE begins where the object starts its last uninterrupted descent (at least 1 cm) before being released onto the receptacle, or at the release if there is no such descent, and runs to the end of the episode.
Decoding. We fit a four-class linear discriminant (LDA) on the successful episodes, with houses held out. Balanced accuracy (the mean of the four per-phase accuracies; chance 0.25) is 0.92 for the action expert at layer 24, 0.86 for the VLM’s image tokens of the two current camera frames at layer 31, and 0.87 for the VLM’s non-image tokens at layer 29. The VLM’s 3-D view (Figure 17) shows the top three LDA directions rather than principal components, because the VLM’s first principal component (78% of variance) tracks the scene rather than the phase; the action expert’s view in Figure 5b uses principal components.
Freezing. The policy sees two frames per query, taken eight steps apart. At landmark moments in each phase (for place, the last query before the release), taken from re-simulations of successful episodes, we query the policy twice at the same moment: once with its normal two frames (natural), and once with the current frame shown twice, so that the world appears to have stopped (frozen; “2-query pause” in Figure 19). Re-simulations that end in failure are kept. The decoded phase of both queries (Figures 5c, 17, and 18) comes from the LDA of the previous paragraph, using the fold that held out each landmark’s house (797 landmarks from 122 episodes). The
39

gripper effect comes from all episodes of the same capture (819 landmarks from 126 episodes): the fraction of the eight executed steps that command a closed gripper, frozen minus natural (Figure 5d; per step in Figure 19).

C.8 Wrist Orientation
Roll sweep (Figures 1 and 6). Starting from one arm pose, we set the wrist joint (Franka joint 7) to 38 evenly spaced angles across its range plus its starting angle, keep all other joints fixed, and re-render both cameras at each angle. We record the action expert at layer 9, using the first action token at the final flow step, and plot the top two principal components of the 39 activations. For Figure 1, we run the same sweep in 64 houses, subtract each house’s mean activation, and fit one PCA to all of them.

Readout and precession (Figures 24 and 23). The natural-rollout readout uses the first action token at the final flow step. In the precession sweep, each joint is kept within the range of the policy’s training actions (1st to 99th percentile) and the hand within 5 cm of its starting position; in each direction, the orbit stops when the hand can no longer be held there.

Steering (Figure 6b–d). The autoencoder is a pair of small MLPs (768–512–256–32 and back), trained on layer-9 activations averaged over the action tokens, from controlled wrist-rotation sweeps with a third of the houses held out. Three pairs of its 32 code dimensions are each constrained to a circle, one per rotation axis, and the other 26 are unconstrained; we rotate only the pair for rotation about the vertical axis, where one degree on the circle corresponds to one degree of physical rotation on held-out sweeps. To rotate by θ, we encode the current activation, rotate its point on that circle by θ, and decode. The edit is the decoded rotated point minus the decoded unrotated one, so a zero-degree rotation gives no edit; it is added to all action tokens at layer 9 at the final flow step and is 19% ± 7% of the activation’s norm. One-shot edits of ±20◦ and ±40◦ are applied at a single query in the middle of a rollout, in eight scenes held out from training the autoencoder, and the response is read from the decoded 8-step action chunk; one scene in which the wrist rotates strongly on its own is excluded, leaving 28 conditions. Held edits are recomputed from the live activation at every query over 32-step rollouts in six held-out scenes (five of these seven plus the excluded one), and the rotation is measured relative to the unedited rollout of the same scene.

C.9 Gripper Pullback
Landmarks. In 304 grasping episodes (170 houses), we take two landmarks relative to the moment the grasp becomes stable (contact held for three consecutive queries, with a lift of at least 2 cm when one occurs): a few queries before it (typically 3–9, gripper open) and a few after (gripper closed and holding the object). At each landmark we pick up to three evenly spaced queries whose executed gripper command is opposite the target, giving 1,666 edits. For 304 of them the unedited chunk already has the target’s sign; flip rates exclude these.

C.10 Real-Robot Analyses
Data. We use DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024), giving each model one frame per camera at each query (no earlier frame). The first 100 episodes, queried every eighth step (3,321 queries), are used for Figures 8, 29, and 33; the wrist traces in the first two instead show the roll-sweep episode described below. A further 2,000 pick-and-place episodes, drawn at random from those with a single grasp-and-release cycle and at least six recorded queries after the release, are used for Figures 31 and 32. Action-expert activations are averaged over the action tokens at the final flow step. VLM activations are averaged over the image tokens, with each token first scaled to unit length for MolmoAct2 and MolmoBot; the MolmoAct2 distance rulers instead average the non-image tokens. Reported scores are 5-fold cross-validated with episodes held out. Closure onset is the first query at which the measured gripper position leaves zero, ignoring blips that stay below 30% of its holding value.
EE position and wrist orientation. Labels are computed from the recorded joint angles with the robot’s kinematic model; the wrist angle is the gripper’s rotation about the vertical axis (the last angle of its xyz Euler decomposition). Probes read action-expert layer 16 of MolmoAct2 and MolmoBot and VLM layer 7 of π0.5. For the roll sweep (Figure 30), an operator rotated joint 7 from +69◦ to −79◦ and back to +84◦ in one episode while the other joints moved by up to 15◦; Figure 30 colors points by this joint angle. The π0.5 action-expert panel uses one fixed noise seed for all queries, the third of the ten flow steps, and the first action token (with a new seed per query at the final step, no arc is visible).
Distance to grasp. We use the queries from the start of each episode up to closure onset, keeping pick-and-place episodes with at least three such queries, and define d as the gripper’s distance from where it is at closure onset. The rulers are fit as in App. C.4, on VLM layer 25 of MolmoAct2 and MolmoBot and layer 14 of π0.5.
Phase. Labels come from the measured gripper position: reach until closure onset, grasp until the gripper reaches 90% of its holding value, transport while it stays there, and place for the six queries (3.2 s) after that; later queries are not scored. The bars in Figure 32 come from four one-vs-rest linear discriminants, one per phase, each fit on all other episodes.
Gripper pullback. We follow App. C.9, adding δ after the final action-expert block, with 15 action tokens for MolmoAct2 and π0.5 (whose activations are 1,024-dimensional) and a gripper weight of 16 for MolmoAct2 and 4 for π0.5 and MolmoBot. MolmoAct2’s gripper command lies in [0, 1], so its targets are 0 and 1 and a flip means crossing 0.5; π0.5 outputs joint velocities, so its arm target is zero velocity. All models share 379 landmarks: queries 3–9 before closure onset (gripper open) or 0–9 after (closing), at most two of each per episode. Rank-k edits project each δ onto the mean and top k principal components of all 379 edits instead of refitting.
Cross-model similarity. MolmoBot is run on the same DROID queries. For each variable, we project each model’s standardized activations onto that model’s own ridge-probe directions for the variable, out of fold (aperture on the 379 landmark states; (cos θ, sin θ) and (x, y, z) on the 3,321 queries; the two rulers on the reach queries of the first 100 episodes). Similarity is one minus the Procrustes disparity between two models’ coordinates on identical rows, after centering, scaling to unit norm, and the best rotation, reflection, and uniform scaling; the null repeats this with the target shuffled across rows. Every coordinate is fit to the same labels in each model, so part of the agreement is built in.
