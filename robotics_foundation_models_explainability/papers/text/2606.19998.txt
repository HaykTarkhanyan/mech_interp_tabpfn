Tri-Info: Generalizable, Interpretable Failure Prediction for VLA Models via Information Theory
Jinghan Yang ∗ Yunchao Zhang Wang Yuan Haolun Wang Jiaming Zhang Zhengyang Hu Yanchao Yang
InfoBodied AI Lab, The University of Hong Kong
Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are increasingly deployed across diverse tasks, yet they remain black boxes whose physical interactions can cause irreversible harm, making generalizable and interpretable failure detection essential. We observe that successful and failed rollouts carry systematically different information-theoretic signatures. Building on this, we formalize VLA control as a closed-loop information pipeline and derive the Triple Information-theoretic (TriInfo) signals that capture whether actions remain diverse, temporally consistent, and coupled to state transitions. Across six VLA models and three benchmark environments, Tri-Info matches the strongest baselines in-domain. Moreover, TriInfo transfers across architectures, environments, and the sim-to-real gap without retraining with labeled data, reaching 70% accuracy on real-world tasks. This establishes Tri-Info as a simple yet powerful method that not only detects failures with strong cross-domain generalization, but also delivers interpretable diagnostics of the underlying failure modes.
1 Introduction
Building on large-scale vision-language pretraining [Radford et al., 2021, 2019, Brown et al., 2020], vision-language-action (VLA) models map multimodal inputs directly to robot actions via behavioral cloning [Brohan et al., 2022, 2023, Kim et al., 2024, O’Neill et al., 2024]. Yet they fail silently under distributional shift, such as unfamiliar objects [Zhu et al., 2025], lighting [Jin et al., 2025, Xie et al., 2024], or tasks [Zhou et al., 2025]. Unlike software errors, such failures are often irreversible: a misguided reach can injure a person or shatter a fragile object. Safe deployment therefore requires predicting failures before they unfold [Brunke et al., 2022, Xing et al., 2025]. Existing detectors either train classifiers on a policy’s internal representations [Gu et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2025, Römer et al., 2025], achieving strong in-domain accuracy, or monitor the temporal consistency of sampled actions [Agia et al., 2024]. The former are tied to one architecture’s feature space and must be retrained for each new policy; both compress a rollout into a single score that signals whether it fails but not how. Both also inspect the policy in isolation, whereas failure arises in the closed loop between the policy and its environment.
We study failure from the closed-loop perspective, drawing on information theory: effective information transfer is a prerequisite for achieving a goal [Shannon and Weaver, 1949]. A closed-loop agent, animal or machine, can succeed only if its perception and action remain informationally coupled with its environment: observations must reduce uncertainty about what to do, and actions must in turn shape what is observed next [Wiener, 1948, Tishby and Polani, 2010]. loop. A successful rollout places three requirements on this flow. First, the agent must retain enough variety in its actions to counter variation in its environment [Ashby, 1956]. Second, its actions must remain coherent over time, since a physical skill unfolds as a sequence of related motions rather than a series of independent decisions [Zhao et al., 2023, Chi et al., 2025]. Third, its actions must stay grounded in what it observes, since a controller that ignores its observations can no longer correct its course [Touchette and Lloyd, 2000]. Failure breaks one of these requirements: action entropy collapses below or surges beyond what the task demands, consecutive actions lose coherence, or actions stop responding to observations. We therefore hypothesize: Success and failure rollouts differ in how these information-theoretic quantities evolve over time.
To test this hypothesis, we formalize VLA-controlled systems as closed-loop information pipelines and derive eight metrics in four diagnostic categories: marginal statistics (H(St), H(At)), policy coupling (I(St; At)), dynamics (I(At; St+1), I(At, St; St+1), I(St, St+1; At)), and temporal coherence (I(St; St+1), I(At; At+1)), each estimated over a sliding window of the rollout. Removing redundancy and favoring action-centric metrics yields three complementary signals, the Triple Information-theoretic (Tri-Info) metrics {H(At), I(At; At+1), I(St, St+1; At)}. They describe action from three angles: how spread the policy’s actions are, how much of that uncertainty is explained by its own recent actions, and how much by what it observes. A healthy policy thus acts with moderate entropy that is grounded in both its history and its observations. On top of them, we build a Tri-Info detector that fuses one GRU per signal to model how these signals evolve over a rollout.
Our experiments support the hypothesis and reveal two further properties. First, the metrics are interpretable: different failure modes shift different metrics (Figure 1). In a freeze, the robot stalls and H(At) collapses. In a drift, the robot abandons its goal and moves erratically, and H(At) surges instead. In a phantom grasp, the robot acts as if holding an object it never grasped, and I(St, St+1; At) drops. The pattern of shifts thus indicates not only that a rollout is failing but also how, and the detector’s alarms align with visually identifiable dangerous events (Figure 2). Second, the metrics are generalizable: Tri-Info matches the strongest baselines in domain and retains its accuracy across VLA architectures, task environments, and the sim-to-real gap, without labeled failure data from the target domain. We attribute this to the metrics summarizing statistical dependencies among states and actions. We evaluate Tri-Info on various VLA models, simulated benchmarks, and real-world robot tasks, and the summarized contributions are:
1. Information-theoretic framework. We formalize VLA control as a closed-loop information pipeline and propose three complementary Tri-Info signals for failure prediction.

2 Related Work
Information-Theoretic Analysis in Robotic Control. Information theory has long informed robotic control. Empowerment, the channel capacity between actions and future states [Klyubin et al., 2005], quantifies an agent’s ability to influence its environment; variational estimators made it tractable [Mohamed and Jimenez Rezende, 2015], enabling applications in exploration [Houthooft et al., 2016] and unsupervised skill discovery [Eysenbach et al., 2018, Sharma et al., 2019]. The information bottleneck [Tishby et al., 2000] has been used to identify decision states for structured exploration and transfer [Goyal et al., 2019].
These ideas have recently entered robotic learning. Hejna et al. [2025] filters low-quality training trajectories using state–action mutual information, Bai et al. [2025] applies the information bottleneck to behavioral cloning, and the multimodal information bottleneck [You and Liu, 2024] preserves taskrelevant information across sensor modalities. Li et al. [2025] uses entropy and mutual information to identify kinematically active scene elements for dual-arm policy transfer, and contact-aware Fisher information maximization [Sathyanarayan and Abraham, 2025] synthesizes information-rich contact behaviors. These works apply information measures to training and representation learning; in contrast, we provide the first taxonomy of information-theoretic metrics for VLA failure prediction at deployment, organized around the perception–action loop and spanning marginal statistics, policy coupling, dynamics, and temporal coherence. This grounds detection in interpretable signals that remain comparable across architectures and yields mechanistic insight unavailable through black-box methods.
Predictive Failure Detection for Robotic Systems. Work on safe reinforcement learning targets training-time safety through constrained optimization [Achiam et al., 2017], human intervention [Saunders et al., 2018], or learned recovery [Thananjeyan et al., 2021]. For VLA models trained by behavioral cloning, the challenge shifts to deployment-time monitoring [Brunke et al., 2022, Gu et al., 2024], where recent detectors divide by classifier input: embedding-based methods train on internal VLA representations, while score-based methods compute a scalar signal and detect on top of it. Among embedding-based methods, SAFE [Gu et al., 2025] trains classifiers on VLA latents with functional conformal calibration, generalizing across tasks but not architectures; Fail-Detect [Xu et al., 2025] casts detection as sequential out-of-distribution (OOD) detection without requiring failure data; and FIPER [Römer et al., 2025] fuses perception-level OOD via random network distillation with action-chunk entropy. All three attain strong in-domain accuracy but are tied to a specific architecture and cannot transfer without retraining. The score-based STAC [Agia et al., 2024] instead measures temporal consistency via the maximum mean discrepancy between overlapping segments of sampled action chunks. It is the closest in spirit to our I(At; At+1), but a distance captures

Our metrics combine the cross-architecture comparability that embedding-based methods lack, with the diagnostic decomposition that a single consistency score cannot provide.

3.2 Information dynamics in the perception–action loop

Whether a robot completes its task depends on how it processes information at every step. We characterize this with an information profile: the entropy of each variable in the perception–action loop over (St, At, St+1, At+1) and the mutual information among them, i.e., how uncertain each variable is and how strongly the variables depend on one another (Figure 3). In a successful rollout, actions vary to a normal degree, stay consistent across steps, and respond to observations. Failure breaks one of these properties: action entropy collapses or surges, consecutive actions lose consistency, or actions stop responding to observations. Our central hypothesis is therefore that success and failure rollouts differ in how these information-theoretic quantities evolve over time. We thus estimate the information over a sliding time-step window and track how it changes during execution.

5 Results
5.1 Predictive Power of Information-Theoretic Metrics
Every metric carries signal; temporal modeling matters. We feed each of the eight metrics as the sole input to two detectors: a memoryless logistic regression (LogReg) that scores it instantaneously, and a GRU that models its temporal evolution (Table 1). Under LogReg, all eight reach ≥ 0.65 average in-domain mBA, with the strongest, H(At), at 0.75, confirming that the predictive signal resides in the metrics themselves. Modeling temporal context with a GRU lifts all metrics to 0.72– 0.77, and the weakest metrics gain the most, so the gap between the best and worst metric closes from 0.10 to 0.05. In conclusion, the metrics are individually informative, and modeling their temporal evolution improves detection performance.

5.3 Ablation Study
Metric ablation: Tri-Info’s three signals contribute to OOD performance. We compare the three-metric detector against all of its 2- and 1-metric subsets and the full 8-metric fusion (Table 2). The three-metric set improves over every smaller subset on both ID (0.88) and OOD (0.87), while expanding to eight metrics holds ID flat (0.87) and substantially degrades OOD to 0.74 – the additional five signals overfit in-distribution patterns and fail to transfer.
Input ablation: how much of the signal needs model internals? Everything so far is estimated on VAE latents of the policy’s internal embeddings, which requires white-box access. We re-ran the pipeline changing only the estimator inputs, replacing at by the executed action at and st by the raw proprioception qt, both visible from outside the policy and low-dimensional enough to enter the estimators without a VAE, unlike the raw observation st. In domain, the internal representations make no difference to detection accuracy: the four choices lie between 0.876 and 0.897, and the highest is at + st. Under shift they do: the OOD mean falls from 0.865 with the embeddings to 0.794 for the black-box at + qt. Tri-Info therefore runs in a fully black-box setting, and the white-box requirement earns its cost only under domain shift.

6 Conclusion
We formalized VLA control as a closed-loop information pipeline and reduced eight derived metrics to three complementary Tri-Info metrics {H(At), I(At; At+1), I(St, St+1; At)} that diagnose action collapse, temporal incoherence, and action–state decoupling. Paired with a per-signal GRU detector, Tri-Info stays interpretable and generalizable, transferring across VLA models, benchmarks, and the sim-to-real gap to reach 70%+ on real-world tasks.

A.2 Training and Evaluation Protocol
This section describes how data are split, how the detectors are trained, and where each component of the pipeline is fitted.
Trajectory-level splits. Every split is made over whole trajectories, before any sliding window is formed, and is stratified by the outcome label f ξ. A window Wt therefore never spans two trajectories, and no trajectory contributes windows to more than one split, so near-duplicate windows cannot straddle the split boundary. In-domain experiments split each dataset 70/30 into a training and a test split; transfer experiments fit everything on the source domain and evaluate on target-domain trajectories the detector has never seen. The calibration set Dc+al is the success subset of the source training split, so calibration consumes no target trajectory. Because those successes also train the GRUs, θ(t) is calibrated in-sample, and we therefore read α as an operating-point knob (Section 5.3) rather than an exact finite-sample coverage guarantee.
Failure detector training. We train classifiers at the timestep level, where each timestep inherits the trajectory-level label f ξ ∈ {0, 1}. The GRU model consists of a single-layer GRU mapping the per-timestep metric scalar (input dimension one) to a hidden state, followed by a two-layer MLP head that halves the hidden size before a single output, with ReLU activation, dropout 0.1, and a sigmoid output. It is trained with Adam (learning rate 10−3, weight decay 10−5); the hidden size and the number of epochs are not fixed but selected per domain by the search of Appendix A.4 (Table 12). The logistic regression baseline uses a single linear layer with sigmoid activation, trained with the L-BFGS solver at an inverse regularization strength of 1.0. Both models receive the per-timestep metrics, normalized using global statistics computed across the entire training set. Unless otherwise noted, every experiment is run with three random seeds, and we report the average across seeds.
VAE training data and leakage. The projection of Appendix D is fitted once per model–dataset domain, on all of that domain’s per-step embeddings – test trajectories included – using reconstruction plus a weak KL penalty. A target domain therefore contributes only unlabelled embeddings to its own projection, and no failure label enters the VAE training pipeline anywhere. The projection does stay transductive in the unlabelled data; fitting it on a disjoint unlabelled pool would remove even that.
Where each component is fitted. Table 5 summarizes where each component is fitted. Only the label-free front end is refitted for a new model or embodiment; the GRU weights, the normalization constants (µ, σ), and the threshold θ(t) are fitted once on the source training split and applied unchanged, with target sequences z-normalized by source statistics rather than their own.

classifier: embedding-based methods that use model representations, and score-based methods that operate on computed scalar signals. This distinction is important, as embedding-based approaches are inherently tied to specific model architectures and cannot generalize across different policies without retraining.
Embedding-based methods. SAFE [Gu et al., 2025] trains a classifier on VLA latent representations to distinguish successful from failed executions, so both its input dimensionality and its decision geometry are fixed to that of embedding space, and it is bound to the architecture by construction. Fail-Detect [Xu et al., 2025] distills the policy’s inputs and outputs into a scalar signal, which makes it look model-agnostic. But its strongest score (logpZO) is a density estimator trained on one policy’s observation embeddings. On a different model or task, the embeddings no longer match what it learned, so almost every trajectory looks out-of-distribution; the score saturates and can no longer tell success from failure. FIPER [Römer et al., 2025] combines two signals: (i) out-of-distribution observations via Random Network Distillation in the policy’s embedding space (RND-OE), and (ii) action-chunk entropy (ACE) over sampled actions. The RND-OE branch detects novelty using a predictor network trained on one policy’s embeddings, so it is tied to that embedding space and fails once the model or task changes, for the same reason as Fail-Detect. The ACE branch, however, reads only the sampled action chunks and trains no model bound to the source policy, so it does carry over. We therefore take ACE as FIPER’s transferable component and compare against it in Figure 12.
Score-based methods. STAC [Agia et al., 2024] computes the statistical divergence between overlapping segments of sampled action chunks to measure temporal consistency in generative policies. Dimension-matched embedding baselines with VAE compression. The embedding-based detectors cannot be applied when the target policy’s latent dimensionality differs from the source, which is the case for PI0.5-LIBERO → ACT-ALOHA-real-cup. To evaluate them in that setting as well, we project the source and target embeddings through the per-domain VAE to the common dz = 7 latent and train and apply each detector on those latents, giving the variants SAFE-VAE, Fail-Detect-VAE, and FIPER-VAE. Table 6 reports trajectory-level AUC and mean balanced accuracy for all methods, and Figure 11 shows balanced accuracy versus trajectory progress for the dimension-matched variants.
In domain, the projection lowers the average accuracy of all three embedding-based detectors, as expected from compressing a high-dimensional latent to seven dimensions: SAFE drops from 0.91 to 0.85 AUC and from 0.82 to 0.79 mBA, while Fail-Detect and FIPER lose at most 0.02 AUC and 0.06 mBA. Under shift, the shared latent makes cross-domain evaluation possible: all three detectors now apply to PI0.5-LIBERO → ACT-ALOHA-real-cup, where the raw variants cannot run. The shared latent also improves transfer for some detectors. SAFE-VAE outperforms raw SAFE on both transfers where the two are comparable, raising AUC from 0.61 to 0.86 on PI0.5-LIBERO → PI0.5-ALOHA-real-block and from 0.64 to 0.74 on PI0-LIBERO → PI0.5-LIBERO. On the latter transfer it also raises mBA from 0.50 to 0.81. FIPER-VAE likewise raises AUC on PI0-LIBERO → PI0.5-LIBERO from 0.56 to 0.77. The gain is not uniform, however: Fail-Detect-VAE falls below chance on all three transfers (0.32 average AUC). Nor does it extend to deployable accuracy. Fail-Detect-VAE and FIPER-VAE sit at 0.50 mBA on every OOD setting, and SAFE-VAE averages 0.64. The cases where AUC stays high but mBA collapses identify the failure mode. These cases are SAFE-VAE on the two transfers to real ALOHA (0.86 vs. 0.53 and 0.79 vs. 0.58) and FIPER-VAE on PI0-LIBERO → PI0.5-LIBERO (0.77 vs. 0.50). There the scores still rank trajectories within the target domain, but their absolute level shifts. The threshold θ(t) calibrated on the source domain therefore no longer separates success from failure, and the deployed detector operates at or near chance.

The VAE projection accounts for a substantial share of whatever transfer an action statistic achieves, which is why we report both variants rather than attributing transfer to the metric alone. The projection is nevertheless not sufficient: Once the latent is matched, entropy and mutual information remain comparable across domains because they describe the distribution of actions (how probability mass is spread and how strongly actions depend on their history and states).

B Limitations.
Firstly, the scope of this paper is mainly alarms, rather than recovery or improvement of the success rate. We do try using the detector to select among sampled actions at inference time, which does raise the success rate (Appendix ??), though closing the loop deserves a more systematic study than we give it here. Secondly, this work is also tested only on VLAs; we additionally try a non-VLA behavioral-cloning policy (Appendix ??), which suggests the method may apply to closed-loop policies in general, but confirming this requires a wider range of architectures. Finally, our approach relies on a VAE to compress high-dimensional embeddings into a low-dimensional latent space where entropy and mutual information can be estimated. Therefore, our method requires training a VAE to compress the embeddings. Future work could remove this compression step by adopting information estimators that scale to high-dimensional spaces.

D MI Estimation
We estimate the three Tri-Info quantities H(At), I(At; At+1), and I(St, St+1; At) nonparametrically over a sliding window of length W , using the Kozachenko–Leonenko estimator [Kozachenko, 1987] for the entropy and the Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator [Kraskov et al., 2004] for the mutual-information terms, both under the max-norm with k = 5 neighbours and N = W − 1 samples per window.
VAE projection. Raw embeddings are too high-dimensional for k-NN estimation, where neighbour distances concentrate and the estimator bias grows. Following Hejna et al. [2025], who estimate state– action mutual information with the same estimator family, we compress each embedding to a matched dz=7 latent with a VAE fitted per model–dataset domain (Table 14; Appendix A.2) and estimate on the posterior means. A VAE rather than an arbitrary projection is what makes the distances usable: its isotropic Gaussian prior gives the latent a well-conditioned geometry, a prerequisite for k-NN statistics and the regime in which these estimators are best characterized. Keeping dz small serves the estimator, as they argue, and matching it across the two encoders keeps the joint dimensions of the three metrics comparable; their ablation over a threefold range of latent sizes reports little effect, and we did not repeat that sweep here.
Assumptions and what the estimates support. Our windowed use stresses the estimators more than the pooled use they were validated under: Hejna et al. [2025] draw batches of 1024 samples from a whole dataset, whereas a window affords 29 consecutive – hence autocorrelated – steps at the default W = 30, and they take the L2 norm within each latent block and the max-norm between blocks, where we use the max-norm in every subspace. Local uniformity inside the k-NN ball is correspondingly loose, across up to 21 joint dimensions for I(St, St+1; At), and the entropy estimator diverges as neighbour distances vanish, so we floor them at 10−15: a degenerate window yields a finite floor for H(At) rather than a divergence. Absolute values in nats are therefore not meaningful and we never use them as such; as in Hejna et al. [2025], only relative comparisons carry weight. We accordingly read each metric as a normalized time series whose shape rather than level drives the GRU, and let the detector integrate the sequence of per-window estimates rather than trust any one of them. This is also why W is the one consequential hyperparameter below – it sets both the sample count and the span over which the regime must be stationary – while detection is insensitive to k, matching the robustness Hejna et al. [2025] report over k ∈ {2, . . . , 10}.
