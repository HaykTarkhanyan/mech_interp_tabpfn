Observing and Controlling Features in Vision-Language-Action Models
Hugo Buurmeijer1, Carmen Amo Alonso1, Aiden Swann2, and Marco Pavone1,3

arXiv:2603.05487v2 [cs.RO] 21 Sep 2026

Abstract— Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) have shown remarkable progress towards embodied intelligence. While their architecture partially resembles that of Large Language Models (LLMs), VLAs exhibit higher complexity due to their multi-modal inputs/outputs and often hybrid nature of transformer and diffusion heads. This is part of the reason why insights from mechanistic interpretability in LLMs, which explain how the internal model representations relate to their output behavior, do not trivially transfer to VLA counterparts. In this work, we investigate whether VLA internal representations support lightweight behavioral steering without retraining. Across four frontier VLA models, linear observers recover stateand action-relevant information in both autoregressive and transformer–flow-matching architectures, and provide robust directions to causally alter VLA outputs. Building on this, we introduce a controller that minimally modifies representations to place observer predictions within prescribed target intervals. Closed-loop experiments with π0.5 in the LIBERO simulator and on DROID hardware demonstrate improved constraint satisfaction while retaining task performance, with only approximately 1% inference overhead. Together, these experiments show that lightweight linear interventions can reliably steer VLA behavior while preserving closed-loop capabilities, enabling alignment with user preferences and task requirements without fine-tuning.
I. INTRODUCTION Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) represent a significant step towards embodied intelligence. By processing multimodal inputs consisting of images, language instructions, and proprioceptive states, VLAs combine perception, reasoning, and action generation in a single model. Similar to other disciplines where generative models have unlocked unprecedented generalization, VLAs enable robots to interpret natural language commands in rich visual contexts and execute complex behaviors, exhibiting improved generalization across tasks and environments [1]. However, VLAs suffer from similar limitations to other generative models: their behavior can be unpredictable, difficult to correct in real time, or misaligned with user preferences and safety requirements [2]. One promising approach to overcoming these limitations is the ability to steer generative models toward desired behaviors while suppressing undesirable ones. In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), intervening internal model representations to influence outputs has become a prominent research direction, referred to as “activation steering” [3]. Achieving a similar level of control is arguably even more critical for VLAs, where steering directly affects physical behavior in the real world. Furthermore, since VLAs often share architectural components with LLMs,

(most notably transformer-based representations [4]), methods developed for LLMs could in theory transfer to VLAs without significant overhead. However, VLAs operate over multiple modalities, including proprioceptive inputs, and must function in closed-loop interaction with the physical world where compounding errors are notorious, thereby posing challenges absent in LLMs. Additionally, robotic tasks such as manipulation naturally demand smooth, continuous actions, which has driven frontier architectures toward hybrid transformer-diffusion designs [5], further complicating the transfer of interpretability insights from LLMs to VLAs.
To close this gap, several works have explored porting concepts from mechanistic interpretability in LLMs over to the domain of VLAs [6], [7], [8], [9]. For instance, [6] overwrites VLA model activations at inference time to modulate basic motion features such as speed and direction. Overall, the effectiveness of existing methods is still limited. Crucially, while the focus of activation steering methods in LLMs is to control the behavior of the model while preserving its original merits, such as natural-sounding generations, in the field of VLAs interventions that preserve natural behaviors have not been studied. Moreover, the effect of architectural design choices, such as purely transformer-based versus hybrid transformer-diffusion architectures, on the interpretability and steerability of VLA representations remains unexplored.
In this paper, we address these challenges by formalizing concepts that are often implicit in work on steering LLMs, with the goal of making them applicable to VLAs. We introduce a unified framework for analyzing and influencing VLA behavior through their internal representations (see Figure 1), and use them to systematically compare common VLA architectures. We find linearly predictive state and action information in both autoregressive and transformer– flow-matching architectures, with performance varying across models, datasets, and features. Building on these findings, we develop lightweight observers and representation controllers, and demonstrate behavioral steering while retaining task performance in closed-loop simulation and hardware experiments.

II. RELATED WORK A. Mechanistic Interpretability in VLAs
The intersection of mechanistic interpretability and robotics, specifically VLAs, remains significantly underexplored, though a number of recent works have investigated these tools in the context of end-to-end robot control. Notably, steering vectors can be extracted from the feed-forward layers of the Transformer backbone to causally affect model behaviors such as speed and height [6]. More abstract concepts, such as spatial relationships between objects and movement targets, were extracted from OpenVLA representations in [8] using linear probes, while [10] probe for recoverable statetransition structure. Such tools have also been used to improve downstream performance, e.g. by mechanistically identifying and fine-tuning task-relevant attention heads [7].
A complementary line of work forgoes pre-specified concepts in favor of unsupervised feature discovery: concurrent work of [9] trains Sparse Autoencoders on π0.5 residual streams, proposes activation statistics to classify generality of features, and validates them via additive and ablative steering. Our contributions are complementary: because we target features specified a priori, observability and controllability are measured against a known ground truth rather than against post-hoc inspection of top-activating episodes.
B. Activation Steering in LLMs A growing body of work has shown that semantic and
behavioral features in LLMs are often linearly represented in their activation spaces, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the linear separability hypothesis [11]. It has been widely

observed that simple linear classifiers can reliably extract attributes such as sentiment, topic, or personality traits from intermediate transformer layers [12], [13], [14], suggesting that these representations encode disentangled, behaviorally meaningful features. Building on this insight, activation steering methods modify internal activations at inference time to influence model outputs without fine-tuning [15], [16]. Techniques such as activation addition [3], [17] have been applied to guide generation toward desired attributes, mitigate harmful behaviors, and improve safety and alignment. Crucially, these methods emphasize preserving the naturalness and coherence of generated text by applying low-rank or minimal perturbations to the model’s internal states.

We then introduce two concepts that enable the formalization of the problem in mathematical terms.

B. Feature Observability and Controllability
A feature is a measurable attribute of the model’s output or the robot’s behavior during execution, such as an action displacement, grasp modality, or the identity of a manipulated object. Formally, a feature is a map ξ : T → Rn assigning to each trajectory a value ξ(τ ); this may be a direct readout of the trajectory, but can also be a label provided by a human annotator inspecting the rollout.
In this paper, we explore how the structure of the representation space in VLAs can be leveraged to (i) observe features, and (ii) steer features into a desired region by controlling their representations. To that end, we introduce the concepts of feature-observability and feature-controllability. Both are properties of a feature relative to a collection of inputs to the VLA: the requirement is that a single map works for every input in that collection, so the property is a statement about the representation space at a layer rather than about an individual rollout.
Definition III.1 (Feature-Observability). A feature ξ is observable at layer ℓ if there exists a single map fℓ ∈ F such that fℓ(xℓ) = ξ(τ ) for every input. The map fℓ is referred to as the observer.
Definition III.2 (Feature-Controllability). Given a target set C ⊆ Rn, a feature ξ is controllable to C at layer ℓ if there exists a single map gℓ ∈ G such that, for every input, setting Xℓ = gℓ(Tℓ(Xℓ−1)) yields a trajectory τ˜ with ξ(τ˜) ∈ C. The map gℓ is referred to as the controller.
Remark III.3. The definitions above admit a broad range of features. One can distinguish (a) output-level features, assessed at a specific point in the trajectory, ξ(τt); and (b) trajectory-level features, assessed on the full trajectory, ξ(τ ). Consequently a feature need not be observable or controllable at every time step: an eventual grasp modality may not yet be distinguishable early in execution, while a choice already realized cannot be undone by later interventions.
In the general case, the set of observers and controllers considered are F ⊆ {f : Rd → Rn} and G ⊆ {g : RN×d → RN×d}. In practice the observer and controller are usually learned rather than prescribed, so F and G are restricted to a specific structure (linear maps, one-layer MLPs, etc.) and fitted on a collection of inputs drawn from rollouts together with their representations and feature values.
We state both notions exactly, as the idealized versions make the structure clearest. Empirically neither holds exactly: we report the accuracy with which a fitted observer recovers ξ(τ ), and the rate at which a controller lands in C, and read these as graded evidence for the corresponding property.
Given the definitions above, the open question is which layers admit observers and controllers for features of interest, and how small the classes F and G can be while still doing so. In what follows we propose that linear choices suffice: linear observers recover features of interest from xℓ, and linear, additive controllers steer the resulting behavior toward specified targets C during execution.

V. EXPERIMENTS In this section, we present the experiments that explore the concepts of feature-observability and feature-controllability in frontier VLA policies: the transformer-based OpenVLA and π0-FAST and the transformer-flow-matching hybrid models π0 and π0.5. We first compare how accurately robot states and actions can be observed in representation space using a linear observer across these architectures. Then, we show that targeted perturbations in the representation space smoothly affect VLA outputs and that precise representation control is achieved. Lastly, we study how minimal control interventions to the representation space lead to effective VLA steering in closed-loop deployment on hardware.
A. Observing Features in VLA Representations We assess whether robot-relevant features can be predicted
using the linear observer defined in Equation (3), and how prediction performance varies across models and datasets. We evaluate π0, π0-FAST, and π0.5 on LIBERO [23] and DROID [24], and OpenVLA [18] on BridgeData V2 [25]. We focus on robot states and actions, which are available in these datasets, and fundamental robot quantities. Following Remark III.3, these are output-level features, while trajectorylevel features are evaluated in Section V-C.
The state features are s = (x, y, z, ϕ, θ, ψ, g), comprising Cartesian end-effector position, roll, pitch, yaw, and normalized gripper aperture. Action features comprise translational and rotational commands (∆x, ∆y, ∆z, ∆ϕ, ∆θ, ∆ψ) and the gripper command ∆g, following each dataset’s conventions. For chunked actions, labels are averaged over the chunk; OpenVLA uses single-step action labels. Continuous quantities are predicted using regression probes, while gripper state and command are predicted using binary classifiers.
For each model–dataset pair, we construct input–feature pairs {(w(i), ξ(i))}iK=1, where, for example, ξ = ∆x ∈ R. Inputs contain scene images, task instructions, and proprioceptive measurements as supported by each model; OpenVLA receives a single image and no proprioceptive measurements. B. Controlling Features Through Representation Interventions Feature predictability alone does not establish that a repre-
sentation can be used to steer model behavior. We therefore examine two requirements for representation control: whether observer-aligned perturbations produce graded changes in the generated actions, and whether the controller can place individual observer predictions within a prescribed target.
Output sensitivity to representation interventions. We compare the LIBERO checkpoints of π0 and π0-FAST to examine flow-matching and autoregressive action generation within the same model family. For each scalar action feature, we perturb the representation along its linear observer direction, xℓ ← xℓ + αWℓ, where α controls intervention strength. Only one layer is perturbed per generation. We sweep the layer and intervention strength, measuring the mean change in the corresponding output feature relative to the unmodified model across 50 LIBERO episodes.
Across the six continuous action dimensions, increasing α produces graded, generally ordered changes in the mean output over the tested intervention strengths. Figure 4 illustrates this behavior for the vertical displacement command ∆z. The trend appears in both models, indicating that observer-aligned interventions can modulate continuous actions under both generation mechanisms. Similar continuous-action responses are observed for the LIBERO and DROID checkpoints of π0.5, which we use in subsequent closed-loop experiments. The binary gripper response is less regular, while still showing an ordering with increasing α. Lower sensitivity in later layers may partly be attributed to increasing representation scales, which can reduce the relative effect of a fixed perturbation.
Control of observer predictions. We next evaluate the minimal controller in Equation (7) in the output space of the scalar affine observer from Equation (3). A fixed additive perturbation shifts all observer predictions by the same amount, preserving their spread. Consequently, it can move the mean prediction into the target interval while leaving individual samples outside, as Figure 5 illustrates. The proposed controller instead adapts the intervention to each representation, satisfying fℓ(xℓ) ∈ [ξmin, ξmax] by construction of the constraint in Equation (6). Figure 5 demonstrates this empirically. This guarantee concerns the observer output; behavioral constraint satisfaction during closed-loop execution is evaluated in the next section.

C. Observing and Controlling Features in Closed-Loop
The preceding experiments examined state and action observability and the effects of representation interventions on model outputs. We now evaluate whether these interventions steer executed behavior during closed-loop interaction, including grasp modalities and object choices, while preserving task performance. We use frozen π0.5 checkpoints [1] in LIBERO simulation [23] and on the DROID hardware platform [24], with policy inference on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.
Evaluation and baselines. We consider transport-lift constraints, preferred grasp modalities, and task-specific object-selection constraints. The three simulation experiments comprise 100 rollouts each, while each of the three hardware tasks uses 50 rollouts per method. Constraint satisfaction is the fraction of rollouts for which ξ(τ ) ∈ C; for categorical features, this is the frequency of the preferred grasp or designated safe object. Task success is evaluated separately.
The main baselines are the unmodified policy, prompt interventions, and value-vector steering [6]. For lift constraints, we additionally evaluate two output-side baselines: rejection sampling and action clipping. Rejection sampling draws up to K action chunks at each replan, selecting a satisfying candidate when available and otherwise executing the leastviolating candidate. Action clipping directly modifies the commanded motion, using a safety margin to account for discrepancies between commanded and executed trajectories. The 50 base policy rollouts are re-used to fit the observers.
Constraining transport motion. We impose either an upper bound of 0.10 m or a lower bound of 0.20 m on the vertical lift during the task’s transport phase. Constraints apply over this phase, and task success requires completion of the original LIBERO task. Figure 6 shows that representation control substantially increases constraint satisfaction while retaining high task success. Prompting, on the other hand, fails to meaningfully alter the policy’s behavior. Increasing the rejection-sampling budget K = 5 to K = 100 leaves satisfaction below 10% for both bounds, while value vectors achieve approximately 74% for the upper bound and 0% for the lower bound. As expected for these geometric constraints, action clipping performs well. Representation control makes the preferred modality dominant at 73%.

Prompting and value vectors increase this to 46% and 38%, respectively, while our method reaches 74%. All methods successfully grasp the mug, so the improvement concerns the executed grasp modality rather than grasp success. Figure 7 illustrates the preferred and undesired behaviors. VI. CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK This paper introduced a principled framework for understanding and controlling Vision-Language-Action models through their internal representations. We used this framework to study how architectural design choices affect the interpretability and steerability of VLA internal representations, and leveraged these insights to enable reliable inference-time steering. We found linearly predictive state and action information in both autoregressive and transformer–flow-matching VLAs, with performance varying across models, datasets, and features. Our lightweight observers and controllers enable behavioral steering without retraining, adding approximately 1% inference overhead. Closed-loop experiments in the LIBERO simulator and on DROID hardware demonstrate improved constraint satisfaction while retaining task performance. On hardware, preferred grasps increased by 60 percentage points, and the designated safe objects were selected in all 50 rollouts, beating both prompting and value vector baselines. Limitations and Future Work. While our framework successfully demonstrates feature observability and controllability, several directions warrant further investigation. First, our current approach requires labeled data to train linear observers, which may not always be available in largescale robotics datasets. Second, we focused primarily on the Transformer components of VLA architectures; extending our framework to the diffusion or flow-matching heads would enable end-to-end interpretability and control across hybrid architectures. Finally, establishing principled safety guarantees and bounds on the effects of representation-space interventions would be crucial for deploying these techniques in safety-critical robotic applications.
