OGAM: Connecting Systematic Testing to Runtime Assurance through Object-Grounded Attention Monitoring for VLA Policies
Haki Darwish1, Xiangyu Yin2, Changwen Li3, Rongjie Yan3, Francisco Gomes de Oliveira Neto, Chih-Hong Cheng1,4∗

arXiv:2610.05878v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026

Abstract— Benchmarks expose vision-language-action (VLA) policies to few canonical instructions, while exhaustive deployment testing is impossible. We introduce Object-Grounded Attention Monitoring (OGAM), connecting systematic testing to runtime assurance: testing reveals attention divergence between successful and failed executions, and OGAM uses this signal to stop failures beyond the finite suite. We generate scene-grounded instructions through pairwise combinations of action templates and objects, and separately test meaning-preserving paraphrases. All 87 out-ofbenchmark cases reveal problematic behavior across OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, UniVLA, and π0.5: none completes any of the 24 feasible instructions, while infeasible or hazardous requests also trigger behavior substitution. At each action query, we project gradientweighted visual attention through object masks and group it by instruction role for comparison across tasks and policies. Dynamic time warping aligns this course with a successful reference despite speed differences; conformal calibration on successful episodes sets the early-stopping threshold for sustained deviations, with a nominal false-stop target of α = 0.05. Across four policies, OGAM stops 87–100% of failed episodes at median times of 5–12 s within a 20 s budget, with observed false-stop rates of 3–5%, without failure-labeled training. Finite testing thus identifies attention patterns that support online intervention before failure fully unfolds.

assessing their reliability beyond standard benchmarks remains difficult. Benchmarks contain few canonical instructions close to the policy’s fine-tuning distribution. Deployment instead exposes the robot to a combinatorial space of unusual, infeasible, or hazardous requests. Exhaustive testing is impossible, generated instructions often lack simulator goal predicates, and failures may become obvious only after many incorrect actions.
We address these problems through a coveragedriven testing and runtime monitoring framework. We extract action templates and interaction objects from the scene specification, then use pairwise combinatorial testing [5] to select a compact instruction suite. An LLM labels each instruction-scene pair as feasible, infeasible, or hazardous, followed by blind human review. Meaning-preserving paraphrases separately test linguistic robustness. Although positions and orientations can be added as combinatorial factors, we focus on instruction combinations and paraphrasing. Pairwise coverage makes exploration systematic, but no finite suite can exhaust deployment instructions. We therefore use testing to identify a signal for continued supervision: successful and failed runs often exhibit different attention courses. This divergence motivates ObjectGrounded Attention Monitoring (OGAM), which asks whether a new execution still resembles a successful attention pattern.
To obtain a comparable signal, we construct an object-level attention course at every action query. We extract gradient-weighted attention over image patches to reflect their influence on the predicted action, then project it onto segmented objects and group the shares by instruction role: the named objects, the strongest competing object, the remaining objects, and the gripper. Because the channels describe roles rather than object identities, they are comparable across instructions, tasks, and VLA architectures. The design uses successful and failed test behavior, but thresholds use successes only, without failure-labeled training.
At deployment, an LLM maps a free-form request

to a canonical action schema and checks its safety and feasibility. For an admissible command, the system retrieves a successful reference with the same action type and named objects. Time-step comparison would be too rigid because successful executions may perform the same phases at different speeds. We therefore use dynamic time warping (DTW) [6] to align phases while preserving their order. Two complementary readouts follow: on-track measures adherence to a successful path, while completion measures whether the complete attention program has appeared. Conformal prediction [7], using leave-one-out calibration, sets the threshold height from successful episodes’ normalized sustained deviations, accounting for both readouts and the sustain window with a nominal 5% false-stop target. If either deviation remains above its threshold for a sustained period, OGAM stops the policy early.
We evaluate OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, UniVLA, and π0.5 on LIBERO-Goal [8]. On existing benchmark tasks, the policies achieve the original goals in 48–92% of episodes under paraphrased instructions. However, none of the four VLA policies completes any of the 24 feasible instructions generated by combinatorial testing, exposing a shared limitation beyond existing benchmark tasks. OGAM detects 87–100% of failed episodes while falsely stopping 3–5% of successful episodes. Median stopping times are 5–12 s within a 20 s budget, saving 39–72% of failing-episode time. Ablations assess component contributions and compare other detectors under the same rule.
Contributions. (1) Coverage-driven VLA testing that combines scene-derived instruction generation, combinatorial coverage, expected behavior labels, and paraphrasing. (2) An action-relevant, object-level attention course whose instruction-role channels are comparable across tasks and policies. (3) A training-free monitor combining open-end DTW, on-track and completion readouts, and success-only conformal calibration of sustained deviations for early stopping, which complements test coverage without failure-labeled training or per-model hyperparameter tuning.
II. RELATED WORK
Testing language-conditioned policies. LIBERO [8] benchmarks language-conditioned manipulation. LIBERO-Para [9] shows that rewording instructions can sharply reduce success, while [10] finds that policies may execute commands contradicting the scene. Combinatorial software testing [5] covers parameter interactions with small suites. We use action templates

and scene objects as factors to isolate linguistic variation. Each command receives an expected behavior label, building on command feasibility, help seeking, and harmful compliance [11]–[14]. Existing VLA testing platforms use language models to mutate scenes or generate instructions [15], [16], primarily as black-box tests. Our tests additionally supply object-grounded attention for runtime monitoring and reveal behavior substitution and failure on all 24 feasible generated instructions across four policies.
Failure detection for robot policies. Runtime detectors judge rollouts with separate models [17], monitor outputs through action-chunk consistency [18], entropy and observation out-of-distribution scores [19], learned action probes [20], or internal features [21], [22]. SAFE, ActProbe, and hide-and-seek require labeled successes and failures. Sentinel and FIPER calibrate on successes; FIPER adds a conformal guarantee. Our monitor uses success-only leave-one-out conformal calibration with a nominal target and empirically assessed false-stop rates (Sec. III-D). It grounds action-relevant attention in object roles and uses DTW to compare patterns across execution speeds. Unlike FIPER, it needs neither repeated generative-policy sampling nor a separately trained observation model, and supports stepwise and chunked policies. The closest attention-head monitor [23] does not combine object-role grounding, time-warped alignment, and sustained early stopping.
Attention in VLA models. Attention supports policy modification through safety filters [24], training-free recalibration [10], and visual token pruning [25]. For post-hoc diagnosis, VLA-Trace [26] uses techniques related to attention rollout [27] and gradient-weighted relevance [28], [29]. We leave policies unchanged and, given debate over attention’s explanatory meaning [30], validate whether its DTW-aligned course detects failures early enough to stop them. Like [10], [25], we connect grounding failures with attention, but make the signal object-level, temporal, and actionable through a runtime stop rule.

Expected and observed behavior. Following [11]– [13], we label instruction-scene pairs feasible (should be completed), infeasible (cannot be meaningfully executed and should fail safely), or hazardous (realizable but unsafe to follow indiscriminately). An LLM assigns scene-conditioned labels for independent human validation. We compare rollouts against these expectations rather than potentially absent goal predicates, recording actions, observations, and attention.
LIBERO-Goal instantiation. We extract objects from the BDDL scene description and templates from the benchmark instructions. Exhaustive instantiation produces 234 candidates; pairwise coverage reduces them to 90. After removing three canonical tasks, 87 tests remain.

B. Instruction Perturbation and Object Poses
Paraphrasing. Whereas the combinatorial suite varies what is requested, paraphrasing varies how a fixed task is expressed. We use the 13 LIBERO-Para types [9]: three rename the object and ten rephrase the action using lexical, structural, or pragmatic changes. Each paraphrase preserves the action, objects, and outcome; adds no visual or spatial attribute; and avoids references confusable with other scene objects. Applying one type to each of the ten LIBERO-Goal tasks yields 130 instructions per policy, all retaining their simulator goal predicates.
Extension to object poses. Position and orientation can be discretized as additional covering-array factors, after which instantiated objects are perturbed to the selected poses. This directly follows the combination, instantiation, and perturbation in testing autonomous driving systems such as ComOpT [31]. The extension is straightforward, but the present experiments keep object poses fixed to isolate linguistic variation.

C. Object-level Attention

The policy’s internal attention is turned into a compact, task-comparable time series in three steps.
Step 1 (one attention map per action query). At each action query, we extract gradient-weighted attention over image patches, in the spirit of GradCAM [28]. Weighting by influence on the predicted action emphasizes action-relevant regions. Maps exist only at policy queries; sampling intervals depend on the action interface (Sec. IV).
Step 2 (projection onto scene objects). Object masks make patch-level maps comparable across time and episodes. In simulation, we use the environment’s per-pixel masks. On a real robot, the same projection can use masks predicted by a segment-everything model [32], here SAM 2 prompted by Grounding DINO [33], [34]. We store one score rj per held-out success, recomputing its task mean (eq. 6) and spread without it; canonical references stay fixed. Runtime uses one monitor fitted on all successes (Sec. IV). Reading inside out, the ratio measures normalized excess cost; the minimum gives the lowest ratio within a 3 s window; and the maxima select the largest window minimum across all eligible windows and both readouts. Each readout uses its corresponding excess and spread under leave-one-out scoring; this

dependence is implicit. For these scoring quantities, rj is the smallest threshold height that avoids stopping episode j: the rule triggers exactly when rj > q. Finally, we sort all n = |S| stored scores and choose q as the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest, using nominal α = 0.05. This height is fixed at runtime, with θ(k) = q s(k) for each readout. Here α = 0.05 is nominal. Split conformal gives the marginal bound Pr(rnew > q) ≤ α for exchangeable calibration and new-success scores with a scoring function fixed independently of calibration [37]. Our jackknife procedure reuses successes to estimate task means and spread; we claim no such finite-sample bound [36]. We assess false stops by leave-one-out refitting and report a split with a fixed training part (Sec. V-C).
IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Environment, benchmark and policies. Simulations ran on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 workstation with 128 GB RAM and Ubuntu 24.04 LTS. We use LIBEROGoal [8]’s ten kitchen tasks with fixed initial states and object poses, deterministic execution, and 20 s episodes

(200 steps at 10 Hz). In-benchmark success uses simulator goal predicates. The fine-tuned policies are OpenVLA-7B [1], OpenVLA-OFT [2], UniVLA [3], and π0.5 [4]. OpenVLA and UniVLA query every 0.1 s; we retain every second attention map (0.2 s). OFT predicts 8-step chunks (0.8 s); π0.5 predicts 50step chunks and executes 10 (1.0 s). Interpolation to the shared 0.2 s grid reads the following query. Nativegrid detection and false-stop counts are unchanged (Table III); OFT’s median stop moves from 9.6 to 7.2 s. We reconstruct stops from rollouts recorded to success or 20 s. Apart from interpolation’s one-query look-ahead, the rule reads no later query; saved time is rollout time after the stop.
Instruction suites. Ten canonical instructions and 130 LIBERO-Para paraphrases [9] (13 per task; Sec. III-B) evaluate four policies using unchanged goal predicates. The 87 pairwise instructions (Sec. III-A) test all four policies beyond benchmark coverage in the task-0 scene. Without goal predicates, rollouts receive human review, free-text behavior notes, and tags.
Data split and calibration. Each policy’s ten successful canonical episodes serve only as references. The 130 paraphrases supply evaluation failures and successes (Table II); the successes also calibrate task means (eq. 6), spread s(k), and q. The deployed monitor fits all successes and scores every failure. Each success is scored by refitting on the others, excluding it from its own threshold. The leave-oneout score quantile (Sec. III-D) at nominal α = 0.05 gives q = 1.99, 1.79, 2.83, and 2.04 for OpenVLA, OFT, UniVLA, and π0.5, respectively. Burn-in (2 s) and sustain (3 s) are shared; no failure data or per-policy hyperparameter tuning enter.
Masks and ablation. All policies use gradientweighted attention (Sec. III-C), primarily with simulator masks. In the segmenter ablation, Grounding DINO [34] instead grounds nine object classes in the first frame, retaining the first accepted box from one or two prompts per class. SAM 2 [33] propagates instances on attention-sampling frames. At 256 × 256 resolution, the three drawers and cabinet body are indistinguishable and form one instance. The gripper retains its simulator mask; on hardware, this would come from kinematics. Attention maps, reference selection, calibration procedure, and stop rule are unchanged; we recalibrate the band height (Sec. V-C).

V. RESULTS
A. Combinatorial testing exposes failures on feasible instructions
Feasibility labels. The LLM classified 87 cases as 24 feasible, 62 infeasible, and 1 hazardous. Blind human review agreed on 74 (85%). The 13 disagreements chiefly concern whether “put X in Y ” with a noncontainer means placement or literal containment.
Failure on feasible instructions. OpenVLA completes none of the 24 feasible instructions, exposing poor generalization beyond canonical benchmark tasks. The other three VLAs likewise follow none of the 87 generated instructions, including all 24 feasible cases.
Behavior substitution. OpenVLA executed or visibly attempted a trained task in 43 of 87 episodes (Table I), ignoring the requested verb and relation. Naming the plate triggered bowl interactions, as in “put the bowl on the plate”; 42 episodes involved unnamed objects.
Hazards out of harmless requests. Thirteen OpenVLA episodes produced unrequested hazards: winebottle tipping during failed grasps (three times), cream cheese squeezed between plate and stove during a reenacted push, a bowl dropped from height, and an activated stove left unattended for “put the rack in the stove”. Non-completion alone does not distinguish safe non-execution from these substitutions and hazards.
B. Paraphrase robustness differs across VLAs
All four policies succeed on all ten canonical instructions. Under paraphrases, success drops to 63/130 (48%) for OpenVLA, 105/130 (81%) for UniVLA, 115/130 (88%) for OFT, and 119/130 (92%) for π0.5. With task, scene, and initial state fixed, wording alone produces this variation, motivating runtime monitoring.

VI. CONCLUSION
Across four policies, systematic testing reveals failures on feasible instructions, behavior substitution, hazards, and attention divergence that motivate runtime assurance beyond finite instruction coverage. Our monitor grounds action-relevant attention in object roles and combines DTW comparison with successful references and conformal calibration of sustained deviations. Across four VLA architectures, it stops 87–100% of failures at median times of 5–12 s within 20 s, with 3–5% false stops, without trained failure detectors, failure-labeled calibration, or per-model hyperparameter tuning. Segment-everything masks cost five percentage points of detection on OpenVLA without extra false stops. While our framework is not restricted to simulation, evaluation on hardware robots remains future work.
