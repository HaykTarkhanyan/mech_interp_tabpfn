THE LINEAR REPRESENTATION HYPOTHESIS FOR VISION-LANGUAGE-ACTION MODELS
Minseok Jeong1 Hyewon Choi1 Hiroyasu Tsukamoto2 Soojean Han1 1KAIST 2University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign {dsa950115,chw110825,soojean}@kaist.ac.kr hiroyasu@illinois.edu
ABSTRACT
The linear representation hypothesis (LRH) has become a standard lens for measuring and intervening on semantic information through the internal representations of large language models (LLMs). A growing body of work has begun extending this perspective to vision-language-action (VLA) models, but the dynamical nature of embodied interaction introduces an additional challenge. Unlike semantic attributes commonly studied in LLMs, such as gender or language, a physical quantity of interest (QoI) in a VLA evolves jointly with the system dynamics: the representation influences the actions selected by the policy, which alter the physical state and, in turn, the next representation. In this paper, we develop a theoretical, signature-based formulation of the LRH for VLA that unifies representations and policies. On the representation side, we establish the existence of representations from which the future evolution of a QoI under a candidate action trajectory can be recovered via linear probing. On the policy side, we introduce a signature generalized linear model for stochastic action chunks. This structure yields a monotonic change in the expected future QoI along linear paths in natural parameter space, enabling linear steering. We first validate this structure in a controlled oracle setting, then examine whether the same probing and steering mechanisms emerge in a pretrained VLA.
1 INTRODUCTION
The linear representation hypothesis (LRH) in large language models (LLMs) states that high-level concepts, such as gender and language, have representations that occupy particular linear directions, or low-dimensional subspaces, in their representation space (Mikolov et al., 2013; Elhage et al., 2022; Park et al., 2024). Its appeal is that this geometry provides a simple mathematical interface to otherwise opaque models. After identifying a linear representation corresponding to a concept, it can be used in two ways: probing measures the concept by projecting representations onto that direction (Alain & Bengio, 2016; Belinkov, 2022), while steering intervenes on the concept by moving representations along it (Li et al., 2023b; Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023). These two operations have become a standard interface to understand the internal structures of LLMs, supported by substantial empirical evidence (Li et al., 2023a; Marks & Tegmark, 2024; Templeton et al., 2024) and, more recently, by systematic theoretical analysis (Park et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025; Garg et al., 2026).
It is natural to seek the same interface for robot control with vision- language-action (VLA) models, where the motivation is even stronger. VLA models (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025) are large, expensive to fine-tune, and trained on demonstration data that is costly to collect (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024; Khazatsky et al., 2024); retraining is rarely an option, e.g., when one needs to understand what the agent has inferred from the scene or when a policy must be made more cautious. The representation space offers a cheaper point of access. If task-relevant physical information is encoded in a representation space and can be measured, then we can use it without additionally instrumenting the environment or modifying the model weights. If behavior is steerable, it can be adjusted at inference time without collecting new robot demonstrations. Recent work has increasingly built on this premise (Lu et al., 2025; Ha¨on et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Miao et al., 2026), bringing much of the LLM-side LRH framework into VLAs: physical concepts and their representations, together with probing and steering.

But the objects of interest are not quite the same. In an LLM, commonly probed semantic directions, such as gender Rep(“Female”) − Rep(“Male”) or language Rep(“English”) − Rep(“French”), are relatively static. In a VLA, by contrast, many quantities of interest pertain to physical systems and, crucially, their evolutions: the pose of a manipulated object, or the force at a contact. A fixed representation direction may suffice to read out such a quantity at the current state, but decisionmaking often depends on how that quantity changes under action. For example, what matters is whether the object will reach a desired height or whether the contact force will not become excessive throughout task execution. Such future quantities depend not only on the current representation, but also on the action trajectory executed from that state and the dynamics through which those actions unfold. Although recent empirical studies on VLA models have shown promising results (Lu et al., 2025; Buurmeijer et al., 2026), a rigorous theoretical framework for characterizing this dynamical coupling remains largely undeveloped relative to the LRH literature for LLMs.
We therefore develop a mathematical abstraction of VLA models centered on the representation– action–dynamics interface. Our contributions are as follows.
1. A control-theoretic formulation of the LRH for VLA models. We connect VLA models to the underlying controlled dynamical system, leading to VLA-specific notions of probing and steering. Building on these notions, we formulate the LRH for VLA models and identify the key challenges arising from their architectural differences from LLMs.
2. Shared linear representations for linear probing. We show constructively that an ideal VLA representation can simultaneously encode the information of multiple physical quantities of interest, with each propagated value under a candidate action trajectory admitting a linear probe in representation space.
3. Generalized linear policies for linear steering. We introduce a path signature-based exponential family formulation of VLA policies, whose geometry provides a dually flat statistical manifold. Building on this geometry, we show that linear probing gives rise to linear steering in the representation space.
4. Validation from oracle models to pretrained VLAs. We first verify the predicted probing and steering mechanism exactly in a controlled oracle construction. We then test the framework on a pretrained OpenVLA-OFT, where both linear probing and steering remain competitive with substantially more flexible nonlinear alternatives.

Basic background on VLA models. We consider a minimal abstraction of a VLA model. Let x denote the underlying physical state, which is not directly accessible to the model. Instead, the VLA receives an observation, o = h(x), which may include visual inputs, multiple camera views, or proprioceptive robot state. Given a language instruction ℓ, the VLA backbone maps o to an internal representation λ¯(o, ℓ). Throughout the paper, we fix ℓ and suppress its dependence. Motivated by recent work on physically meaningful visual representations (Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025), we conjecture that such information is accessible from the VLA representation. We therefore define λ(x) := λ¯(h(x), ℓ). The representation λ(x) is then passed to a stochastic action policy π, such as a diffusion-based action-chunk policy. For our analysis, we model the resulting action chunk as a continuous-time open-loop trajectory over a horizon H, u := {u(t)}t∈[0,H] ∼ π(· | λ(x)). The resulting VLA pipeline is summarized as
x −→h o −λ¯−(·−,ℓ→) λ(x) −→π u ∼ π(· | λ(x)).
We ask whether the LRH extends to this largely unexplored architectural setting.

2.2 PROBING AND STEERING IN VLA MODELS
We now adapt the notions of probing and steering to the VLA setting. Unlike LLM representations, VLA representations are coupled to actions that induce physical state transitions through interaction with the environment. We must therefore reformulate probing and steering to reflect this interaction. For probing, we consider a physically meaningful QoI ψ(x) and ask whether its future value under a candidate action trajectory u can be inferred from the VLA representation.
Probing Problem
Given a QoI ψ : X → R and a candidate action trajectory u ∈ UH , can the propagated QoI ψ(Fu(x))
be predicted using only the VLA representation λ(x) and u, without explicit access to the underlying physical state or dynamics?

Existing VLA probing methods focus on static QoI, asking whether ψ(x) can be decoded directly from the internal representation (Lu et al., 2025; Bhardwaj et al., 2026), with promising empirical results. However, static probing does not capture the action-conditioned evolution of the physical quantity. This distinction is particularly important for VLA models, whose latent representations both generate state-transitioning actions and provide a space for steering them (Ha¨on et al., 2025).2
For steering, we ask whether an intervention in the representation space can alter the action trajectory generated by the policy so as to induce a desired change in the propagated physical state.
Steering Problem

Given a QoI ψ : X → R, can we modify the VLA representation λ(x) so that the resulting policy generates an action trajectory u ∼ π(· | Steer(λ(x))) that drives the propagated QoI

toward a desired value?

In general, a steering map λ(x) → Steer(λ(x)) need not admit a simple algebraic form. Recent work on VLA steering has therefore explored more structured interventions on internal representations, including conceptor-based subspace operators and learned distribution-matching maps that go beyond a single additive steering direction (Miao et al., 2026; Khayatan et al., 2026).
Together, these two problems characterize the role of the representation λ in a VLA model: probing asks whether the representation contains sufficient information to predict the effect of a candidate action, whereas steering asks whether modifying the representation can systematically alter that effect through the policy.

3 LINEAR PROBING AND STEERING IN VLA MODELS
The linear representation hypothesis posits that both problems above admit a common resolution: linear structure in the VLA representation space. We extend the LRH for LLMs (Park et al., 2024) to VLA models by characterizing two complementary notions: linear probing and linear steering. Definition 3.1 (Linear Probing in VLA). Let ψ : X → R be a QoI. We say that ψ admits linear probing in the representation λ : X → Rd if there exists a map γψ : UH → Rd such that
ψ(Fu(x)) = ⟨λ(x), γψ(u)⟩Rd , ∀ x ∈ X , u ∈ UH .
Note that this structure differs from the LLM probing setting. There, the probe is typically given by a single global direction γψ ∈ Rd, and the QoI is estimated from that direction alone; in our setting, the corresponding object depends on the action trajectory u. Such dependence again reflects the interaction structure discussed above. The usual static probing setting is recovered by restricting to a no-transition case, in which action dependence is no longer required.
Challenges in linear probing. In the LLM setting, the relevant linear measurement direction γψ is naturally tied to the rows of the output projection (or unembedding) matrix preceding the softmax. In contrast, VLA models do not provide an analogous, predefined measurement structure for propagated physical QoIs. A key challenge is therefore to characterize an action-conditioned map γ : UH → Rd that associates action trajectory with the representation space. Definition 3.2 (Linear Steering in VLA). Let ψ : X → R be a QoI. We say that ψ admits linear steering at x ∈ X if there exists a nonzero vector λψ(x) ∈ Rd such that
α −→ Eu∼π(·|λ(x)+αλψ(x)) [ψ(Fu(x))] is nondecreasing.

In our formulation, unlike standard LLM steering approaches that rely on a fixed global direction λψ ∈ Rd, the steering direction λψ(x) is generally state-conditioned. This is a natural extension in the VLA setting, since the propagated QoI ψ(Fu(x)) depends on both the current state and the action trajectory through the system dynamics; consequently, the same change in action can have different effects on the propagated QoI at different states.
Challenges in linear steering. To control a physical QoI through a linear translation λ → λ + αλψ, we need to understand how such an intervention changes the downstream policy distribution and, consequently, the resulting physical behavior. To characterize this relationship, we adopt the information-geometric perspective of the Softmax (Park et al., 2026), viewing λ → π(· | λ) as a parameterization of a family of VLA policies. This motivates us to seek a generalized linear model (GLM) parameterization of the VLA policy under which the geometry induced by variations in λ admits an explicit characterization. However, owing to their structural differences from LLMs, modern generative VLA policies do not naturally admit such a convenient parameterization.

4 EXISTENCE OF REPRESENTATION AND MEASUREMENT MAPS

In this section, we take a closer look at linear probing in VLA models and address two fundamental theoretical questions as follows:

1. Can the VLA representation λ capture the physical information necessary to characterize multiple quantities of interest, Ψ = {ψj}jp=1?
2. For each QoI ψj, can we construct an action-conditioned measurement map γψj such that the propagated QoI admits a linear probe from the VLA representation λ?

Before stating our main result, we introduce a key object used in its construction. Analogous to the role of unembedding vectors in the softmax-based measurement of LLMs, the action-conditioned measurement map γ can be chosen to factor through a signature of the action trajectory.

Thus, the action dependence is captured by the signature, with Lψj specifying the QoI-specific measurement structure.

5 GEOMETRY OF THE REPRESENTATION SPACE

Section 4 addressed the representation-side question of whether propagated QoIs can admit actionconditioned linear probes. Linear probing alone, however, does not determine how an intervention on the representation changes the downstream action distribution. Thus, we next introduce a geometry on the representation space for formulating linear steering. To this end, we view stochastic action-chunk policies, such as autoregressive and diffusion policies, as defining a family of conditional distributions over action trajectories u ∈ UH

P := {π(· | λ) : UH → R≥0} ,

and regard this family as parameterized by λ. To emphasize that the policy is parameterized by the representation λ, we henceforth write the policy as πλ. Motivated by the geometric structure induced by the softmax-based distribution family in (Park et al., 2026), we would like to introduce a corresponding notion of geometry in representation space through P.

For the policy-side analysis, we adopt the following identification. In general, the distribution family induced by a modern generative policy may possess a highly complex parametric structure. To obtain a tractable yet expressive representation of path-dependent statistics, we lift action trajectories u → S(u) into the signature space S. This construction leverages the universality of linear functionals on signatures for approximating continuous path functionals (Lyons, 2014).

We then place this construction in a Hilbert-space setting by replacing the finite-dimensional representation space Rd with the Hilbert signature space HS, obtained by completing S under a suitable inner product ⟨·, ·⟩HS . This formulation naturally bridges the representation and policy modules. Specifically, we regard λ(x) ∈ Θ ⊆ HS simultaneously as the VLA representation and as the natural parameter of the signature exponential family. This identification allows us to draw on the
well-developed body of work on linear representations in LLMs, including their geometry, and
steering (Park et al., 2024; Marks & Tegmark, 2024; Park et al., 2026).

7 CONCLUSION
We developed a control-theoretic formulation of the linear representation hypothesis for visionlanguage-action models, centered on the evolution of physical quantities under candidate actions. On the representation side, we showed that propagated QoIs admit action-conditioned linear probes through signature-based measurement maps. On the policy side, we introduced a signature generalized linear model and showed that this geometry induces linear steering directions along which the expected propagated QoI changes monotonically.
Our results support the proposed linear probing and steering framework at both oracle and pretrained-VLA levels. An explicit oracle construction verifies the predicted mechanisms exactly, including the derivative–variance identity, while experiments on OpenVLA-OFT provide evidence that analogous structure can emerge in a learned VLA. Given the still-emerging VLA landscape, however, identifying a representation λ naturally suited to both probing and steering remains an open challenge. We therefore view our experiments primarily as a proof of concept for the existence and realizability of such a representation.

D.1 SETUP

Model and representation. OpenVLA-OFT decodes an action chunk in parallel: the last-layer

hidden states of the Llama-2 backbone at the positions of the action tokens are mapped by a small

latent

where P keeps the leading principal components of λ(x), fitted on training states. The representation λ(x) of the theory corresponds to the full representation; z(x) is its truncation to the leading principal coordinates, and we apply Theorem 3.1 with z in place of λ. The policy chunk is a deterministic function of the representation, u(x) = head(λ(x)): re-applying the head to the stored λ(x) reproduces the executed chunk exactly. Representation interventions therefore act directly on the action, without passing through the language model again.

Task and data. We use the checkpoint fine-tuned on LIBERO-10 and the task shown in Figure 4. We roll out the policy from all 50 initial layouts (46 successful) and take a snapshot every 16 control steps (788 states). At every second snapshot (404 states) we restore the simulator and execute 32 candidate chunks of Hchunk = 8 steps: the policy chunk and 31 smooth perturbations of it.
Quantities of interest. The QoIs are the 3D positions of the two mugs (mug1: white, mug2: yellow-and-white) and of the end effector (EE); the EE coordinates serve as a control, since under delta end-effector actions their change follows directly from the action. For steering, we additionally use four task-level QoIs: the negative horizontal distance −d(mugj, plate) of each mug to its target plate and the negative distance −d(EE, mugj) between the EE and each mug, so that increasing the QoI means moving towards the goal; the target plate of each mug is the one it ends on in all successful episodes. All results are on held-out episodes.

• static: the static probe direction L∅ψ, which changes what the representation encodes about the current value ψ(x).
• simulator gradient: ∇zJψ,x at α = 0, obtained by central differences of the simulated QoI along the 32 principal directions. It uses no probe and serves as an oracle for the best local linear steering direction in this subspace.
• random: three random directions.
What the construction guarantees. Along zψ = Dϕ⊤λψ, the probe’s prediction increases to first order, Jˆψ,x(α) = Jˆψ,x(0) + α ∥Dϕ⊤λψ∥2 + O(α2), for any differentiable head. Whether the true QoI increases depends on the probe residual r(u) = ψ(Fu(x)) − ψˆ(Fu(x)): the true slope is ⟨Dϕ⊤λψ + ∇zr(u(z)), zψ⟩, so the probe must be accurate for the chunk variations that the intervention induces, which is not implied by a high R2 on the data. Monotonicity beyond first order is not guaranteed and is tested empirically.
Computation. Dϕ is obtained by central differences along the 32 principal directions (step 0.25 standard deviations), which requires only re-applying the action head to the displaced representations. Re-applying the head to the stored (float16) representation reproduces the executed policy chunk up to 3.3 · 10−3 on the arm dimensions, far below the size of the perturbations. The simulator gradient uses the same displacements, executed in the simulator. All directions are rescaled to the same displacement of λ per unit α, 0.5% of ∥λ(x)∥, and α ∈ {−8, −4, −2, 0, 2, 4, 8}, i.e. up to 4% of the representation norm.
Evaluation protocol. Episodes are split into two halves. The principal components, both probes and all directions are fitted on one half, and steering is evaluated at states of the other half; the roles are then swapped. For each QoI we steer at the 10 states per half with the largest spread of the QoI among the candidate chunks; for the mug heights and the mug–plate distances we require that at least half of the candidate chunks change the QoI by more than 2 mm, which selects states in which the mug responds to the action at all. We report (i) the share of states with a monotone response (Spearman correlation between α and Jψ,x(α) at least 0.8), (ii) the median effect Jψ,x(8)− Jψ,x(−8), (iii) the share of states in which a direction’s effect exceeds that of all three random directions, (iv) the median cosine with the simulator gradient, and (v) for Dϕ⊤λψ, the ratio of the measured slope (Jψ,x(2) − Jψ,x(−2))/4 to the probe-predicted slope ⟨λψ, Dϕ zψ⟩. Table 5 lists (i)–(iv) for every QoI, and Figure 5 shows the full response curves.
A linear steering direction exists. The simulator gradient raises the QoI monotonically at all 140 held-out states, by +9 to +13 mm over the range of α (Table 5, Figure 5). Thus, within the leading 32 principal components, the representation of OpenVLA-OFT admits a linear steering direction in the sense of Theorem 3.2 for each of the seven QoIs, including the four task-level distances, which are nonlinear functions of the object positions.
The probe direction, transported through the head, finds it. Dϕ⊤λψ is monotone at 138 of the 140 states and reaches the effect of the oracle for every QoI (e.g. +12.9 vs. +12.9 mm for the white mug’s height and +12.2 vs. +12.4 mm for the distance of the yellow-and-white mug to its plate), with median cosine 0.98–1.00 to the simulator gradient. The two failures (one state for each of the two EE–mug distances) are states at which the probe is inaccurate for the induced chunk variations. In words: the probe correctly predicts how the head-induced change of the chunk moves the QoI, and combining it with the head’s Jacobian recovers the simulator’s own gradient without querying the simulator.
The static direction is not a steering direction. The effect of the static probe direction depends on the QoI but is remarkably consistent across states for a given QoI. It lowers all three heights on median (−2 to −4 mm) and increases the distance of the white mug to its plate at all 20 states (between −6 and −12 mm on the negative-distance QoI, median −11.3 mm; median cosine −0.61 to the simulator gradient). Changing what the representation encodes about the current value therefore does not make the policy produce the corresponding change, and for several QoIs it produces the opposite. A possible reading is that the policy compensates for a perceived state; we have not tested this interpretation.
The probe gradient alone is not enough. For the heights, γψ is nearly orthogonal to the simulator gradient for the EE and the white mug (median cosine 0.03 and 0.13) and performs close to random; for the yellow-and-white mug it is monotone but reaches about half of the oracle effect. For the mug– plate distances and the distance of the EE to the white mug it does better: it is monotone at all states and reaches 59–74% of the oracle effect, with median cosine 0.35–0.43; for the distance of the EE to the yellow-and-white mug it is weak again (+1.7 vs. +9.4 mm). It remains clearly below Dϕ⊤λψ in every case. This is the expected consequence of Eq. (29): without Dϕ, the direction changes how the probe reads the representation, but not necessarily how the head changes the action; how much of the right direction it retains varies with the QoI, for reasons we have not established.
Relation to Theorem 5.3. OpenVLA-OFT is deterministic, so the exponential-tilting mechanism of Theorem 5.3, which yields a monotone increase with slope Var[ψ] along λψ itself, does not apply. What the experiment establishes is weaker and complementary: the gradient of the probe-predicted QoI, Dϕ⊤λψ, is an effective linear steering direction in the sense of Theorem 3.2, and it coincides with the simulator’s gradient. The comparison with γψ shows which ingredient of Theorem 5.3 is missing in a learned VLA: the identification Dϕ = ∇2A that lets the probe direction be used as is. With a learned head this identification is replaced by the head’s Jacobian, which the model itself provides.
