RoboIRS: Inference-Time Internal Representation Steering for Generalist Robot Policies
Jiuzhou Lei1, Chang Liu1, Dayou Li1, Zhiyuan Zhang2, Xiao Liang3, Yu She2, Zhiwen Fan4, Minghui Zheng1

arXiv:2610.04681v3 [cs.RO] 8 Oct 2026

Abstract—Vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action models (WAMs) often degrade under out-of-distribution task variations despite retaining partial task capability. To recover such capability, we propose RoboIRS, an inference-time internal representation steering method that uses successful and failed rollouts to train linear classifiers, select outcome-relevant intervention locations, and derive task-specific steering directions without updating policy parameters. On 15 simulation tasks with a frozen π0.5 policy, RoboIRS improves the average success rate from 44.4% to 66.2%, outperforming alternative inferencetime intervention baselines while adding little inference time. We further validate RoboIRS on real-robot manipulation using the same π0.5 policy and demonstrate its applicability to a worldaction model Cosmos Policy, where the average success rate improves from 35.4% to 55.4%. These results show that directly steering internal robot-policy representations can improve the performance of robot policies at inference time. Project website is available at https://rollingoat.github.io/roboirs/.

I. INTRODUCTION
Vision-language-action (VLA) models and world-action models (WAMs) have recently emerged as promising architectures for robotic manipulation by integrating visual perception, language-conditioned task understanding, and action generation with future prediction in a unified policy. However, their performance often degrades on out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, such as unseen scenes, novel object layouts, and new object appearances [1], [2]. Importantly, this degradation does not necessarily imply complete incapability: a policy may still succeed on a subset of these OOD tasks, albeit at a lower success rate. This suggests that the policy may retain some of the internal computations required for successful execution even when its behavior becomes unreliable.
A similar phenomenon has already been observed in LLMs, which may produce both truthful and untruthful responses despite being queried under similar conditions. This inconsistency suggests that the model may already contain internal representations associated with the desired behavior, even when they are not reliably expressed in its outputs [3]. Recent

Here, internal representations refer to intermediate output that encode information during model computation; in transformer-based models, these may include residualstream activations passed between layers, as well as activations associated with individual neurons, attention heads, or other localized model components [4]. Prior work has shown that high-level behaviors such as truthfulness, refusal, sentiment,

2

and task execution can be localized in hidden representations and modified through activation-space interventions. Li et al. [3] steer LLMs toward truthful answers by intervening on truth-related activations, while Turner et al. [5] and Rimsky et al. [6] show that contrastive activation directions can control generation behavior without retraining the model. Zou et al. [7] frame such methods as representation engineering, where internal activations are treated as controllable objects for modifying model behavior.
Motivated by the findings in LLM, can we similarly steer the internal representations of a robot policy at inference time to improve its performance on OOD tasks? Recent work has already extended mechanistic interpretability to visionlanguage-action (VLA) models. Grant et al. [8] and Swann et al. [9] use sparse autoencoders to identify interpretable residual-stream features associated with robot behaviors, but do not demonstrate how these features can improve downstream policy performance. Ha¨on et al. [10] and Buurmeijer et al. [11] go further by intervening on internal representations to steer behaviors such as motion speed and end-effector height; however, their evaluations are limited to a small set of predefined behavioral attributes rather than general task performance. Hong et al. [12] instead use an LQR controller to steer the diffusion transformer’s hidden activations such that the resulting activations lead to robust robot behaviors and require constructed contrastive residual-stream pairs from matched nominal and perturbed observations to provide target activation levels.
In this work, we aim to improve the performance of a frozen robot policy under distribution shift through inference-time steering of its internal representations. Rather than retraining the policy, we use these outcome-labeled activations to identify representations associated with successful and failed rollouts and intervene on the policy at inference time using a steering vector. The goal is to bias an already capable but unreliable policy toward internal states that are more predictive of successful action selection. Inspired by [3], we first train a linear classifier to classify the internal representations and then use the classifier weight as steering vectors to change the internal representations such that the predicted actions are more favorable of successful rollouts. We evaluate the proposed approach on two representative robot policies: the vision-language-action model π0.5 [13] and the world-action model Cosmos Policy [14]. We conduct experiments in both simulation and real-world robotic settings to assess whether inference-time internal representation steering improves task success rates under distribution shift.
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
• We propose RoboIRS, which adapts the internal representation-steering paradigm from LLMs to robot policies and enables inference-time intervention on frozen policies using steering vectors derived from the internal representations of successful and failed rollouts.
• We conduct simulation and real-robot experiments showing that RoboIRS improves the average SR of a fully trained π0.5 policy under distribution shift from 44.4% to 66.2%, outperforms alternative inference-time intervention baselines, improves real-robot manipulation perfor-

mance, and is also effective on Cosmos Policy despite its different training recipe and model architecture, increasing the average SR from 35.4% to 55.4%.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Mechanistic Interpretability and Steering in LLMs
Mechanistic interpretability increasingly studies how highlevel concepts are represented in neural activations and whether these representations can be causally manipulated [4], [15]. Many intervention methods build on the observation that properties such as truthfulness and sentiment can be represented by linear directions or low-dimensional subspaces [16], [17], [18]. Activation-steering methods exploit this structure by constructing directions from contrastive examples or demonstrations and injecting them into hidden states to control behavior without retraining [5], [6], [19], [20]. Such interventions have been applied to truthfulness, hallucination, and refusal behavior [3], [21], [22], [23].
B. Mechanistic Interpretability and Internal Representation Steering of Robot Policy Models
Recent robotics work has begun to apply mechanistic interpretability techniques to characterize the internal representations of VLA policies, with several studies further showing that the discovered features can serve as causal handles for behavior steering. Khan et al. [24] use sparse autoencoders to identify action-related latent directions from contrastive action pairs and show that these directions can be manipulated to steer VLA action outputs without retraining. Grant et al. [8] conduct a cross-architecture mechanistic study using activation injection, sparse autoencoders, and linear probes to analyze how visual, language, and action-generation pathways contribute to VLA behavior. Swann et al. [9] use sparse autoencoders to identify interpretable features in VLA hidden states, distinguishing transferable motion primitives from episode-specific memorization, and causally validate selected features through steering experiments.
Several VLA papers move from feature discovery to explicit control of robot behaviors. Buurmeijer et al. [11] formalize feature observability and controllability in VLA models and use lightweight linear interventions to steer encoded features during closed-loop execution. Ha¨on et al. [10] identify sparse semantic directions in transformer activations and use those directions to control policy behavior without retraining. Mitra et al. [25] identify task-relevant attention heads and selectively update them, framing policy adaptation as targeted modification of relevant internal mechanisms. COAST [26] constructs success- and failure-associated conceptor from rollout hidden states to steer VLA activations toward successcritical representations at inference time. COAST is evaluated on imperfect policies, such as early fine-tuning checkpoints, using tasks drawn from the original benchmark distribution, whereas RoboIRS focuses on recovering the performance of fully trained frozen policies under unseen task perturbations. Hong et al. [12] take a control-based approach to representation steering, constructing contrastive directions from paired nominal and perturbed inputs and using them to define

C. Inference-Time Intervention of Robot Policies
Another line of work improves robot policies at inference time through action selection or generative guidance. VGPS [27] samples actions from a frozen policy and re-ranks them with an offline-RL value function, while RoboMonkey [28], MG-Select [29], and RoVer [30] select or refine VLA action candidates using learned verifiers, intrinsic confidence, or process rewards. Related methods intervene directly during action generation: value-guided diffusion [31] biases diffusion trajectories with value gradients. Guided Action Flow [32] keeps the VLA frozen and uses gradients from a critic trained on successful and failed rollouts to steer flow-matching action generation toward higher-return actions. PPGuide [33] trains a performance predictor and uses its guidance signal to steer pretrained diffusion policies away from predicted failure modes during inference.

Since π0.5 is the primary VLA model used in our experiments, we use it as a running example, although the formulation should apply more generally to transformer-based robot policies.

B. Steering Vector
Data Collection. Constructing the steering vectors requires residual streams from both successful and failed rollouts. For

4

rollout i with Ti environment timesteps, we record R(ℓi,t),n for every timestep t, transformer layer ℓ, and denoising step n. The resulting activation tensor is R(i) ∈ RTi×L×N×Ta×d. For example, a 30-step rollout with π0.5 produces a tensor of shape (30, 18, 10, 10, 1024), corresponding to 30 environment timesteps, 18 transformer layers, 10 denoising steps, 10 action tokens, and a hidden dimension of 1024.
We collect up to 10 successful and 10 failed rollouts per task to reduce underdetermination when fitting the linear classifier while keeping data-collection cost manageable. For tasks with very high or very low success rates, fewer examples of the minority outcome may be collected to avoid an excessive number of rollouts.
Contrastive Difference as Steering Vector. Inspired by representation-steering methods for LLMs [6], [3], we consider two approaches for constructing steering vectors. Such sweeps are relatively inexpensive for LLMs, where performance can often be evaluated with a single question–answer interaction. In robotics, however, each candidate typically requires multi-step policy inferences, making an exhaustive search over intervention sites prohibitively expensive. Instead, we use the linear classifier’s AUC on held-out validation rollouts to select the steering location. From the collected successful and failed rollouts, we reserve a subset from each outcome as validation data and use the remaining rollouts for classifier training. Linear Classifier Weight as Steering Vector. The second approach uses the weight of a linear classifier as the steering direction, following analogous approaches in LLMs [3]. For each task, transformer layer, and action-token position, we train a linear classifier to distinguish success-like from failurelike residual streams. The residual stream’s label from each timestep is binary: all timesteps of successful rollouts are 0, and a failed rollout’s timesteps become 1 from the first step where its end-effector leaves the space swept by the successful trajectories.

where y = 1 denotes failure, w ∈ Rd is the classifier weight, and b ∈ R is the bias. We use w as the corresponding steering direction. Thus, each layer–token pair has its own classifier and steering vector. Although this requires training multiple classifiers per task, both the classifiers and datasets are small; in our experiments, all classifiers can be trained in under three minutes using multiple CPUs.
As noted in [8], linear probes trained on residual streams from the action expert achieve high area under the ROC curve (AUC) for predicting task success. Consistent with this finding, our classifiers also achieve good AUC when distinguishing representations from successful and failed rollouts. The average validation AUC is 0.88 for all trained classifiers.

A. Effectiveness of Internal Representation Steering
Q1: Does steering the internal representations improve task success rates in both simulation and the real world?
We first evaluate the proposed method using the π0.5 policy on LIBERO-PRO. We select 15 LIBERO-PRO tasks for which the unsteered policy achieves a non-saturated success rate between approximately 10% and 85%. This ensures that both successful and failed rollouts are available for identifying outcome-relevant internal representations. Table I reports the success rate of each task with and without steering. The Suite column denotes the original LIBERO task suite together with the LIBERO-PRO perturbation type, where env changes the environment or background scene and swap changes object positions to create an unseen layout. Although LIBEROPRO also includes perturbations such as changes to object appearance and task instructions, we exclude those settings because the π0.5 policy either fails almost completely or already achieves nearly 100% success, leaving little room to evaluate steering effectiveness.
For π0.5, we apply steering only at the final denoising step and select the action-token position with the highest validation AUC within each transformer layer, as detailed in Section IV-B. We sweep each task on α = [0.03, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20] for 20 rollouts on one random seed to identify the best steering strength and then use the best steering strength for evaluation for 150 rollouts across 3 random seeds. Overall, steering improves the average success rate from 44.4% to 66.2% on LIBERO-PRO tasks, corresponding to a gain of 21.8 percentage points. Among the 15 evaluated tasks, 12 tasks (80%) improve after steering, and all 12 satisfy the criterion that the steered mean SR exceeds the unsteered mean SR plus one standard deviation. The remaining three tasks do not show a clear improvement in mean SR: L5 and L12 decrease slightly, while L8 remains unchanged. However, the decreases for L5 and L12 remain within the standard-deviation range of the unsteered performance, suggesting that steering does not cause a substantial degradation on these tasks.
We additionally evaluate RoboIRS on two real-robot manipulation tasks, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In both tasks, RoboIRS improves the SR of a π0.5 policy fine-tuned on demonstrations collected under clean-background conditions and evaluated under visually shifted settings.
B. Comparison with Other Baselines and Ablation Studies
Q2: Does the proposed approach outperform alternative inference-time intervention methods?

6

We compare the proposed approach against other inferencetime intervention baselines: contrastive activation addition (CAA) [6], sample and re-rank (Re-rank), and gradient guidance on the flow velocity (Grad.).
This CAA baseline constructs the steering vector using the difference between the residual streams of a selected token from successful and failed rollouts. For a controlled comparison, the CAA baseline uses the same successful and failed rollout data and intervenes at the same model positions. Steering strengths are selected using the same validation protocol.
Following the same candidate generation and selection paradigm as prior sample and re-rank methods [27], [29], [28], we implement a re-ranking baseline for our setting. At each inference step, the observation is replicated across a batch of size k, so that a shared forward pass through the vision encoder and multimodal prefix produces k candidate action chunks that differ only in their flow-matching noise. Each candidate is scored by a learned linear classifier estimating its likelihood of leading to successful execution, and the highestscoring candidate is executed. We evaluate K ∈ {8, 16} and report K = 16, which achieves the higher average SR.
Following the same classifier-guided inference paradigm as prior gradient-guidance methods [32], we train a two-logit classifier for each task to estimate P (success | s, a) using the mean-pooled PaliGemma layer-17 prefix s and flattened action chunk a. In addition, we conduct an ablation study to evaluate whether steering the highest-AUC action token in each transformer layer is beneficial for the π0.5 model. We compare three intervention strategies: steering all action tokens in the single layer with the highest AUC at the final denoising step, steering the highest-AUC token in each layer across all denoising steps, and steering only the highest-AUC token in each layer at the final denoising step. As shown in Table II, steering the highest-AUC token in each layer only at the final denoising step achieves the best overall performance for π0.5.
We also compare classifier-based steering with CAA applied using randomly selected action tokens. Although CAA can outperform probe-based steering on a few individual tasks, its average SR is lower when the intervention token is not selected using the classifier AUC, indicating that the AUC-based token selection could help. Besides, a random-direction control performs near the unsteered baseline, further supporting the importance of the classifier-derived steering direction.

As shown in Table IV, steering improves the macroaverage success rate of Cosmos Policy from 35.4% to 55.4%. Among the eight evaluated tasks, six achieve meaningfully higher success rates after steering.
However, Cosmos Policy differs substantially from π0.5 in its action-generation mechanism. It does not use a separate action expert. Instead, it repeatedly tiles an action chunk into a latent frame and generates that frame with the video diffusion transformer. The action is decoded by averaging these repeated representations. In the LIBERO-PRO configuration, the action frame contains 196 tokens. Steering a single token therefore affects only a small part of the action latent. We instead steer all 196 action-frame tokens in the final transformer block. The corresponding classifier is instead trained on mean-pooled residual streams over the 196 tokens. Because the classifier is trained in normalized residual-stream space, there are two choices of steering vector w/σ and w ⊙ σ. Interpreting w as the classifier normal gives w/σ, which corresponds to the gradient of the classifier score with respect to the raw residual. Alternatively, interpreting w as a displacement direction in normalized representation space gives w⊙σ after mapping the displacement back to the raw residual coordinates. Empirically, for Cosmos Policy, w/σ performs better on tasks C1–C5, whereas w ⊙ σ performs better on C6–C8. Overall, these results show that RoboIRS transfers beyond π0.5 to a policy with a different architecture and training recipe, while requiring architecture-specific choices for classifier and intervention sites.

This might suggest that a direction predictive of success and failure may recover some success-like residual representations without necessarily providing a causally effective steering direction for improving task SR.
What types of failure modes are corrected by steering? We observe two broad categories of failure modes, consistent with the failure taxonomy discussed in Sentinel [36]. The first is dominated by decision-making errors, including L3, L6, L9, L10, L11, L14, and L15, where the policy often makes an incorrect high-level choice despite relatively accurate lowlevel execution. For example, in L10, the unsteered policy frequently selects butter instead of alphabet soup after the object-position swap, whereas the steered policy usually selects the correct object. Across these tasks, RoboIRS improves the average SR by 24.9%.
The second category is dominated by action-precision errors, including L1, L2, L4, L5, L7, L8, L12, and L13. Typical failures include inaccurate grasps that cause objects to slip, repeated unsuccessful execution leading to timeout, or the

gripper becoming stuck on the drawer handle during opening. For these tasks, the average SR improves by 19.1%.
For the three tasks where steering does not improve performance, the corresponding classifiers also have lower validation AUCs: 0.701 for L5, 0.827 for L8, and 0.820 for L12, compared with an average AUC of 0.90 across the selected steering locations for all tasks.
Can a steering direction transfer across perturbations of the same task? Not necessarily, transfer appears to depend on whether the perturbations induce similar failure reasons. For “Put the bowl on the plate,” transferring the L2 steering vector to L5 improves SR from 62% to 86% on the same evaluation seed. Both tasks commonly fail by grasping the bowl inaccurately. In contrast, transferring the L10 vector to L6 for “Pick up the alphabet soup and place it in the basket” reduces SR to 46%. In L10, the robot mainly confuses alphabet soup with butter, whereas it confuses it with salad dressing in L6, suggesting that the learned directions may encode different failure-specific concepts.
V. CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
Conclusion. We propose RoboIRS, an inference-time representation-steering method that uses successful and failed rollouts to identify and manipulate outcome-relevant internal representations without updating policy parameters. RoboIRS improves π0.5 performance on LIBERO-PRO from 44.4% to 66.2%, also improves real-robot manipulation, and transfers to Cosmos Policy, where success rate increases from 35.4% to 55.4%. These results show that internal representations of frozen robot policies can be directly steered to improve task performance at inference time.
Limitations. The proposed method may be less effective on tasks where successful and failed rollouts do not exhibit clear differences in their actions, making it difficult to label and train a classifier. Our experiments are also limited to a small number of VLA and WAM policy architectures, as the primary goal of this work is to investigate the feasibility of transferring representation-steering techniques from LLMs to robotic policies. Different model architectures may encode task-relevant information differently and may therefore require different intervention locations, steering vectors, or steering strategies. Finally, like related representation-steering methods for LLMs, RoboIRS requires empirical selection of hyperparameters such as steering strength, introducing additional cost before deployment.
