Altered Thoughts, Altered Actions: Reasoning Chain as Control Surface for a
Vision-Language-Action Policy
Tuan Duong Trinh Basim Azam Mohammed Ishaq Ansari Mohammed Yaqoob Ansari Naveed Akhtar University of Melbourne, Parkville, VIC, Australia
{tuanduong.trinh.1, basim.azam, naveed.akhtar1}@unimelb.edu.au {ishaq.ansari, yansari}@student.unimelb.edu.au
Abstract
Vision-language-action policies map camera images and natural-language instructions to a robot’s motor actions. Some of these policies are designed to reason in text before acting, generating a reasoning chain and decoding actions conditioned on that chain. The works introducing this design offer the reasoning chain as an oversight interface: text a person can read and edit to correct the policy. What an edited reasoning chain does to the policy’s motor actions, whether it repairs them or corrupts them, has so far been measured only in part. We measure both directions, repair and corruption, with our deterministic entity swap applied to the instruction the policy receives and to the reasoning chain it generates. A forty-task observed backdrop across all four LIBERO simulation suites reveals that the cost of corrupting the reasoning chain concentrates where language alone determines the goal. There, on LIBERO-Goal, we run the counterfactual intervention with DeepThinkVLA, chosen because its reasoning chain is exposed as plain text. The policy receives a corrupted instruction, but its reasoning chain is replaced by the one it generates when that instruction is clean. This counterfactually correct reasoning chain recovers 47.8 pp of the lost success, our pre-registered confirmatory test. Had the chain merely restated what the camera image already determines, the replacement could have changed nothing. Instead, all 10 tasks move in the predicted direction, though success falls short of the clean runs by 38.0 pp, a gap we had predicted at 5–15 pp. The reasoning chain is therefore a working control surface: text written into it moves the robot, repairing behaviour when the text is right and corrupting it when the text is wrong. Whether to expose such a control surface is a deployment tradeoff, and part of it can now be measured.
1 Introduction
Robot manipulation is increasingly addressed by vision-language-action (VLA) policies, which adopt a vision-language model pretrained at internet scale as their backbone [1–4]. The resulting policy maps a camera image and a natural-language instruction to motor actions. Its pretrained backbone contributes open-vocabulary perception and instruction following that no robot-collected dataset could supply alone. That capability has made natural language the interface through which a human operator commands the robot. The same capability is carrying these policies toward deployments where errors have physical consequences. Several of these policies are therefore built for operation under human oversight, in which a person monitors the robot’s intent and intervenes when that intent is wrong [5, 6]. For such oversight to mean anything, the policy’s intent has to be visible somewhere a person can read, and correctable somewhere a person can write.

Reasoning VLAs make that oversight requirement architectural: adapting chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning from language models [7], they generate an intermediate reasoning step in the same context that later conditions their actions. This intermediate step takes different forms across models: text, predicted future images, or latent states [8, 9], of which only text can be read or rewritten by a person. The works introducing text-reasoning VLAs present this affordance as the design’s motivation: the text reasoning chain improves interpretability and lets a person inspect and correct the plan [5, 6]. Zawalski et al. [5] also test that correction: a chain rewritten from human feedback and fed back into their policy raises its success on the hardest tasks they evaluate. A second body of work treats the same reasoning as an attack target. Attacks on language models corrupt the reasoning chain directly [10–12]. Meanwhile, the attacks we are aware of that reach a VLA’s reasoning chain do so indirectly, through an optimized patch in the camera image [13]. The reasoning chain therefore carries two simultaneous roles, oversight interface and attack surface, which have grown in separate literatures. Both literatures have edited the chain, but neither, as we review in Section 2, measures how much of a corrupted instruction’s cost a correct reasoning chain can recover. We study this gap at entity grounding level, where the instruction channel and the reasoning chain channel compete to determine the object acted on. When the instruction and the reasoning chain name different objects, which text does the robot’s success follow, the human operator’s instruction or the policy’s own reasoning chain?
That question has consequences in two directions, because the two roles, oversight interface and attack surface, are a single causal property of the reasoning chain channel. A reasoning chain can always be rewritten by a human operator, but the rewrite corrects the robot only if the actions decoded afterward depend on the chain’s content. A channel with that dependence transmits corruption as well as repair. Corruption therefore matters under a minimal assumption: an adversary with write access to text, and nothing else. That access exists wherever the chain is rendered as plain text between reasoning generation and action decoding, where an adversary controlling the serving stack can replace it. It needs neither physical access to the workspace nor any perturbation of the camera image. Whether such access is plausible is an argument about deployments, not a result of this paper. We measure what the access enables with a counterfactual comparison in which two conditions receive the same corrupted instruction from the same initial states and differ in the reasoning chain.
Two readings of the chain compete: either the actions depend on its content, or the chain merely restates what the camera image already determines. Had the second been true, injecting a correct chain against a corrupted instruction could have changed nothing. That injection is one of four conditions that disagree about which object the task concerns (Figure 1). The disagreement is produced by our deterministic entity swap, which renames every object a text mentions, consistently across its occurrences. In the clean reference (A), both texts are left intact. • The chain is a working correction interface. • The same property is an attack surface. Turned against the chain, with the instruction intact (condition B), the same swap corrupts behaviour, lowering success on the commanded task by 20.8 pp, an observed cost. Repair and corruption are one property, reachable with write access to text alone.
• The tradeoff is largest where language carries the goal. In only one of LIBERO’s four suites does language alone determine the goal, and the observed costs concentrate exactly there.
2 Related Work
Reasoning VLAs. CoT reasoning entered VLA policies with Embodied Chain-of-Thought reasoning [5], which trains a policy to reason step by step in text before acting. That work argues that the intermediate steps improve interpretability and let a human inspect and correct the plan. Its correction experiment fixes the policy’s own errors under a correct instruction: a chain rewritten from human feedback, once per rollout, raises success on its hardest tasks from 32% to 80%. Successor models vary what the intermediate is: CoT-VLA [8] reasons in predicted future image frames, Fast-in-Slow [9] passes its slow reasoning system’s output to its fast acting system as latent features, and DeepThinkVLA [6], the policy we instrument, decodes a text reasoning chain before each action chunk. What matters for our question is a property these models share: the intermediate is not a log written after the fact but an input, generated at inference time into the same context that conditions the actions. In the text case, that input is exposed at an interface where whatever can be read can also be written.
Attacks on reasoning channels. That writable interface, however, is not where attacks on VLA policies concentrate: adversarial perturbations of the camera image degrade or redirect manipulation [15], backdoors implanted at training time fire on visual triggers [16], and jailbreaks of embodied agents arrive through the instruction [17]. None of these takes the reasoning chain as its entry point. That channel is attacked in a parallel literature on language models, through preemptive wrong answers [10], hijacked safety reasoning [11], and backdoored reasoning steps [12], but there the consequence of a corrupted reasoning chain is a wrong answer in text. TRAP [13] bridges the two: it attacks a VLA’s reasoning chain, but only indirectly, through an optimized patch in the camera image. Its sharper contribution is a diagnostic: pairing each retained reasoning chain with a mismatched instruction, TRAP finds that which input wins is model-dependent. One evaluated policy, GraspVLA, completes the reasoning chain’s task in 94.20% of shuffled trials and the instruction’s task in none. We ask the same question with a causal design in place of shuffled pairs: two of our conditions receive the same corrupted instruction and differ in the reasoning chain. Comparing them measures how much of that instruction’s cost a correct chain recovers.
Chain-of-thought oversight. A third literature [18, 19] investigates the read side of the same interface: it asks whether the reasoning chain can be trusted for oversight. Both name the chain a reasoning trace; we keep reasoning chain, following Zawalski et al. [5]. Shiromani and Richter [18] give the pessimistic reading: average-case monitor performance collapses on exactly the inputs where the monitor is the only remaining defence. Their threat model grants the adversary direct write access to the reasoning chain. Emmons et al. [19] give the safe case: when a task cannot be solved without CoT reasoning, the computation must surface in the reasoning chain, so necessity gives the monitor visibility. Their experiment is causal: forcing a wrong intermediate step into the chain pulls the final answer after it, on text tasks. The regime both papers reason about is a reasoning chain that

3 Method: Isolating the Reasoning-Chain Channel
In this section we describe the intervention that separates the two text channels of the policy we study. One channel is the task instruction, which the human operator provides; the other is the reasoning chain, which the policy generates from it. We apply the same entity swap to both, which yields four conditions that disagree about which object the task concerns. We then argue that one contrast among these conditions isolates the reasoning-chain channel, locate the regime where that contrast is meaningful, and state the analysis plan. We fixed the plan before running the confirmatory condition.
3.1 The Policy and Its Two Text Channels
We study DeepThinkVLA [6], a 2.9B-parameter VLA policy. It is built on a PaliGemma backbone [20], in which a SigLIP vision encoder [21] feeds image tokens to a Gemma decoder [22] that also receives the task instruction as plain text. Before acting, the policy performs CoT reasoning: it autoregressively generates a reasoning chain, delimited by <think> tags, that names the objects in the scene and states the motion to perform next. Conditioned on the image, the instruction, and this reasoning chain, the decoder emits a chunk of 10 seven-degree-of-freedom actions in a single bidirectional pass. The policy is queried again every 10 simulation steps and regenerates its reasoning chain at each query. Both text inputs therefore occupy one context window and reach one action decode, as Figure 1 summarizes. Because the reasoning chain is realized as text at inference time, it can be read and replaced before the actions are decoded. This is the read-and-edit affordance that text-reasoning VLAs advertise for oversight and interactive correction [5]. Our intervention operates through exactly this interface.
3.2 The Entity Swap and the Four Conditions
Entity swap. The intervention requires an edit that changes which object a text names and that either channel can carry. To this end, the entity swap matches a text against a fixed pool of benchmark object names and maps each distinct mentioned object to a different object drawn from the same pool. All occurrences are replaced consistently, the surrounding text is preserved verbatim, and the draw is deterministic given a seed. No other fixed-rule corruption meets both requirements: an instruction is one short sentence, so reordering or token-level noise destroys it, while swapping a name leaves it fluent, in-vocabulary, and wrong only about the object. We apply the same swap with the same pool to both channels: Figure 2 shows it rewriting turn on the stove into turn on the cream cheese box in the instruction and inside the reasoning chain alike. The four conditions below differ in which channel finally carries the swap: neither (A), the reasoning chain alone (B), both (C), or the instruction alone (D).
Clean reference (A). Both text channels are left untouched: the instruction as the human operator provided it, the reasoning chain as the policy generated it. A supplies the reference success rate against which the three edited conditions that follow are measured.
Corrupted reasoning chain (B). The instruction stays intact, while at every query we entity-swap the reasoning chain the policy has just generated before it reaches the action decode. B therefore measures the cost of a corrupted reasoning chain in the presence of a correct instruction.
Corrupted instruction (C). We entity-swap the instruction once per task, and the policy regenerates its reasoning chain from what it was given. The corruption propagates downstream: both text channels end up naming the wrong object. C therefore measures the cost of a corrupted instruction when the policy is left to reason from it.
Counterfactual reasoning chain (D). Under D the policy receives the same corrupted instruction as C, byte-identical, which we verify per task. At every query, however, we inject the reasoning chain that the policy itself generates from the clean instruction and the current image. D therefore measures rescue: whether the correct reasoning chain restores success despite the corrupted instruction. D is the confirmatory condition: the analysis plan was fixed before it ran. B and C were measured before that plan existed, so we label them observed wherever their numbers appear.
Each condition runs the 10 tasks of LIBERO-Goal [14] for 3 run seeds (42, 123, 456) with 20 episodes per task, i.e. 600 episodes per condition, under a 300-step cap and greedy decoding. The episode seed is the run seed plus the episode index; it draws the episode’s initial state and seeds B’s swap. C and D share each task’s instruction corruption within a run seed.
Why the contrast isolates the channel. Conditions D and C therefore share the corrupted instruction, the initial states, and the query cadence. They differ in whether the reasoning chain is regenerated from the corrupted instruction or injected from the clean one. Two readings of the reasoning chain make opposite predictions here. If the reasoning chain merely restated what the camera image already determines, injection would change nothing and D would sit at C’s level. If the actions depend on its content, D recovers toward the clean reference. The injected reasoning chain is not synthetic text: we extract it from the policy at the same step, prompted with the clean instruction and the current image, so it stays grounded in the live scene. The extraction and the action decode are separate passes: D’s actions are decoded from the image, the corrupted instruction and the injected chain alone, so the clean instruction reaches them only through the injected text. This injection pathway, which B shares, differs from the native generation that A and C use (Appendix A). We checked it at a single state: re-injecting the chain the policy had just generated changed the first action of the chunk by at most 0.024, about a quarter of that action’s largest translation component, 0.092. The pathway’s effect on task success was not measured. Finally, LIBERO-Goal places its 10 tasks in one scene, so the observation is compatible with every goal in the suite; once the instruction is corrupted, the injected reasoning chain is the only input that still names the original goal. Rescue under D therefore shows that the actions depend on the chain’s content: replacing the regenerated chain with the correct one moves them back toward the object the task intends.

3.3 Where the Tradeoff Is Measurable, and the Analysis Plan
The structural rule. Whether corrupting either text channel can matter at all depends on what the evaluation benchmark actually asks of language. We therefore parse the ground-truth task specifications of all four LIBERO suites (Table 1; the per-task parse appears in Appendix E). We run the intervention on LIBERO-Goal, the one suite where language alone determines the goal and therefore where a language-channel tradeoff is largest and most cleanly measured. We concede that every claim made anywhere in this paper is scoped to the regime this structural rule identifies.
Analysis plan, fixed before the confirmatory run. The unit of analysis is the task: we average each task’s successes over the 3 seeds and 20 episodes and treat the 10 tasks as paired observations, never the seeds as independent samples. The design pre-specifies one confirmatory test, rescue, D over C, evaluated with an exact one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test; at n = 10 the null distribution is enumerated exactly, and the test rejects at α = 0.05 exactly when W + ≥ 45. It also pre-specifies a bound: the one-sided upper 95% confidence bound on the residual, clean minus D, read against a prediction band of 5–15 pp written before the run. The comparison of D against B is descriptive, an effect with its confidence interval and no test, and we give the reason beside the result. Every comparison carries a paired Cohen’s d, a 95% percentile bootstrap interval that resamples the 10 tasks, and its n; the intervals speak to tasks selected by the structural rule, not to a wider population. The full pre-registration, with its falsification criteria, is reproduced in Appendix G. Each job ran on one NVIDIA A100-40GB GPU, and the study used approximately 140 GPU-hours including verification gates (Appendix I).
4 Results
We report the confirmatory rescue first and read it against its pre-registered prediction band (§4.1). We then set the rescue beside the observed cost of a corrupted reasoning chain, compare the two corruptions (§4.2), and close with the scope of the data (§4.3). Every comparison below is paired by task and reports an effect size and a 95% confidence interval; only the confirmatory comparison carries a significance test, one-sided and exact.
4.1 A Correct Reasoning Chain Partially Repairs a Corrupted Instruction
The confirmatory rescue. Table 2 reports success rates under all four conditions on the 10 LIBEROGoal tasks. With both channels clean (A), the policy succeeds in 97.3% of episodes. Corrupting the instruction while the reasoning chain regenerates from it (C) collapses success to 11.5%, a degradation of 85.8 pp; injecting the counterfactually correct reasoning chain in its place, under the identical corrupted instruction (D), lifts it back to 59.3%. Our confirmatory test concerns exactly this contrast: D exceeds C by 47.8 pp (95% CI [31.7, 63.8], paired d = 1.78). The exact one-sided Wilcoxon returns W + = 55 of 55, the maximum attainable at n = 10, with p = 0.0010. Figure 3 shows the contrast task by task: the sign is uniform, the magnitude is not. The per-task rescue spans 10.0 pp to 86.7 pp, and D returns some tasks to their clean reference while on others most of C’s loss remains. The pre-registered causal claim is supported: with the instruction corrupted, replacing the reasoning chain moves behaviour back toward the intended goal on every task. A chain that merely restated what the camera image already determines could not have done that.
The pre-registered band. Did the rescue match the size we predicted for it before the run? We pre-registered a residual, clean minus D, of 5–15 pp, the expectation being that a correct reasoning chain would recover nearly all of what a corrupted instruction costs. The observed residual is 38.0 pp, well above the band; its one-sided upper 95% bound, 51.2 pp, shows how large it could plausibly be. The prediction we wrote before the run is falsified, and we report that as the pre-registration doing its work. Without the band, a rescue of 47.8 pp at the maximum attainable test statistic would have read as evidence that the reasoning chain carries nearly all of the grounding. The residual says otherwise. The rescue is partial: the correct reasoning chain recovers more than half of C’s 85.8 pp loss. The remaining 38.0 pp cannot be attributed to the instruction alone: it can also contain a cost of holding two conflicting texts in one context, and any effect of the injection pathway (Appendix B). The same pathway separates D from C, so it can also shift the size of the rescue, though not its direction (Appendix A). Every later claim in this paper uses the partial reading.

4.2 What Corrupting the Reasoning Chain Costs, and How the Two Corruptions Compare
The observed cost. What does the same entity swap cost when it edits the reasoning chain instead of the instruction? With the instruction intact, entity-swapping the reasoning chain at every query (B) lowers success from A’s 97.3% to 76.5%, a degradation of 20.8 pp. B is an observed condition, measured before this design was written, and we attach no test to it. Because success is scored only on the commanded goal, this cost does not show what the failing episodes did instead. We inspected five rendered B episodes of a single task at a single seed (Appendix D): three succeed, and in both failures the policy acts on the object the swap names, as in Figure 1(c). Redirection therefore happens, but these episodes cannot say how much of the aggregate cost it accounts for. Read together with §4.1, the two directions are one property of the channel. The reasoning chain that can repair a corrupted instruction can also, when corrupted itself, pull behaviour away from a correct one.
Comparing the two corruptions. When the two channels are corrupted one at a time, which corruption costs the policy more? Corrupting the instruction with the reasoning chain kept correct (D) costs 38.0 pp; corrupting the reasoning chain with the instruction kept correct (B) costs 20.8 pp. The difference, D minus B, is -17.2 pp (95% CI [-30.7, -2.5], paired d = −0.72, n = 10): under these two protocols, corrupting the instruction is 1.82× as damaging on this suite. That difference carries no test. A power analysis on the pre-existing conditions, conducted before the confirmatory condition ran, showed an achieved power of only 0.39 at n = 10 tasks. At that power a null says little and a rejection tends to overstate the effect, so we demoted the comparison from a pre-registered test to a description. No p-value for this comparison appears anywhere in the paper. The comparison is also

4.3 Scope of the Confirmatory Data
Two properties of the data bound what the numbers above can and cannot mean. The first is scope: the confirmatory data are LIBERO-Goal, a scope fixed by a pre-registered gate rule rather than chosen on results.1 Before this design, B had been measured across all four LIBERO suites in the original environment: degradations of 19.3 pp on Goal, 6.2 pp on Spatial, 4.7 pp on Long, and 3.2 pp on Object, observed and untested. That pattern is consistent with the structural reading of §3.3: the cost of corrupting the reasoning chain concentrates where language alone determines the goal. The Goal cell re-measured in the reconstructed environment is 20.8 pp. The small difference is the environment reconstruction quantified in Appendix H, and we report the two figures side by side rather than reconciling them. The second property we rely on is the determinism of the evaluation pipeline itself. Repeated runs of the full evaluation reproduce byte-identically (Appendix H), so per-task success rates carry no run-to-run sampling error. Repeating an episode therefore adds nothing, though new initial states would sharpen each task’s own rate; the intervals we report resample tasks, and n = 10 tasks is the correct unit of analysis.
5 Discussion
Deployment. Deployments rely on text-side mechanisms that operate on the instruction channel: instruction filtering, command confirmation, refusal behaviour, and human approval. Our data show why that coverage matters: corrupting the instruction, with the reasoning chain regenerated from it, costs 85.8 pp (§4.1). However, it is also incomplete, because the policy reads a second text input: the reasoning chain. The chain is exposed precisely so that a person can read and correct it, and none of those mechanisms reads it. That exposure carries both directions of our result at once: it is what makes the 47.8 pp rescue of §4.1 possible, and what makes the 20.8 pp cost of §4.2 possible. Whether a policy should emit its reasoning chain is therefore a deployment decision, and part of the tradeoff behind it can now be measured. Readability, authorized editing and adversarial write access are distinct properties of a deployment; our measurements assume write access and do not establish which deployments grant it.
Generality. Both directions were measured on DeepThinkVLA, a manipulation policy. The cost direction, though, is also observed in CoTinyVLA [23], another VLA policy that conditions its actions on an episode-level plan. Corrupting or emptying that plan lowers CoTinyVLA’s success rate. The rescue direction has a precedent in ECoT’s correction of its policy’s own errors (§2), but against a corrupted instruction held fixed it has, to our knowledge, not been measured before. The reasoning chain itself is not confined to manipulation: Alpamayo-R1 [24], a driving policy, decodes a text reasoning chain at inference and conditions its trajectory decoder on it. Any policy whose actions condition on an editable text intermediate faces the same question: does the reasoning chain move the action? Here, it did, though the magnitudes are specific to DeepThinkVLA.
Broader impact. Three statements bound whatever risk the measurements in this paper could create. First, access: editing the reasoning chain requires an internal interface between its generation and action decoding, so it presumes control of part of the serving stack. It requires neither physical access to the workspace nor any perturbation of the camera image. Second, the reading we consider actionable is defensive: the channel exposed for human oversight is currently the channel the text-side mechanisms do not check. Third, scope: this paper measures how far the reasoning chain moves the action, in both directions. Attack optimization, adaptive adversaries, and detection or defence are downstream of that measurement and absent here. Code and data will be released with the archival version of this work. The editing requires a reasoning chain rendered as text at inference and replaceable before decoding. DeepThinkVLA meets that requirement, and serves here as the instrument that makes the channel measurable, not as a sample of vision-language-action models. The confirmatory data are LIBERO-Goal, a scope fixed by the pre-registered gate rule of §4.3 rather than chosen on results; the claims hold in the regime where language alone determines the goal (§3.3). The evidence is simulation: LIBERO’s success checks are programmatic, and whether the magnitudes transfer to physical perception and actuation is unmeasured. Our entity swap is mechanical: it is a seeded, fixed edit that does not adapt to the policy, and we make no claim about optimizing adversaries. Finally, n = 10 tasks is a between-task bound, and only large effects are testable at this n. The rescue clears that bar (d = 1.78); the asymmetry does not (power 0.39, reported descriptively). Because the evaluation is deterministic, repeated episodes would not tighten these intervals; more tasks would. Beyond their precision, three inexpensive controls would sharpen what these intervals measure: an empty chain against the corrupted instruction, scoring the swapped goal, and re-injecting the policy’s own chain over whole episodes (Appendix B). Appendix H restates each of these limitations in granular form and sets the full environment, determinism, and integrity record beside them.
7 Conclusion
In this work we isolated the two text channels of a reasoning vision-language-action policy, the instruction and the reasoning chain. We then edited each channel in turn with the same entity swap. On LIBERO-Goal, a counterfactually correct reasoning chain, injected in place of the one the policy generates from the corrupted instruction, recovers 47.8 pp of the lost success, our pre-registered confirmatory test. The same edit applied to the reasoning chain instead costs 20.8 pp against a correct instruction. Nonetheless, policies of this kind emit a reasoning chain so that a person can read and correct it, and the correction and the corruption are one property of that exposure. Whether to expose the reasoning chain is therefore a deployment decision. In practice the choice is currently settled implicitly, by the selection of an architecture, and part of the tradeoff it commits to can now be measured: how far the chain moves the actions once someone can write to it.

A The Injection Pathway
Conditions B and D supply the reasoning chain the actions are decoded from, and both do so through the same injection pathway. Conditions A and C use the policy’s native generation, in which a single call generates the chain and then decodes the action chunk. The injection pathway makes two separate calls at each query. The first is a native call on the image and the clean instruction. Its chain is kept, and entity-swapped under B, while its actions are discarded. The second builds a fresh input from the image, the instruction the condition receives (intact under B, corrupted under D) and the kept chain, wrapped in the tokens that open and close a reasoning chain and open the action span, and decodes the action chunk from that input in a single forward pass. No state is carried from the first call to the second.
Doubled delimiters. The kept chain is the decoded text of the first call, and that text retains the chain’s own <think> and </think> delimiters. The tokenizer maps them back to the same delimiter tokens the pathway adds, so the injected input carries each delimiter twice where the native input carries it once:
native: . . . <think> chain </think> <action> injected: . . . <think> <think> chain </think> </think> <action>
At the first query of LIBERO-Goal task 0, checked after the experiments, re-encoding the decoded chain reproduced the policy’s own chain tokens exactly, so there the doubled pair is the only difference between the two inputs. Other states were not checked. B and D carry the doubled delimiters, and A and C do not. D minus B therefore compares two injected inputs, while the rescue (D minus C), the residual (clean minus D) and the cost of a corrupted reasoning chain (clean minus B) each compare an injected input with a native one. No condition measures what the doubled delimiters alone do to task success. They carry no information about any goal, so they cannot by themselves move success toward the goal that only the injected chain names. They may still shift the size of each contrast that crosses the pathway.
The single-state check. The check reported in §3.2 compares the two pathways directly. At the first query of LIBERO-Object task 0 from initial state 0, the native call’s first action was compared with the first action decoded after re-injecting that call’s own chain, doubled delimiters included. They differ by at most 0.024, against a largest translation component of 0.092 in the native action. The check covers one action at one state and does not measure task success.
B Readings the Design Leaves Open
The design fixes the direction of the rescue: once the instruction is corrupted, the injected chain is the only input that names the goal success is scored on (§3.2). The sizes of the effects admit more than one reading, and each reading below comes with the further cell or re-scoring that would separate it.
Supplying versus removing. The rescue replaces the chain the policy regenerates from the corrupted instruction with the correct one, so it combines removing a wrong chain with supplying a correct one. A corrupted instruction paired with an empty chain would separate the two.
Redirection versus other failures. The cost of a corrupted chain is scored on the intended goal only, so it does not distinguish pursuit of the swapped object from other failures. Scoring the swapped goal, where the scene can satisfy it, would.
The injection pathway. B and D reach the actions through the injection pathway of Appendix A, and A and C do not. Re-injecting the policy’s own chain over whole episodes would measure that pathway’s effect on success.
The residual. The residual can hold both the conflicting instruction’s own influence and a cost of holding two conflicting texts in one context. An instruction perturbed without contradicting the chain, by synonym for instance, would separate them.
Achievable goals. Most names in the swap pool are objects the Goal scene lacks, so a corrupted instruction often names a goal the scene cannot satisfy. Whether the size of the rescue depends on this was not tested. Splitting the rescue by whether the replacement exists in the scene would test it. For a corrupted reasoning chain, the five rendered episodes of Appendix D suggest that the same split matters.

Every interval in this paper comes from the same bootstrap procedure over tasks. E Structural Parse of the LIBERO Task Specifications
Table 1 summarizes the four suites. This appendix gives the parse behind that summary, with per-task tables for Goal and Long. Every entry below is read from the ground-truth .bddl task specifications. The specification files of all four suites were verified byte-identical to the public LIBERO distribution before parsing.
Goal. The 10 tasks share one scene, one fixture set, and one object set, and differ only in their goal predicates, listed in Table 4. Because the scene is constant and every goal is available in it, vision cannot determine what to do. Language alone determines the goal, which is the property the design of §3.3 requires.
Spatial. One scene contains two identical black bowls, and all ten goals are the single predicate On(bowl, plate). The tasks differ only in the initial arrangement. The instruction picks out the intended bowl by its spatial relation to landmarks, so language selects which of two identical objects to act on, never what to do.
Object. Ten scenes host one task each, and every goal is the single schema In(x, basket). The action is constant across the suite, and language supplies only the identity of x.
Long. The ten tasks span nine scenes, listed in Table 5. Eight goals are conjunctions of two predicates, one is a conjunction of three, and one is a single containment. They use In, On, and Turnon, as Goal does, and Close where Goal uses Open. Eight of the nine scenes host exactly one task, so the scene largely determines the task. Only LIVING ROOM 2 hosts two tasks, and there language selects which two objects go in the basket. Language therefore carries little of the goal on Long. The cost of corrupting the reasoning chain there is correspondingly small, 4.7 pp in the forty-task backdrop, observed and untested.
F The Entity Swap: Specification and Six Alternatives
Specification. The pool holds the object names of all four LIBERO suites: alphabet soup, cream cheese box, cream cheese, salad dressing, bbq sauce, ketchup, tomato sauce, butter, milk, chocolate pudding, orange juice, black bowl, wine bottle, moka pot, white mug, yellow and white mug, cookie box, ramekin, wooden cabinet, microwave, basket, caddy, book, plate, bowl, stove, cabinet, rack, and drawer. A text is matched against the pool case-insensitively, longest name first, without word boundaries. A shorter match inside a longer one is discarded, so cream cheese box is never read as cream cheese. Each distinct name found is mapped to a replacement drawn uniformly from the rest of the pool by a generator seeded for the condition. Every occurrence of that name is replaced, and the surrounding text is untouched. Under B, the seed is the episode seed, which is the run seed plus the episode index. The swap is applied afresh to the chain at every query, so a name keeps the same replacement only while the regenerated chain mentions the same objects in the same order. Under C and D, the seed is the run seed, and the swap is applied once per task to the instruction. D’s corrupted instruction is checked byte-identical to C’s before D runs.

Six alternatives. Section 3.2 argues that no other fixed-rule corruption meets the design’s two requirements: the edit must change which object a text names, and either channel must be able to carry it. The rest of this appendix shows the empirical side of that argument. Before this design existed, we had measured seven corruptions of the reasoning chain on the forty tasks of the four LIBERO suites, observed in the original environment. Figure 5 reports six of them as a matrix. The seventh, graded token replacement, applies the random-token corruption at increasing fractions rather than as one condition, so it has no single cell. The entity-swap row is the forty-task backdrop of §4.3, and its Goal cell is the 19.3 pp figure reported there.
Each of the other six fails one of the two requirements. Shuffled reorders sentences, and a onesentence instruction offers no order to permute. Negation flip reverses direction words rather than the named object. Random tokens destroys a one-sentence instruction outright rather than changing the object it names. Padding appends inert text and touches no object name. Graded token replacement inherits the random-token defect at every fraction. The LLM-adversarial rewrite, generated by a large language model prompted to produce a plausible but wrong reasoning chain, is neither deterministic nor identically applicable to both channels.
G The Pre-Registered Design
This appendix reproduces the design under which the confirmatory condition ran. The design was written and last amended before condition D was run, and no part of it was written after any confirmatory data existed. The document is append-only. So amendments are dated in place rather than overwritten, and what follows is the state that was in force when the run started.
Registered hypothesis. The registered hypothesis held that the reasoning chain, not the instruction, is the channel that grounds the target object. It carried two testable consequences. The first is that a correct reasoning chain injected against a corrupted instruction should recover nearly all of the loss. The second is that corrupting the instruction, with the reasoning chain kept correct, should cost less than the reverse. Section 4.1 and §4.2 report how each fared.
Condition status. A (both texts intact), B (intact instruction, corrupted reasoning chain), and C (corrupted instruction, corrupted reasoning chain) predate this design, so they are observed conditions and are labelled that way wherever they appear. D (corrupted instruction, clean reasoning chain injected) is the pre-registered confirmatory condition. A post-hoc pattern in the observed conditions is never presented as pre-registered anywhere in this paper.

The registered band. The design registered a bound rather than a second test. That bound is the one-sided upper 95% confidence bound on the residual, clean minus D, read against a predicted band of 5–15 pp. The interpretation of every region was fixed before the run. A residual below the band would mean the reasoning chain nearly fully mediates grounding. A residual inside the band would mean mediation is partial, which was the predicted outcome. A residual comparable to the observed cost of corrupting the reasoning chain would weaken the claim toward co-equal channels, and a residual at or beyond that cost would falsify the registered hypothesis. The observed residual of 38.0 pp lies in the falsifying region, and §4.1 reports the prediction as falsified.
Falsification criteria. The design registered one further criterion, which reads the confirmatory test’s own null rather than a new comparison. D indistinguishable from C on Goal would mean the injected reasoning chain contributes nothing, and that is the falsification of the causal claim. It did not fire. The two registered instruments therefore returned different verdicts, and the paper reports both. The band falsifies its prediction, that the reasoning chain carries the grounding almost on its own. The test rejects its null, that the reasoning chain carries none. Every outcome pattern, including the null, carried an interpretation written before the run, so no result could arrive without a pre-committed reading.
The demoted comparison. The second registered consequence, that D would degrade less than B, was assessed for power before D existed, using only the pre-existing conditions, and the achieved power at n = 10 is 0.39. We therefore demoted this comparison to a descriptive result before the confirmatory run. The pre-committed reporting rule forbids attaching a significance test to it in either direction, because selecting a tail after observing the sign doubles the true error rate. Section 4.2 reports the comparison under that rule.
The withdrawn confound analysis. The design also registered a confound analysis, and withdrew it before any correlation was computed. The plan was a rank correlation, across the forty tasks of the four suites, between the per-task effect and a visual-determinacy measure: the number of scene objects sharing the target’s category in the ground-truth task specifications. H Determinism, Environment, and Granular Limitations
Determinism. The evaluation pipeline is byte-identically deterministic under greedy decoding. Four replicates of the full 200-episode clean condition, spanning two GPUs and two hosts, differ pairwise in 0 episodes. Run seeds vary the episode initial states rather than the model, and repeated runs are exact copies. Per-task success rates therefore carry no run-to-run sampling error.
The reconstructed environment. The observed conditions were measured in an earlier campaign, and the confirmatory campaign rebuilds that software environment from the earlier campaign’s committed bootstrap recipe. One difference was recoverable, a minor-version pin of the transformers library, and it was closed by giving the reconstruction its own virtual environment. Rather than assert an equivalence the record cannot support, we measured it.
The replication gate. The gate re-runs the clean condition for 200 episodes at one run seed and compares per-task success against the stored reference. Against that reference the reconstruction differs in 3 of 200 episodes across two tasks, a mean shift of 0.5 pp. The pre-committed rule for exactly this branch, a deterministic pipeline with a real environment difference, forbids comparing conditions across the two environments. That rule is what forced the clean and C re-runs on Goal reported in §4.3, and it is the rule that fixes the scope of the confirmatory data.
The probe at the mid-range operating point. The clean condition sits near ceiling at 97.3%, where environment drift is least able to show. The environment term was therefore re-measured on C, the arm of our pre-registered confirmatory test that sits mid-range. Re-running C for 200 episodes moved the mean by 0.5 pp, with 9 episodes differing across 5 tasks and a rank correlation of 0.932 between the per-task rates. Neither pre-committed materiality criterion, a mean shift of at least 5 pp or a rank correlation below 0.8, was triggered. The per-task differences reach 10 pp and cancel in the mean. Differences of that size are negligible against the rescue of 47.8 pp but not against the asymmetry of -17.2 pp. The corrective re-runs, including B (mean shift 0.8 pp), therefore left all twelve Goal cells, four conditions by three seeds, measured in the reconstructed environment.
Integrity checks. Before analysis, 32 result artifacts pass a content-level integrity script that recomputes every reported aggregate from its per-episode records and verifies full coverage, rather than checking that files exist.
Granular limitations. Section 6 states five limitations, and this appendix gives the record behind each. First, the policy is the instrument rather than a population. The design needs a reasoning chain rendered as plain text at inference and replaceable before the action decode, and DeepThinkVLA was selected under that rule. Both halves of the rule are checked rather than assumed. The chain is plain text between its <think> delimiters. Re-injecting the chain the policy has just generated changes the first action of the chunk by at most 0.024 at the single state checked (Appendix A). No coefficient in this paper is claimed for any other policy. Second, a pre-committed rule sets the scope of the confirmatory data. Because the gate branch above fired, the D cells on the other three suites have no same-environment references, and they are neither reported nor tested. The forty-task backdrop of §4.3 remains observed, original-environment data. Third, the evidence is simulation. Success is
18

the task specification’s goal predicate evaluating true over the simulator state, and those predicates are printed in Table 4 and Table 5. The measurement is therefore exact and mechanical, with no human rater and no perception in the loop. For the same reason, the success rates carry no rater noise and say nothing about a physical system. Fourth, the swap is fixed and seeded, and nothing here measures an optimizing adversary. The swap is a deterministic function of the text and the seed and reads no output of the policy, so no quantity the policy produces can enter the corruption. It was also never searched against this policy. It was chosen from seven corruptions measured before this design existed, on the criteria stated in §3.2 and evidenced in Appendix F. The reasoning chains it edits are the policy’s own, generated live at each query. Fifth, n = 10 tasks bounds what is testable. The exact test rejects only at W + ≥ 45, so only large effects are testable at this n. The rescue (d = 1.78) clears that bar, and the asymmetry, at power 0.39, does not. Because the pipeline is deterministic, repeating an episode adds no information. New initial states would sharpen each task’s rate, and additional tasks would narrow the intervals.
I Compute and Superseded Runs
The study ran on NVIDIA A100-40GB GPUs, and the figures below are single-GPU hours. Planning used 133 seconds per episode, an upper bound measured on the heaviest condition of the earlier campaign. Measured clean throughput in this campaign ran near 57 seconds per episode. The preregistered budget was 96 GPU-hours: condition D at 2400 episodes, approximately 89 GPU-hours at the planning rate, plus the first 200-episode replication gate at approximately 7.4. Verification and correction added runs that we disclose rather than absorb silently: three further gate replicates of 200 episodes each, the C probe of 200 episodes at approximately 3 GPU-hours, the corrective re-run of clean and C on Goal after the gate branch fired, four cells of 200 episodes each at approximately 14 GPU-hours, and the B re-run of 600 episodes at approximately 4 GPU-hours. Summing every run at its recorded per-run figure puts the study at approximately 140 GPU-hours, the total the body reports.
Three superseded or preliminary items belong in this record. First, the initial confirmatory analysis compared D against the stored C from the original environment, which the pre-committed gate rule forbids. That analysis was caught and retired, and every confirmatory number in this paper now comes from the twelve same-environment Goal cells. The headline barely moved, which is not a reason the correction could have been skipped. Second, D was collected on all four suites, 2400 episodes in total. The Spatial, Long, and Object cells lack same-environment references and are not reported as results anywhere in this paper. Third, a pre-registered confound analysis was withdrawn before any correlation was computed (Appendix G) and consumed no compute. The original-environment backdrop of §4.3 and the corruption matrix of Appendix F predate this design and were produced in an earlier campaign whose compute is not counted above.
