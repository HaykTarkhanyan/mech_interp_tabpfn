1. The paper in one minute

The question. How do you score a feature-attribution method (SHAP, LIME and friends) when, on real data, nobody knows the correct attribution and the metrics that need conditional distributions cannot be computed?

The answers.

  1. Use synthetic data whose distribution you know. With Gaussian, Gaussian-mixture or multinomial features, every conditional distribution is known in closed form, so metrics like "distance to the true Shapley values" become computable. XAI-BENCH packages such datasets, five metrics and eight explainers as a library.
  2. Which explainer is best depends on the metric. On one setting, SHAPR wins three of five metrics, MAPLE wins infidelity, and breakDown is best on monotonicity while worst on ground-truth Shapley (Table 1).
  3. Correlation hurts. Explainers become less faithful as feature correlation rises; LIME's faithfulness drops by as much as about 90% when features are almost perfectly correlated (Fig. 2).
  4. Real datasets can be "converted". Fit a Gaussian to a real dataset's covariance and label the samples with a nearest-neighbour model of the real target (Section 5.3, wine and forest fires).
My take, one line. This is the closest prior work to your pitch, and Section 5.3 is the precedent you generalize. It is a useful library with thin real-data simulation, a scale-free headline metric, and one undiscussed result in its own Table 1: two of its four proxy metrics barely agree with its own ground truth.

2. Background you need first

Feature attribution. An explainer takes a trained model f and one input x and returns one number per feature: how much that feature pushed this prediction. SHAP, LIME, MAPLE, L2X and breakDown are different ways to compute such numbers.

Shapley values need "remove a feature". The Shapley value of feature i averages how much the prediction changes when i joins every possible subset S of the other features. That needs a value for "the model's output when only the features in S are known". The standard answer is an expectation, and there are two:

  • Observational (conditional): E[f(X) | X_S = x_S]. Fill in the unknown features by their conditional distribution given the known ones. "True to the data": importance spreads to correlated features, even unused ones.
  • Interventional: fill in the unknown features from their marginal distribution, as if independent. "True to the model": unused features get zero.

Both are legitimate; they answer different questions (the demo in Section 6 shows the difference). On real data the conditional version cannot be computed exactly, because nobody knows the conditional distributions. That is the paper's opening.

Schematic of Fig. 1. The blue box is what makes the orange box computable.

The five metrics (Section 3.2, Appendix E). Faithfulness: correlation between each feature's weight and the change in prediction when that feature is replaced by its conditional expectation. Monotonicity: do features, added in order of weight, add diminishing amounts? ROAR: retrain the model with the top features removed and see how fast it degrades. Infidelity: mismatch between the output change and the weights under noisy perturbations (lower is better). GT-Shapley: the Pearson correlation between the explainer's weights and the true Shapley values of the trained model. Only GT-Shapley compares against a ground truth; the other four are proxies.

3. Finding 1: explainer rankings depend on the metric

What they did. Train a multilayer perceptron on Gaussian features with pairwise correlation 0.5 and a nonlinear additive label (sin, absolute value, square, exponential of single features). Explain it with seven methods, score with five metrics (Table 1).

Faithfulness and ROAR agree well. How sure: high that the numbers are right; low that the pattern generalizes.

4. Finding 2: correlation between features breaks explainers

What they did. Vary the pairwise feature correlation ρ from 0 to 0.99 (all off-diagonal covariance entries equal to ρ), for linear, nonlinear-additive and piecewise-constant labels and for three model types, and track faithfulness (Figs. 2, 3; all metrics in Appendix G, Figs. 6-10).

What they found. "A general trend is that explainers become less faithful as feature correlation increases." At ρ = 0 on the linear dataset, most methods sit above 0.9. LIME's faithfulness drops by as much as about 90% at ρ = 0.99. MAPLE stays relatively stable on the nonlinear-additive and piecewise-constant datasets. Most explainers do well for linear regression up to ρ = 0.75, and MAPLE does markedly worse on the decision tree (Fig. 3).

My take. These curves are the evidence your review uses for "dependence is the main driver of explainer failure". Note what kind of dependence it is: a single number ρ shared by every pair of Gaussian features. Real tables have uneven, nonlinear and mixed-type dependence; how explainers behave under that is what the curves cannot tell you, and what a data-fitted generator could. How sure: high.

5. Finding 3: simulating real datasets (Section 5.3)

What they did. Take the wine-quality data (4,898 rows, 11 continuous features; the size matches the white-wine set) and the forest-fire data (12 features). Standardize the features, compute the empirical covariance, and sample multivariate Gaussian features with that covariance. Label each synthetic point with a k-nearest-neighbour model fitted to the real (x, y) pairs.

How they checked fidelity.

  • Mean Jensen-Shannon divergence of the feature marginals, real versus synthetic: 0.20 (wine), 0.17 (forest fires). Of the targets: 0.23 and 0.15. The paper calls these "a good fit".
  • Train models on real and on simulated wine, explain both on the same held-out real test set, and compare the explanations by MSE (Table 5): SHAP 0.028 / 0.047 / 0.028 (linear / tree / MLP), LIME 0.047 / 0.009 / 0.037, MAPLE 0.027 / 0.052 / 0.040, L2X about 0.0008, Random 1.988.

What they found on the simulated wine data (decision tree, Table 2): GT-Shapley SHAPR 0.945, SHAP 0.825, LIME 0.745, MAPLE 0.685; faithfulness SHAP 0.534 and SHAPR 0.528 ahead of LIME 0.368 and MAPLE 0.034. On forest fires (Table 3), SHAP is best on three of five metrics; SHAPR is not reported there.

My take. Three things limit this as a precedent. The Gaussian keeps only linear correlation, so skewed wine features (residual sugar, chlorides) lose their shape, which a marginal JSD of 0.20 reflects. The kNN target is a black box, so the data-level "true explanation" is not readable, only the model-level Shapley values. And Table 5 compares explanation MSE across explainers whose weights live on different scales: L2X's weights sum to 1 by construction (Eq. 15) and RANDOM draws standard normals, so "low MSE" partly measures scale. How sure: high.

6. Try it: two "true" Shapley values

Exact computation, not made up: a linear model f(x) = 2·x1 + 1·x2 + 0·x3 on three standard Gaussian features with equal pairwise correlation ρ, explained at the point x = (1.0, -0.5, 1.2). Interventional Shapley values are w_i·x_i. Conditional (observational) Shapley values use E[X_k | X_j] = ρ·x_j and E[X_k | X_j, X_l] = ρ/(1+ρ)·(x_j + x_l), the Gaussian formulas behind the paper's Eq. 5.

7. My critique

What's good. It made ground-truth Shapley evaluation practical and released it as a maintained, documented library with five metrics and eight explainers. It states its own central trade-off honestly ("there is a trade-off between data realism and ground truth availability") and does not oversell the synthetic data as a replacement for human studies. The correlation sweeps are a clean controlled experiment.

8. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

It is the precedent the pitch must cite and beat. Your review (Section 3.4 and implication 1) positions the pitch as a generalization of Section 5.3. Concretely, the pitch adds: a learned generator with realistic marginals and nonlinear dependence (ARF) instead of a Gaussian; a readable fitted target (symbolic regression) instead of kNN, so data-level ground truth exists; targets with stated numerical error instead of "exact"; fidelity beyond marginal JSD; and many datasets instead of two.

It is also a ready-made baseline arm. The realism question your review flags as unmeasured ("whether realistic data changes explainer rankings at all") can be asked directly: run the same explainers on (a) XAI-BENCH's Gaussian conversion of a dataset, (b) an ARF fitted to the same dataset, and compare rankings. If they agree, realism adds little; if they differ, which one is right is the question a known-truth test (see the Advani explainer) answers.

Metric lesson. Score explainers by error magnitude against the target (for example a normalized L2 or absolute error), not only by correlation, so scale errors count. Keep GT-Shapley-style correlation as a secondary view.

The meta-evaluation seed. The Spearman numbers in Finding 1 are a seven-explainer preview of the review's implication 9 ("measure which proxy metrics track actual explanation error"). A full version, many datasets and settings, is a strong candidate experiment.

9. Glossary
Feature attributionOne importance number per feature for one prediction.
Shapley valueA feature's average marginal contribution over all subsets of the other features.
Observational vs interventionalTwo ways to fill in "unknown" features: from their conditional distribution given the known ones, or from their marginal distribution as if independent.
Kernel SHAP, BF-SHAPThe SHAP library's sampling approximation, and a brute-force re-implementation; both assume independent features.
SHAPRAas et al.'s Shapley estimators for dependent features; here the nonparametric "empirical" variant.
LIME, MAPLE, L2X, breakDownA local linear surrogate, a local linear model with tree-based neighbourhoods, a learned feature selector, and a greedy sequential decomposition.
Faithfulness, monotonicity, infidelity, ROARProxy metrics that compare weights with how the model's output changes when features are removed or perturbed; ROAR retrains the model.
GT-ShapleyPearson correlation between an explainer's weights and the true Shapley values.
Jensen-Shannon divergenceA symmetric, bounded (0 to 1) distance between two distributions.
Spearman correlationPearson correlation computed on ranks; 1 means identical orderings.

10. How to read the actual paper
  1. Pages 1-2 and 4-6: introduction, metrics (Section 3) and why synthetic data (Section 4.1). Read in full.
  2. Page 7: Table 1 and the paragraph under it.
  3. Page 8: Figures 2 and 3 and Section 5.3, the part your pitch extends.
  4. Page 9: Tables 2-3 and Section 5.4.
  5. Appendix E.2 (page 18) to know what each explainer actually is (SHAPR in particular); Table 5 (page 21).
