1. The paper in one minute

The question. Most XAI evaluation checks whether an explanation reproduces the model's predictions, which says little about whether it reflects how the model decides. Intervention-based synthetic ground truth solves this for colour images (FunnyBirds, Hesse et al. 2023). Can the same idea work for binary images, time series and tabular data?

The answers.

  1. Ground truth from the model. A component's importance is the change in the model's output when that component is removed by an intervention that stays inside the training distribution: φj = f(x) - f(x \ cj) (Eqs. 1, 5).
  2. Three instantiations. Binary FunnyBirds silhouettes (47 classes; remove a body part). Time series traced from the silhouette's outline (distance to a fixed centroid, up to 1,251 steps; remove the part in the image, then re-trace). Tabular data: three features on a 100-level grid, where the intervention lowers one feature by one level and the label is a thresholded sum of sines (Eqs. 4-5, 9).
  3. Results. Images: gradient methods top SIM at about 0.38, LIME and KernelSHAP top AUCborji. Time series: RISE best on every metric (SIM 0.522). Tabular: Gradient "near-perfect" (SIM 0.980), LORE second (0.784), LIME last (0.560) (Tables 5-7).
  4. Conclusion. No method is best on all data types, in line with the "No Free Lunch Theorem for explanations" (Section 7).
My take, one line. Model-level, intervention-based ground truth is the right kind, and the time-series construction is clever. But the tabular "first faithful local ground truth" is a one-step finite difference, essentially the gradient, so Gradient's near-perfect score is built in; the tabular task is three features with a deterministic label and repeated rows; and several tables have impossible or contradictory entries. Its future-work sentence describes your project.

2. Background you need first

SAIG. "Synthetic Artificial Intelligence Ground truth": datasets built so that the importance of each input component is known by design (a term from the first author's 2026 review, ref. [37]).

Two kinds of ground truth (Section 3). The paper distinguishes the data-generating process (which features truly drive the label) from the model's behaviour (which features the trained model uses), and targets the second, "even if the model relies on spurious or undesirable correlations". This is the same distinction OpenXAI makes (see that explainer).

Intervention-based importance. Remove a component in a realistic way and measure the output change. In FunnyBirds, a body part is replaced by the background it covered, and the training data already contain birds with parts removed, so the intervened images are not out of distribution (Section 4).

Sections 4.1-4.3. The tabular data are not derived from the birds; they use the ssin-2c function of Cortez and Embrechts (2013).

The three scores (Section 5.1), taken from the saliency literature. SIM is the overlap of two normalized importance maps, Σ min(gi, φi), from 0 to 1. KLdiv is the information lost when the explanation replaces the ground truth (lower is better). AUCborji asks whether the explanation ranks important locations above randomly sampled ones (0.5 is chance); it is not used for tabular data.

3. Finding 1: binary images

What they did. Fine-tune ResNet50 (ImageNet weights, frozen backbone, new 128-unit head) on 47,470 binary silhouettes (precision 0.91, Table 1). Explain with RISE, LIME, Gradient, DeepLIFT, KernelSHAP and Integrated Gradients; LIME and KernelSHAP use Quickshift superpixels (Table 2).

The paper's reading. The gradient methods top SIM but are "significantly below the theoretical optimum" and only marginally better than LIME; on AUCborji all but LIME and KernelSHAP are near chance, because Quickshift keeps their importance off the background.

My take. Four things in this table. (1) SIM between two normalized maps lies between 0 and 1, so KernelSHAP's standard deviation of 1.210 is impossible; the same 1.210 appears in its KLdiv cell, which looks like a copy error. (2) The SIM spread among five methods (0.342 to 0.380) is a tenth of their standard deviations (about 0.4); no test is reported, so "top-performing" is not resolved. (3) KernelSHAP is run with 25 samples on superpixel images, far too few for a sampling estimator, and that is not justified. (4) The head is described as "a single neuron with a Softmax activation" for 47 classes; a softmax over one neuron always outputs 1, so the description must be wrong. How sure: high on (1), (2) and (4); medium on how much (3) matters.

4. Finding 2: time series

What they did. Trace each silhouette's outer contour and record its distance from a centroid fixed per original shape (Eq. 2), giving series up to 1,251 steps; removing a part in the image and re-tracing gives the intervened series. Classify with MultiRocket features and a calibrated ridge classifier (F1 0.63, Table 1). Explain with RISE, LIME, KernelSHAP and T-SHAP (ROI and Window).

What they found (Table 6). RISE is best on all three metrics (SIM 0.522 ± 0.136, AUCborji 0.709, KLdiv 0.790), LIME second (SIM 0.442), T-SHAP intermediate (0.386 and 0.424), KernelSHAP far behind (SIM 0.007, KLdiv 5.315). The authors suggest KernelSHAP "treats each time step as an independent feature" and that its small balanced-centroid background may hurt.

My take. Turning image interventions into time-series interventions through the outline is a neat way to get ground truth where none existed, and the fixed centroid keeps time indices aligned. KernelSHAP's near-zero score, though, mostly reflects running a sampling estimator on 1,251 separate features with a small "balanced centroid" background chosen "to maintain computational feasibility", which the authors half-acknowledge; it is a configuration result, not a property of the method. How sure: medium.

5. Finding 3: tabular data

What they did (Sections 4.3, 5.2). Three features on a grid of K = 100 levels. The label is ssin-2c(x) = [0.5·sin(π/2·x1) + 0.25·sin(π/2·x2) + 0.125·sin(π/2·x3)] > 0.6 (Eq. 9). About 4 million samples, 10,000 held out; an MLP with three 128-unit layers, trained for 5,000 full-batch epochs, reaches precision, recall and F1 of 0.98. The ground truth for feature i is f(x) - f(x with xi one level lower) (Eq. 5), claimed as "the first faithful local ground truth construction for the evaluation of XAI methods in tabular data".

Table 7, SIM (higher is better). KLdiv follows the same order: Gradient 0.092, LORE 1.204, others 1.98 to 3.81.

My take: the winner is built into the reference. A one-level difference is a finite-difference derivative: f(x) - f(x - Δei) ≈ Δ·∂f/∂xi, with the same Δ for every feature. After normalization it equals the normalized absolute gradient up to curvature terms, so Gradient (taken "in absolute value", Section 5.3) matches by construction. Integrated Gradients and DeepLIFT with a zero baseline, and KernelSHAP and LIME with occlusion value 0, all measure what happens when a feature is moved to 0, a large move on a thresholded, nonlinear function. Table 7 ranks perturbation sizes, not explanation quality. The demo below shows the same ranking flipping when only the reference's step changes.

My take: the data and the text.
  • Repeated rows. 100 levels per feature allow at most 100³ = 1,000,000 distinct inputs (1,030,301 if levels run 0 to 100), but there are about 4 million samples, so at least 3 million are repeats, and a random test point almost surely appears in training. The 0.98 is not a held-out score. (The repeats are certain; how the test points were drawn is not stated.)
  • Scale. Eq. 4 defines levels as integers 0 to K; plugged into sin(π/2·x) they give only the values 0, 1, 0, -1. The features must be rescaled to [0, 1] first; the paper never says so.
  • Boundary. At level 0, "one level lower" leaves the domain; not addressed.
  • Text versus table. The results paragraph says KernelSHAP "performs slightly worse than the other two methods, which achieve values very close to the optimum", but its KLdiv (2.315) is far from LORE (1.204) and Gradient (0.092), and one sentence breaks off ("Integrated The KLdiv metric ...").
  • "First". XAI-BENCH (2021) already computes per-instance ground-truth Shapley values of the trained model on tabular data, yet the paper lists it (ref. [29]) among methods whose relevance "is determined globally by construction".
How sure: high on all five.

7. My critique

What's good. It commits to model-level ground truth and says why. It takes the in-distribution problem seriously in every modality (augmented images, re-traced outlines, a discrete grid). The time-series construction is original. It uses metrics from the saliency literature rather than inventing new ones, and releases code and trained weights.

8. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

Its future work is your project. Section 7: "extending the proposed framework to more complex and realistic data generation processes, as well as exploring hybrid evaluation strategies that combine synthetic ground truth with real-world validation". Quote it in your motivation; their tabular benchmark is three features with a sine label, which is the gap your data-fitted worlds fill.

Same principle, different estimand. They define truth by the trained model's response to an intervention, as your pilot does with err_model. Their intervention is one feature moved one step; yours is interventional SHAP, an average over coalitions against a 1,000-row background (DECISIONS #24). Their Table 7 is the clearest demonstration in the set that the estimand decides the ranking, so cite it when you defend yours, and say that methods targeting other quantities are scored as estimators of interventional SHAP.

The grid is a coincidence worth one sentence. They discretize to keep interventions in distribution; you snap values to a 2^-12 grid so that TreeSHAP's float32 thresholds are exact (DECISIONS #24). Different reasons, but a reviewer who knows this paper may ask, so make the reason explicit.

Your review already cites it (mironicolau2026a, under "compute the model-level target exactly") as intervention-based ground truth. That description holds; if the review is revised, add that the tabular version is a finite-difference reference that favours gradients.

9. Glossary
SAIGSynthetic Artificial Intelligence Ground truth: data built so component importance is known by design.
InterventionRemoving or changing a component in a way that keeps the input realistic, then reading the output change.
FunnyBirdsRendered birds built from parts (beak, eye, feet, tail, wings, body) whose combination defines the class.
SIMOverlap of two normalized importance maps, the sum of element-wise minima; 1 means identical.
KL divergenceInformation lost when one distribution stands in for another; 0 means identical.
AUCborjiArea under the curve for separating important locations from randomly sampled ones; 0.5 is chance.
RISEImportance from the model's response to many random masks.
LORELocal rule-based explanations from a decision tree fitted to a synthetic neighbourhood.
DeepLIFTBackpropagates differences from a reference input instead of gradients.
T-SHAPShapley values over windows or regions of a time series.
MultiRocketRandom convolution features for time series, usually followed by a linear classifier.
Finite difference(f(x) - f(x - Δ)) / Δ, the slope over a small step; it approaches the derivative as Δ shrinks.

10. How to read the actual paper
  1. Pages 1-4: motivation, related work and the two notions of ground truth (Section 3).
  2. Pages 4-6: the method for each data type (Sections 4.1-4.3, Eqs. 1-5).
  3. Pages 7-8: metrics and models, including Eq. 9 and the tabular data size.
  4. Pages 11-12: Tables 5-7 and the results text.
  5. Pages 17-19 (after the references): Figures 1-6; Fig. 1 and Fig. 4 explain the interventions best.
  6. Skip on a first read: the hyperparameter Tables 2-4.
