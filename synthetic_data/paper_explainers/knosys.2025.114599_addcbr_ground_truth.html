1. The paper in one minute

The question. How can you evaluate feature-attribution methods (SHAP, LIME) on a real regression problem, here predicting flight take-off delays, when the data is proprietary and there is no ground-truth attribution?

The answers.

  1. Make synthetic data from cluster seeds. Cluster the real data, take an equal number of seed rows per cluster, add zero-mean Gaussian noise to the continuous features, keep the categorical ones, and draw each target at random within its cluster's range. Feature distributions match the original with an average Jensen-Shannon divergence of about 0.10 (Section 3, Fig. 4).
  2. Use additive case-based reasoning (AddCBR) as the reference. A 3-nearest-neighbour regressor, weighted by the XGBoost model's feature importances and rescaled so contributions add up to the prediction, serves as the "baseline" attribution (Section 4).
  3. Measure stability with CQV. The quartile coefficient of variation compares how much attributions vary with how much feature values vary (Section 5.3).
  4. Verdict: SHAP agrees with AddCBR better than LIME on ranking, variability and feature impact (Tables 6-7, Fig. 8).
My take, one line. Important to know as the second "real-data-derived" precedent for your pitch, but weak as ground truth: inside each cluster the synthetic target has no relationship to the features, the reference attribution comes from a model rather than a known function, the new stability metric is ill-defined for numbers that can be negative, and the text and figures disagree in several places.

2. Background you need first

Additive feature attribution. SHAP and LIME split a prediction into per-feature contributions plus a base value: prediction = φ0 + φ1 + ... + φM (Eq. 1). The paper evaluates such splits for a regression model, where keeping contributions on the original scale ("conservation") matters.

Case-based reasoning (CBR). Predict for a new case from the most similar past cases. Similarity is a weighted Euclidean distance; the global feature weights make the method interpretable.

3. Finding 1: synthetic data from cluster seeds

What they did. EUROCONTROL flight data (May to October 2019, 7,613,584 rows, 42 features, target: take-off delay), cleaned of missing values and outliers. Density-based clustering with dynamic time warping. Standard rules for the number of clusters (Elbow, Calinski-Harabasz, Jump) disagreed, so they chose 6 clusters because the cluster-average delays were most clearly separated (Fig. 2); 2 and 8 clusters are sensitivity analyses in the supplement. Per cluster, 300 seed rows, each perturbed 100 times: continuous features get zero-mean Gaussian noise, categorical features are copied, and the target is drawn at random within the cluster's original range. Result: 180,000 rows, 144,000 for training (Table 2).

Fidelity. Jensen-Shannon divergence between real and synthetic distributions, per continuous feature and per cluster (Fig. 4). The text reports a range of 0.05 to 0.24 and an average of 0.102 ± 0.05, and compares favourably with XAI-BENCH's 0.20.

My take. Three things do not hold up. (1) Fig. 4 shows feature a33 in cluster 6 at about 0.39 (approx., read at 300 dpi), above the stated maximum of 0.24; the text itself notes "the exception of a33". (2) The average is given as 0.102 in one sentence and 0.105 in the next. (3) The XAI-BENCH comparison sets per-cluster JSD on one dataset against whole-dataset JSD on different datasets. Also, Fig. 3 shows the synthetic targets as near-flat blocks per cluster: by construction, the features inside a cluster tell you nothing about the target. How sure: high.

The paper also describes the seeds in two ways: as "the instances closest to the centroids" (Section 3.2) and as "random seed instances" (Section 3.3.1).

4. Finding 2: AddCBR as the reference attribution

What they did. Train XGBoost on the synthetic data (grid search over 288 settings). Build a CBR regressor that predicts the average target of the 3 nearest neighbours, with distance weighted by XGBoost's global feature importances w. Turn it into an additive attribution (Eq. 2, Algorithm 1): with prediction ŷ for row x, set s = ŷ / Σ x_j w_j and give feature j the contribution s · x_j · w_j. The contributions then sum to ŷ.

How they validated it. AddCBR and XGBoost predict almost the same: test MAE 0.167 versus 0.152, largest single difference 1.22 minutes (Table 4), so the authors call them "functionally equivalent". AddCBR's local and global feature rankings agree more than XGBoost's importances do (Fig. 5). Its top-5 features move predictions more than its bottom-5 in every cluster when values are scaled by 2 to 6 times (difference 11.3 percentage points, p = 0.001; Table 5).

My take. This is a reasonable reference model but not a ground truth. Contribution s · x_j · w_j grows with the raw size of feature j's value, whatever that feature's actual effect on this prediction, and it has no baseline term, so it is not the kind of quantity SHAP estimates. "SHAP agrees with AddCBR" therefore measures agreement between two attribution recipes, not correctness. How sure: high.

5. Finding 3: SHAP beats LIME against that reference

Ranking agreement (Table 6). nDCG of each method's feature ranking against AddCBR's, over all test rows: SHAP mean 0.852 (sd 0.038), LIME 0.844 (sd 0.039). Both put the same feature (a34) first; among AddCBR's top 5, SHAP shares all 5 and LIME 3 (Fig. 7).

Variability (Fig. 8). For each feature and cluster, plot the CQV of the feature values against the CQV of the attributions; closeness to the 1:1 line counts as better. Average distance to the line:

Fig. 8 legend values, exact (average distance of the points to the 1:1 line, per cluster). Lower = closer to the reference line. Note cluster 5, where LIME (1.56) is closer than SHAP (2.57), which the text's "SHAP is closer" summary does not mention.

Feature impact (Table 7). Scaling a method's top-5 features moves predictions more than its bottom-5: across all clusters, SHAP 27.0% versus 16.0% (difference 11.0, p = 0.001), LIME 20.8% versus 18.2% (difference 2.6, p = 0.217, not significant).

Conclusion in the paper: SHAP's ranking, attribution and impact are all better than LIME's, consistent with the view that LIME's standardization hurts regression explanations.

My take. The nDCG gap (0.852 versus 0.844) is tiny next to the spread (sd about 0.04). The CQV comparison is the paper's new metric, and it has a basic problem: the axes of Fig. 8 run to 68.5, but for positive numbers CQV = (Q3 - Q1)/(Q3 + Q1) can never exceed 1. Values that large only appear when Q3 + Q1 is near zero, which happens with quantities that are centred around zero, like attributions. The demo below shows it. How sure: high.

7. My critique

What's good. It takes regression explanations seriously (most XAI evaluation is classification), it starts from a real, large, consequential dataset, it states hypotheses before testing them, it tests sensitivity to the number of clusters, and it releases data and code. Using a transparent model as a reference attribution is a sensible idea in itself.

8. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

It is the second real-data precedent in your review (Section 3.4), and the most recent one (2025), so a reviewer of your thesis may well know it. The pitch differs on exactly the points where this paper is weak, and it is worth saying so explicitly:

Islam et al. 2025Your pitch
Feature generatornoisy copies of seed rows, categoricals copiedARF fitted to the whole table, mixed types
Targetrandom within cluster rangeclosed-form formula fitted to the real target
Reference explanationattributions of a surrogate model (AddCBR)estimand-specific targets of the known formula and of each fitted model
Fidelity checkmarginal JSD per featuredistributional, conditional and explanation fidelity
Scale1 dataset, 2 explainersmany datasets, many explainers

Lessons to take. Define the estimand before the metric; avoid ratio-type metrics on signed attributions; and keep the text, tables and figures generated from one results file so numbers cannot drift apart (your repo's "JSON first, report derived" rule is exactly that).

One shared goal worth keeping. Releasing a realistic benchmark without releasing the sensitive data is a real benefit of the pitch too; if you claim it, test it (for example, distance-to-closest-record checks), because ARF samples also stay close to training data.

9. Glossary
Additive feature attributionSplitting a prediction into a base value plus one contribution per feature.
CBRCase-based reasoning: predict from the most similar stored cases.
AddCBRThe paper's additive version of a CBR regressor, used as the reference attribution.
CQVCoefficient of quartile variation, (Q3 - Q1)/(Q3 + Q1): a robust relative spread for positive data.
nDCGNormalized discounted cumulative gain: a 0-to-1 score of how well one ranking matches a reference ranking, weighting the top more.
Jensen-Shannon divergenceA symmetric, bounded (0 to 1) distance between two distributions.
DTWDynamic time warping: a distance between sequences that tolerates shifts in timing.
Density-based clusteringClustering that finds dense regions of arbitrary shape and treats sparse points as noise.
ConservationKeeping a regression explanation on the original output scale, so contributions add up to the prediction.
