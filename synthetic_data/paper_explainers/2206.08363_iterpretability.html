1. The paper in one minute

The question. Treatment-effect models are almost always judged by how accurately they estimate the effect (PEHE). In medicine it matters at least as much whether they find the right drivers of the effect: which patient characteristics make the treatment work better or worse. Do they?

The answers.

  1. A semi-synthetic benchmark. Keep the covariates of a real dataset (TCGA gene expression, Twins births, News word counts, ACIC2016 perinatal data; 39 to 100 features), pick random disjoint sets of "prognostic" and "predictive" covariates, and simulate treatment and outcomes from them (Algorithm 1). The predictive covariates are then known.
  2. A metric. Explain each fitted model's effect estimate with Integrated Gradients and measure Attrpred, the share of the absolute attribution that lands on the true predictive covariates (Eq. 2).
  3. Findings. Stronger predictive effects are easier to attribute; the S-learner is consistently worst; nonlinearity lowers attribution but does not change which learner is best; confounding hurts most when treatment is assigned on the predictive covariates; CFRNet's balancing penalty can destroy attribution (Figs. 2-4).
  4. Attribution and accuracy disagree. The S-learner has the best PEHE when effects are weak but the worst attribution (Section 5.1).
My take, one line. A clean idea, and the closest precedent for "keep real feature rows, simulate the target, know the relevant features". But it ranks treatment-effect learners, not explainers; its ground truth is a feature set, not attribution values; its targets are random functions, not fitted to any real outcome; and the metric has an unstated chance level of about 0.4 that several curves never leave.

2. Background you need first

Treatment effects. Each patient has two potential outcomes, Y(1) if treated and Y(0) if not; only one is ever observed. The conditional average treatment effect is τ(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | X = x]. PEHE is the root mean squared error of an estimate of τ on test data.

Prognostic versus predictive covariates. Prognostic covariates (say, age) shift the outcome whether or not you are treated, so they cancel in τ. Predictive covariates (say, hormone-receptor status in cancer) change how much the treatment helps, so τ depends on them. A good effect model should attribute its estimate of τ to the predictive ones only.

The learners compared (all neural networks from the CATENets package, matched in capacity, Appendix C.1): the S-learner fits one model with the treatment as an extra input; the T-learner fits one model per treatment arm and subtracts; TARNet shares a representation between the two arms; CFRNet adds a penalty that makes that representation look alike across arms; the DR- and X-learners regress directly on pseudo-outcomes built from first-stage estimates.

Schematic of Fig. 1. Only the orange step is synthetic; the explainer (Integrated Gradients) is a fixed measuring instrument, not what is being evaluated.

The data-generating process (Algorithm 1). With nI = floor(0.2·d), draw 3·nI distinct covariates and split them into a prognostic set Iprog and two predictive sets I0, I1. Then Y(0) = μprog(xIprog) + ωpred·μpred0(xI0) and Y(1) = μprog(xIprog) + ωpred·μpred1(xI1), plus Gaussian noise; τ depends only on Ipred = I0 ∪ I1. Data are split 80/20; feature importance is computed for up to 1,000 test points with a zero baseline; results average 30 seeds (Experiments 1-2) or 10 (Experiment 3) (Appendix C.2-C.3).

3. Finding 1: stronger effects are easier to attribute

What they did. Linear prognostic and predictive functions with weights drawn from U(-1, 1); vary the predictive scale ωpred over {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1}; treatment assigned at random (Section 5.1, Fig. 2).

What they found. Attrpred "substantially increase[s]" with ωpred for all learners and datasets, and prognostic misattribution falls. The S-learner "does substantially worse" on Attrpred; the other learners "perform very similar to each other". The S-learner has the best PEHE at low ωpred, which "does not translate into better discoveries", because PEHE rewards any method that shrinks effects toward zero.

Attrpred at ωpred = 1, read off Fig. 2 (approx.): the best learner and the S-learner, against the score of an attribution spread evenly over all covariates (my computation, 2·floor(0.2·d)/d).

My take: the floor is about 0.4, not 0. The paper says Attrpred = 0 "corresponds to a model that does not identify any predictive covariate". But by construction 40% of the covariates are predictive (2·nI of d: 40/100 for TCGA and News, 22/55 for ACIC2016, 14/39 = 0.36 for Twins), so an attribution spread evenly scores 0.40. At ωpred ≤ 0.01 every learner sits at about 0.36 to 0.40 (approx.), which is chance. On ACIC2016 even the best learner reaches only about 0.50 at the strongest effect, ten points above an explainer that knows nothing. The direction of every finding survives, but the effect sizes are much smaller than the 0-to-1 scale suggests. How sure: high on the arithmetic; the plotted values are approx.
Rerun of the released code (2026-10-27). I ran the predictive-sensitivity experiment on Twins with the authors' simulator, the five catenets learners and their attribution score (commit 8121b9f; one seed, 300 training iterations instead of 1,000, 50 explained rows, Integrated Gradients and feature ablation). Two things in the code change the chance level and one metric:
  • run_experiments.py uses 8 important features for Twins and 10 for ACIC, where the paper's rule floor(0.2 d) gives 7 and 11. With the code's value the chance level for Twins is 16/39 = 0.41 (random attributions scored 0.42 here), not the 0.36 computed above from the paper's rule;
  • get_prognostic_features() returns the tuple from np.where, so the prognostic score is a top-1 hit rate, not top-k (for the S-learner at the weakest effect: 0.60 as computed upstream, 0.25 at top-k).
Predictive-feature accuracy with Integrated Gradients: at predictive scale 0.01 every learner scores 0.43 to 0.50, which is chance; at scale 2 the T-learner, TARNet and DR-learner reach 0.64 to 0.65, the X-learner 0.61 and the S-learner 0.49. The noise sd, which the paper does not state, is 0.1 in synthetic_simulate.py. Details: synthetic_data/benchmarks/report.html. How sure: high on the code facts; medium on the accuracies (one seed, shortened training).

4. Finding 2: nonlinearity and the choice of explainer

Nonlinearity (Section 5.2, Fig. 3). Mix a linear and a nonlinear part with weight ωnl ∈ [0, 1], each nonlinear function drawn from ten candidates (|x|, exp(-x²), 1/(1+x²), cos, sin, arctan, tanh, log(1+x²), √(1+x²), cosh; Appendix C.4). As ωnl grows, Attrpred falls for every learner and prognostic confusion rises, but "the relative ordering of methods does not change".

Which explainer (Appendix B). Integrated Gradients is chosen because it satisfies sensitivity, completeness, linearity and implementation invariance (Appendix Table 1) and is cheap (about 50 backward passes per point). Repeating Experiments 1-2 with other explainers (Figs. 5-6): Shapley values (Monte Carlo sampling) are "nearly identical" to Integrated Gradients, feature ablation is "marginally lower", and feature permutation "substantially underperforms"; the ordering of learners is unchanged.

My take. Appendix B.2 is the only place explainers are compared, and it is done against data-level truth: whether attribution lands on the covariates the simulation used. With correlated real covariates (expression levels of genes, like TCGA's 100 most variable ones, are usually strongly correlated; I did not measure it here), an explainer that correctly reports that the model uses a correlated stand-in is scored as wrong, the exact problem OpenXAI warns about (see the OpenXAI explainer). The paper never discusses correlation between covariates; the only mentions of "correlated" are about treatment assignment. How sure: high that it is not discussed; medium on how much it moves the numbers (the demo below shows the mechanism).

5. Finding 3: confounding

What they did (Section 5.3, Fig. 4). Assign treatment with probability sigmoid(ωπ·Zscore[ψ(x)]) for three choices of ψ: the predictive part (doctors treat those who will respond), the prognostic part (those who would do well anyway get treated) or one irrelevant covariate. Add CFRNet with three balancing strengths γ.

What they found. Attribution worsens as assignment becomes more biased, most under predictive confounding, "a result of less observed variation across predictive covariates within a group". Prognostic confounding hurts least; on News it even hurts less than confounding on an irrelevant covariate, which the authors explain by two competing effects. CFRNet's balancing can cause "a very large drop" in Attrpred under predictive confounding: on TCGA the strongest penalty (γ = 0.01) falls from about 0.7 to about 0.4 (approx., Fig. 4a), back to chance.

My take. The experiment is well designed, and separating the three kinds of confounding is new, as the authors say. The bands in the ACIC2016 and Twins panels are wide and overlapping, so the learner differences there are not resolved (approx., Fig. 4b, 4d). How sure: medium (read off small plots).

6. Try it: chance level and correlated covariates

A seeded simulation (seed 509), not the paper's data: 20 Gaussian covariates with equal pairwise correlation ρ; 4 prognostic, 4 + 4 predictive and 8 irrelevant covariates; linear Algorithm 1 outcomes with weights from U(-1, 1), noise sd 0.5, random treatment, 1,000 training and 500 test points, averaged over 10 replicates. The learner is a T-learner made of two ridge regressions (λ = 1); for a linear model Integrated Gradients with a zero baseline is exactly coefficient times feature value.

7. My critique

What's good. It asks a question PEHE cannot answer and shows the answer differs (the S-learner result). The data-generating process is simple, documented and reusable with any covariate set. The three-way split of confounding is a genuinely new experimental knob. The learners are matched in capacity, results average 30 seeds, and the explainer choice is checked against alternatives.

8. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

It is the precedent a reviewer will raise first. The ChatGPT conversation of 2026-10-24 (synthetic_data/chatgpt_conference_submission_plan.md) flagged it, and your review (81 references) does not cite it yet. The overlap is the construction "keep real rows, simulate the target, know what matters". The differences are what your paper should say plainly:

  • it evaluates treatment-effect learners with a fixed explainer; your pilot evaluates explainers;
  • its targets are random functions on random covariates; your builder targets (additive, forest, MLP in TARGETS of transfer_pilot_v2.py) are fitted to the real target in D;
  • its truth is a feature set scored at data level; you compute exact attribution values at model level (err_model, with err_data and model_gap alongside);
  • it ignores dependence between covariates; dependence is your whole premise.

An extra arm it suggests. Every builder in your grid fits its target to D. Adding an ITErpretability-style target, a random function on the same real rows, isolates whether fitting the target to real labels matters for ranking transfer, or whether realistic rows alone carry the effect. It is one more entry in TARGETS, cheap compared with a new generator.

The chance-level lesson. Put every agreement number next to its null. Your primary metric (agreement on reality's resolved pairs, DECISIONS #24) has an obvious null of 0.5 for random pair orientation, plus the generic null world; keep both in every table and figure.

9. Glossary
Potential outcomesY(1) and Y(0), a unit's outcome with and without treatment; only one is observed.
CATEConditional average treatment effect, τ(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | X = x].
PEHERoot mean squared error of an estimated τ on test points.
Prognostic covariateAffects the outcome equally under both treatments, so it cancels in τ.
Predictive covariateChanges the size of the treatment effect, so τ depends on it.
Attrpred, AttrprogShare of absolute attribution on the true predictive, or prognostic, covariates, averaged over test points.
S-, T-, X-, DR-learnerOne model with treatment as input; one model per arm; two direct effect regressions combined; a regression on a doubly robust pseudo-outcome.
TARNet, CFRNetA shared representation with one head per arm; the same plus a penalty making the arms' representations look alike.
ConfoundingTreatment assignment depends on covariates that also affect the outcome.
Integrated GradientsThe gradient averaged along a straight path from a baseline to the input, times the input difference.

  1. Pages 1-5: introduction, the prognostic/predictive distinction (Section 3) and the benchmark (Section 4, Algorithm 1, Eq. 2). Read in full.
