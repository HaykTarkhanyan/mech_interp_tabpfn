1. The paper in one minute

The question. Feature-attribution methods (LIME, SHAP, gradients) are compared in scattered, small experiments with home-made metrics. Can one open library benchmark them reproducibly across datasets, models and metrics?

The answers.

  1. A library plus leaderboards. Seven real tabular datasets and one synthetic generator, two pre-trained models per dataset (logistic regression and a two-layer neural network, so 16 models), six explainers plus a random baseline, and 22 metrics: 8 for faithfulness, 3 for stability, 11 for fairness (Section 2).
  2. Ground truth must describe the model, not the data. If the label depends on features A and B, but A is correlated with C, an accurate model may use C. An explanation that correctly says "C" is then marked wrong against the data's ground truth (Section 2, citing Faber et al.).
  3. Their fix for synthetic data: make features independent. SynthGauss draws well-separated Gaussian clusters with identity covariance, and a theorem claims the most accurate model must then follow the data's ground truth (Appendix, Theorem 1).
  4. Benchmark results (NeurIPS version): gradient methods are perfect on four ground-truth metrics; LIME leads on sign agreement (+61.6%); SmoothGrad leads on PGU (+43.03%); no method is the most stable everywhere; Gradient x Input shows the least disparity (+8.9%). Several of these changed in v5.
My take, one line. Valuable as infrastructure and for stating the data-versus-model ground-truth problem cleanly. Weak as evidence about explainers: on real data its "ground truth" is the logistic regression's coefficient vector, which gradients reproduce by construction, and the published rankings changed between versions without an erratum.

2. Background you need first

Post hoc feature attribution. Given a trained model and one input, return one number per feature saying how much it mattered. OpenXAI includes LIME (fit a local linear surrogate on perturbed samples), KernelSHAP (a sampling approximation of Shapley values), Vanilla Gradients (the derivative of the output), Gradient x Input, SmoothGrad (gradients averaged over noisy copies of the input) and Integrated Gradients (gradients averaged along a path from a baseline).

Data-level versus model-level ground truth. This is the paper's central conceptual point, and it is the right one. Data-level ground truth says which features the label-generating process uses. Model-level ground truth says which features the trained model uses. Post hoc explanations are supposed to describe the model, so scoring them against the data is unfair whenever the two differ:

Schematic of the example on page 4 of the NeurIPS version. The demo in Section 6 lets you move the correlation and see how much attribution moves to the proxy.

The four metric families.

  • Ground-truth faithfulness (from Krishna et al.'s "disagreement problem" paper): Feature Agreement (share of shared top-K features), Rank Agreement (same features at the same ranks), Sign Agreement, Signed Rank Agreement, Rank Correlation (Spearman) and Pairwise Rank Agreement. FA, RA, SA and SRA are reported as the area under the curve over all K (v5, Appendix A).
  • Predictive faithfulness: PGI and PGU, the change in predicted probability when the top-K (PGI) or the other (PGU) features get small Gaussian noise.
  • Stability: RIS, RRS and ROS, the largest relative change in the explanation divided by the relative change in the input, a hidden layer, or the output, over nearby points with the same prediction.
  • Fairness: the gap between a metric's average for majority (male) and minority (female) instances.

What "ground truth" is on the real datasets. The NeurIPS main text never says. The v5 appendix does: ground-truth explanations are the "coefficients of logistic regression models" (Appendix A), and v5 evaluates ground-truth faithfulness only for logistic regression models (Section 3).

3. Finding 1: SynthGauss and its theorem

What they built (Section 2; v5 Appendix C, Algorithm 1). Draw each point from one of K clusters, cluster k being N(μ_k, I) with centers on the axes at distance δ = 6. Each cluster gets a random mask m_k (each feature included with probability 0.25) and random weights w_k ~ U(-1, 1). The class probability is the sigmoid of (w_k ⊙ m_k)·x, and the label is 1 when that probability is above the median, so classes are balanced. The mask m_k is the ground-truth explanation for every point in cluster k. Defaults: d = 20 features, K = 10 clusters (v5 D.3).

The guarantee they claim (Theorem 1): with independent features, well-separated clusters and a unique ground truth per cluster, "the most accurate model trained on such a dataset will adhere to the unique ground truth explanation of each local neighborhood". The proof is by contradiction: if a model's top-T features differ from the truth, "we can construct another model M' ... and the accuracy of M' will be higher".

My take: the guarantee does not reach the reported numbers. Three gaps.
  • The proof asserts the key step (a better model can always be constructed) without showing it, and "the top-T features used by a model" is never defined for a nonlinear model.
  • The premise is the most accurate model. The labels are a deterministic function of x, so near-perfect accuracy is possible, yet OpenXAI's own models reach 81.76% (logistic regression) and 90.32% (neural network) on the synthetic data (v5 Table 7). A single logistic regression cannot even represent ten cluster-specific weight vectors.
  • In v5, Vanilla Gradients scores a perfect 1.000 on all six ground-truth metrics for the synthetic dataset (Table 16). That is only possible if the reference there is the logistic regression's coefficient vector, not the cluster masks m_k, so the theorem's ground truth is not what the synthetic-data tables measure.
How sure: high on the first two; high that the v5 synthetic tables use coefficients (the perfect gradient scores force it), medium for the NeurIPS tables, which do not say.

4. Finding 2: ground-truth faithfulness on real data

What they did. Explain the logistic regression model on HELOC and Adult Income with all methods and score against the ground truth (Tables 2-3; more datasets in the appendix).

What they found, and how it changed. NeurIPS: "Vanilla Gradients, SmoothGrad, and Integrated Gradients produce explanations that achieve perfect scores on four ground-truth faithfulness metrics" and LIME "outperforms other methods on the signed agreement (SA) [+61.6%] and signed-rank agreement (SRA) [+65.3%] metrics". v5: the same three gradient methods are perfect on all six metrics, and LIME is described as "approaching the performance of the above gradient-based explanations". NeurIPS Table 2 versus v5 Table 2 (means; standard errors omitted). The random baseline's FA drops from 0.498 to 0.149, so the metric itself was redefined or recomputed (v5 reports the area under the curve over all K and uses the first 1,000 test instances).

My take: the perfect scores are built into the metric. For a logistic regression f(x) = σ(w·x + b), the gradient is σ'(w·x + b)·w, a positive multiple of the coefficient vector at every input. Rank it, compare signs, take the top-K: it matches the coefficients exactly, always. SmoothGrad averages such gradients, and Integrated Gradients with multiply_by_inputs=False (v5 D.3) also averages them, so all three are perfect by algebra. SHAP and Gradient x Input are scored as poor because they return local contributions (for a linear model SHAP gives w_i(x_i - E[x_i])), which rank features by |w_i·(x_i - baseline)|, not by |w_i|. On German Credit, v5 reports SHAP's rank correlation with the "ground truth" as -0.001, no better than random (Table 17), for a method that is exact for linear models under its own definition. The benchmark is asking "which method returns the coefficient vector", not "which explanation is correct".

The NeurIPS sign agreement of 0.469 for gradients fits one specific bug: explaining the predicted class instead of a fixed class flips every sign whenever the prediction is class 0. HELOC is close to balanced, which would give about half. How sure: high on the algebra; low on the cause of the NeurIPS numbers, which the papers never explain.

5. Finding 3: stability and fairness

Stability. NeurIPS: on the synthetic data, Gradient x Input is the most stable (+93.5% on RIS, +59.2% on RRS; Table 4), but degrades on real data; SmoothGrad has 63.2% higher RRS than the others on average across real datasets; "no single explanation method is consistently the most stable". v5 reports log averages, drops RRS for logistic regression ("equivalent to ROS within this model class"), and concludes the opposite on the synthetic data: Integrated Gradients is best with log averages of 1.95 (RIS) and 1.86 (ROS) across datasets; Gradient x Input sits at 3.37 and 4.49.

Fairness. NeurIPS: there are male/female disparities in PGU for Vanilla Gradients, Integrated Gradients and SmoothGrad, and Gradient x Input shows the least disparity (+8.9%), "a trade-off between various evaluation metrics" (Figs. 2-3). v5: the notable disparities are "most notably, Gradient x Input and SHAP on the Adult Income dataset".

My take. Both verdicts flipped between versions. On the figures themselves, the NeurIPS gaps are small. On Adult Income (Fig. 3) the largest gaps, for Vanilla Gradients and Integrated Gradients, are about 0.004 on bars near 0.08 (approx.). On German Credit (Fig. 2) the error bars of every pair except Integrated Gradients overlap, SmoothGrad's gap looks smaller than Gradient x Input's (approx.), which contradicts "Gradient x Input results in the least amount of disparity", and the legend box covers the LIME bars. No test is reported. The NeurIPS Table 5 caption says "values closer to zero are desirable" while several values are negative (SmoothGrad -4.780 on German Credit); for a log of a ratio where smaller means more stable, "closer to zero" is not the right reading, and v5 treats the lowest values as best (Integrated Gradients wins with the smallest log scores). How sure: high on the version differences; medium on the caption, because the NeurIPS main text does not define the scale.

6. Try it: a proxy feature steals attribution

Exact population formulas, not the paper's experiment: the label is y = A + noise, A and C are standard normal with correlation ρ, and the model is ridge regression on (A, C), whose weights are (Σ + λI)⁻¹·Cov(x, y). The data-level ground truth gives A all the importance and C none. It illustrates the example on page 4 of the paper.

7. My critique

What's good. It made benchmarking cheap: loaders, pre-trained models, a common API and public leaderboards, the bar every later tabular benchmark is measured against. It states the data-versus-model ground-truth problem clearly and builds the synthetic generator around it. The library is open and has been maintained, and v5 documents hyperparameters, seeds and model accuracies in detail.

8. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

It names the problem your pilot already solves. OpenXAI avoids the proxy problem by forbidding dependence. Your transfer pilot keeps dependence and computes the model-level truth exactly instead: run_task in synthetic_data/scripts/transfer_pilot_v2.py records err_model (against the trained model's exact SHAP values), err_data (against the true function's) and model_gap between them. model_gap is a direct measurement of OpenXAI's A/C effect in every world; report it per generator, because it is the number that shows why independence is not realistic.

The coefficient lesson applies to you too. Your estimand is interventional SHAP (DECISIONS #22). Path-dependent TreeSHAP, Saabas, LIME, occlusion and mean-baseline SHAP target other quantities, so the pilot ranks them as estimators of interventional SHAP. That is fine for the transfer question, but say it in the paper, and consider a cheap robustness check: re-score one world family against a second estimand and see whether the transfer conclusion survives. If it does not, the estimand choice is driving the result, exactly as here.

Your "independent" generator is the OpenXAI-style world. It drops all dependence while keeping the real marginals, so in the pilot it measures what OpenXAI's design choice costs in ranking transfer.

Version drift. If the paper compares with OpenXAI numbers, cite the version (NeurIPS or v5), because they disagree. Your own setup (exact pins in synthetic_data/pyproject.toml, raw JSON per run) is the defence against the same thing happening to you.

9. Glossary
Data-level ground truthWhich features the label-generating process uses.
Model-level ground truthWhich features the trained model uses; can differ when features are correlated.
SynthGaussOpenXAI's generator: separated Gaussian clusters with independent features and a per-cluster feature mask.
FA, RA, SA, SRAShare of top-K features shared with the reference, also matching rank, sign, or both.
RC, PRASpearman rank correlation with the reference, and the share of feature pairs ordered the same way.
PGI, PGUPrediction change when noise is added to the top-K (PGI) or the other (PGU) features.
RIS, RRS, ROSRelative stability: explanation change over input, hidden-layer or output change.
Gradient x Input, SmoothGrad, Integrated GradientsThe gradient times the input; the gradient averaged over noisy inputs; the gradient averaged along a path from a baseline.
Ridge regressionLinear regression with a penalty on large weights; it spreads weight across correlated features.

10. How to read the actual paper
  1. NeurIPS pages 1-4: contributions, related work, and the A/B/C/D proxy example (page 4). Read in full.
  2. Pages 5-7: datasets, explainers and the metric list.
  3. Pages 8-10: Tables 2-5 and Figures 2-3. Read them next to v5 Section 3, because the numbers differ.
  4. v5 Appendix A (metric definitions, and the one sentence saying ground truth is the logistic regression's coefficients), Appendix C (SynthGauss and Theorem 1), D.3-D.4 (hyperparameters and model accuracies).
  5. Skip on a first read: the leaderboard snapshot (Fig. 1) and the per-dataset tables 8-35 in v5.
