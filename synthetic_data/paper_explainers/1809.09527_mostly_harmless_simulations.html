1. The paper in one minute

The question. Econometricians choosing between treatment-effect estimators were advised to run an "empirical Monte Carlo study" (EMCS): build a simulation that mimics their own dataset, run all estimators on it, and pick the one that does best. Does that actually pick the estimator that is best on the real data?

The answers.

  1. In theory, only under restrictive conditions. The simulation must reproduce both each estimator's bias and the order of their variances. Miss either and it can pick the wrong one with certainty (Section 3).
  2. On real data, for bias, worse than random. On the LaLonde job-training data, where the true effect is known from an experiment, both EMCS designs rank estimators by absolute bias with negative correlation to the true ranking: Kendall's tau of -0.21 and -0.37 (Table 1).
  3. For mean squared error, better than random but no better than a bootstrap. Tau 0.60 and 0.64, versus 0.83 for a plain bootstrap (Table 1).
  4. Why: the simulations copy the estimators' variances well but not their biases.
My take, one line. The single most important empirical warning for your pitch, and also a ready-made template for testing it: they measured whether a simulation fitted to a dataset ranks methods the way the real data does, with Kendall's tau, regret and a random and a bootstrap baseline. Nobody has run that test for explainers.

2. Background you need first

The task. Estimate the average effect of a job-training programme on the people who took it (the ATT, average treatment effect on the treated), from observational data, assuming that once you control for the observed covariates, who got trained is as good as random (unconfoundedness). Many estimators exist for this (regression, weighting, matching and combinations), with similar large-sample properties. Which to use in a given dataset?

Two ways to build a dataset-specific simulation.

Schematic of the two EMCS designs (Section 2). The question the paper asks: does the pick on the right match the best estimator on the real data?

Bias, variance, MSE. An estimator's error splits into bias (it is off on average) and variance (it scatters). MSE = bias squared + variance. Ranking estimators by MSE and by absolute bias can give different answers.

How they score a ranking. Kendall's tau between the simulation's ranking and the true ranking (1 = identical, 0 = unrelated, -1 = reversed), and average regret: how much worse the chosen estimator is than the truly best one, as a percentage. Two baselines: random choice, and a nonparametric bootstrap (resample the real data and rank estimators by their bootstrap error).

3. Finding 1: the theory, simulations must copy both biases and variance order

What they did. Take two estimators whose sampling distribution is bivariate Gaussian, the most favourable setting possible. Let the simulation have its own biases and variances. When does the simulation pick the truly better estimator?

  • Scenario 1: biases and variances reproduced exactly. It always picks right.
  • Scenario 2: both unbiased, but the simulation gets the variance order wrong. It always picks wrong.
  • Scenario 3: variance order right, but one estimator is biased in reality and the simulation does not reproduce that bias. It picks the biased one whenever the bias exceeds a threshold that shrinks with sample size: |b2| > sqrt((σ1² - σ2²)/n).

Why these are realistic. Both happen in ordinary treatment-effect settings. If treated outcomes are noisier than untreated ones (heteroskedasticity), the placebo design, which builds fake treated units from untreated ones, cannot see it and gets the variance order wrong. If the outcome slopes differ between treated and untreated, OLS is biased in reality, but in the placebo world (all untreated) it is not. A structured design fitted with a misspecified model has the same blind spot.

What they found in a stylised simulation. With heteroskedasticity, the placebo design picked the better estimator 1.9% of the time and the structured design 3.0%, against 97.6% and 100% without it. Random guessing gets 50%. In the Scenario 3 example the rates were 2.3% and 0.2% (Section 3.2; details in the supplement).

The general lesson, in their words: "misspecification in the construction of the DGP can lead the ranking of estimators to be incorrect for the original dataset".

4. Finding 2: on real data, bias rankings are worse than random

What they did. LaLonde's National Supported Work data is a classic test bed: the programme was randomized, so the true effect on earnings is known from the experiment ($1,794). Treat the experimental treated group (185) plus a trimmed CPS comparison group (7,660) as a finite population. Draw 1,000 samples of 100 treated and 1,900 comparison units. In each sample, compute seven estimators' errors against the truth (this gives the true ranking) and run each EMCS design 1,000 times on the sample (this gives the simulation's ranking): one million replications per design.

What they found (Table 1). Kendall's tau between the simulation's ranking and the true ranking:

Table 1, exact values. Random ranking has tau 0 by construction (dashed line). Higher is better.

For absolute bias, average regret is 3,067% (placebo) and 3,766% (structured) of the best estimator's bias, against 1,184% for random choice and 1,000% for the bootstrap: the chosen estimator is on average about 30 to 37 times worse than the best. "In two out of three cases we study, the rankings produced are negatively correlated with the true ranking."

5. Finding 3: for MSE they help, but a bootstrap does as well

For MSE the picture changes: average regret is 18.2% (placebo) and 16.3% (structured), much better than random (141.9%), but the bootstrap's 7.9% is lower still, with tau 0.83 versus 0.60 and 0.64 (Table 1).

Why. In these data MSE is driven mostly by variance, and the simulations reproduce variances well: tau for variance rankings is 0.77 and 0.81. They reproduce biases badly. The bootstrap captures variance just as well with fewer design choices.

My take. This decomposition is the part to carry over. An explainer's error against a known answer also has a "variance" part (Monte Carlo noise, sensitivity to the sample) and a "bias" part (a systematic error from a violated assumption, like independence or additivity). The variance part depends on generic properties a fitted generator copies easily. The bias part depends on exactly the structure (dependence, interactions) that a generator and a fitted formula may not reproduce. So one would predict the same split for explainers. That prediction is testable. How sure: medium (it is an analogy, not shown in the paper).

6. Finding 4: the result survives two robustness checks, mostly

No sampling error in the truth (Table 2). Use the experiment's randomized-out controls as the "treated" group, so the true effect is exactly zero. Bias tau is -0.27 (placebo) and -0.47 (structured), against 0.32 for the bootstrap; MSE tau 0.65 and 0.55 against 0.81. Same conclusion.

Unconfoundedness by construction (Table 3). Impute missing potential outcomes by 4-nearest-neighbour matching, assign treatment by a perturbed propensity, so the assumptions the estimators rely on hold exactly. Now bias rankings are about as good as random (tau -0.003 and 0.057), slightly better than the bootstrap (-0.300), and for MSE both designs beat random (tau 0.74 and 0.73) and marginally beat the bootstrap (0.63).

My take. The third test softens the headline: when the estimators' assumptions hold by construction, the simulations are no longer worse than random on bias and are the best of the three on MSE. The abstract's "worse than random" is true for the two tests on the real data, not universally. Secondary sources, including my own first version of your literature review, quoted only the bias half. How sure: high.

8. My critique

What's good. It asks the right question (does the simulation pick the method that is best on this data?) and answers it with a design where the truth is known, with two sensible baselines (random and bootstrap) and three ways of scoring a ranking. The theory is simple enough to check by hand and pins down the mechanism. The robustness tests attack the obvious objections. The advice at the end is modest and correct.

9. How it connects to your work

Everything in this section is my own analysis.

It is the strongest argument against the pitch's transfer claim. The pitch slide says "anything you observe on the simulated side transfers to the real one". For estimators, a simulation fitted to the very dataset in question chose worse than random on bias. Your literature review (Section 3.5, implication 2) already uses this; the corrected version now also reports the MSE half and the mechanism.

It is also the template for the test the pitch needs. Advani et al. had a setting with a known truth and asked whether the data-fitted simulation reproduces the true ranking. The same design works for explainers if you have a world where the correct explanation is known and realistic. Two ways to get one: (a) a nested test, where one rich semi-synthetic world plays "reality", and the benchmark pipeline (fit an ARF and a target to samples drawn from it) is checked against it; (b) data with a known mechanism, such as the physics formulas of SRSD or Feynman datasets sampled at realistic ranges. Score with their exact metrics: Kendall's tau and regret, against a random ranking and a bootstrap-style baseline.

A prediction to test. From Finding 3: explainer errors that are "variance-like" (Monte Carlo noise in KernelSHAP, sensitivity to background size) should transfer from a fitted benchmark; errors that are "bias-like" (independence-assuming explainers under strong dependence, additive explainers under interactions) transfer only if the generator reproduces the dependence and the fitted formula reproduces the interactions. Measuring that split would be a genuinely new result.

Their closing advice applies too. "Test sensitivity of estimator performance to parameters of the simulation" is the case for declared difficulty dials (correlation, interaction strength, noise) rather than one fitted world per dataset.

10. Glossary
ATTAverage treatment effect on the treated: the average effect for the people who actually received the treatment.
UnconfoundednessGiven the observed covariates, treatment is as good as randomly assigned. Also called selection on observables.
EMCSEmpirical Monte Carlo study: a simulation designed to mimic a particular real dataset.
Placebo EMCSAssign fake treatments to untreated units using the fitted propensity model; the true effect is zero.
Structured EMCSFit parametric distributions to all variables and sample a new dataset with a computable true effect.
Kendall's tauRank correlation between two orderings: 1 identical, 0 unrelated, -1 reversed.
Average regretHow much worse the chosen estimator is than the truly best one, averaged over samples.
Nonparametric bootstrapResample the observed data with replacement many times to approximate an estimator's sampling distribution.
HeteroskedasticityThe noise variance differs across groups or covariate values (here, treated versus untreated outcomes).
Semiparametric efficiency boundThe smallest variance any regular estimator can achieve without assuming a functional form.
NSW / LaLonde dataA randomized job-training programme from the 1970s, used since LaLonde (1986) to test non-experimental estimators against an experimental truth.
Oaxaca-Blinder, IPWRAA regression-based decomposition used as an ATT estimator, and inverse-probability-weighted regression adjustment, a doubly robust estimator.

11. How to read the actual paper
  1. Pages 1-5: summary and introduction, including the three main results stated plainly. Read in full.
  2. Pages 6-10: Section 3.1, the two-estimator scenarios. Short and worth working through once.
  3. Pages 15-17: Section 4.2, the design of the real-data test. This is the part to copy.
  4. Pages 19-22: Table 1 and its notes, then Section 5.2; skim Table 3.
  5. Skip on a first read: the variance algebra in Section 3.2.1 and Appendix A, and the estimator details in Appendices B-C.
